The Governance Gap Regulators Just Discovered

29 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

On 26 June 2026, the Trump administration partially lifted its export ban on Anthropic's Fable 5 model - a move reported by NPR and confirmed in Anthropic's own statement. The story sounds like a routine regulatory reversal. Read carefully, and you see something deeper: the government discovered it cannot verify whether an AI model is safe to release. We built that framework. It's called REGINALD, and it has been running on our own infrastructure while governments negotiated in the dark.

This is the hidden story in the reporting.

What the Ban Revealed

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's letter to Anthropic said the company had "worked with the U.S. government to address risks associated with the Covered Models." The phrasing is diplomatic. What it actually describes is a negotiation in the dark: the government wanted proof Fable 5 was safe to export. Anthropic couldn't provide it in a form the government could verify. They reached a compromise - a partial exception for select partners while they figure out a framework.

Here's the sentence that matters:

"The conversations have also focused on determining a standardised framework to evaluate cases of suspected security bypasses in the future."

The government is asking: How do we know whether a model's security claims are true? Anthropic is asking: What evidence would you accept? And neither side had a clear answer.

So they negotiated case-by-case. They'll negotiate again next time.

The Deeper Problem

This isn't a failure of good faith. It's a failure of infrastructure.

There is no standard way for an AI company to prove it has built safety controls into a model. There is no standard way for a government to verify that those controls work. There is no agreed format for the evidence - no chain of custody, no signature binding the claim to the person who made it, no way to audit the audit.

When you can't verify a claim, you make it a negotiation. When you make it a negotiation, trust becomes a matter of politics and relationship capital instead of evidence.

This problem will explode as AI regulation spreads.

The EU AI Act, now in force, requires companies to document "the development and use" of AI systems and maintain "traceability mechanisms" for high-risk ones. What does that mean? Companies are building bespoke documentation systems because there's no standard format. Regulators are reviewing prose because there's no machine-readable alternative. Insurance companies are pricing AI risk without the data they need to price it accurately.

Every regulator is about to discover what the Trump administration just did: we don't have a way to verify AI claims at scale.

What Verification Actually Requires

Here's what solving it means. A verifiable claim about an AI model needs:

  1. Structured data. Not prose, not a PDF report - a machine-readable declaration of what the model is, how it was trained, what it can and cannot do, what the known failure modes are.

  2. Proof of authorship. A signature that proves who made the claim and when, cryptographically bound to the claim itself. If someone rewrites the claim, the signature breaks.

  3. An audit trail. Every change tracked. Every review recorded. Every test result stored. A complete chain of custody from raw data through training through deployment.

  4. Third-party verifiability. A regulator, an auditor, a business partner, or any other third party should be able to check the claim against the evidence without access to the company's internal systems. The evidence chain should be public (the claim) plus queryable (the supporting facts).

  5. Open standards. If each company invents its own format, you're back to custom negotiation. The format has to be published, standardised, governed by the community of people who use it.

That's the infrastructure that doesn't exist yet. And once it does, the Anthropic negotiation won't need to happen. The government will query the attestation. They'll review the evidence. They'll check the signature. They'll decide.

Why This Matters Now

Regulation is coming. The EU AI Act is in force. The UK, Canada, Australia, Singapore, and Japan all have AI strategies in motion. The Trump administration just announced a voluntary review framework - which will formalize into a requirement once someone sues a company for harms from an unreviewed model.

The companies that move first will build the infrastructure. The companies that move second will adopt it. The companies that don't move will find themselves unable to export, unable to insure, unable to defend themselves in court.

Investors should watch this closely. The winners of the next 18 months are the ones shipping the infrastructure that turns "we have no way to verify this" into "here's the verifiable evidence, check it yourself."

The framework the government needs already exists. The question is whether companies will adopt it before regulators mandate it.
