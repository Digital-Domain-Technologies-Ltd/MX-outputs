Why Machines Need Human Creativity

16 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

I use machines every day to do work I wouldn't want to do without them. I use them to research: to gather sources, surface prior work, and assemble the background I need before I can form a view. I use them to check spelling and grammar, the mechanical layer that has to be right and that a person reads past too easily. I use them to check consistency: whether a term is used the same way throughout, whether a claim made early is contradicted later, whether the structure holds.

Each of those is real help, and I wouldn't give it up. Notice, though, what none of them is. None of them decides the subject of the piece. None of them sets the angle, judges whether the argument is sound, or answers for the result. I direct and control. I author. The machine is an aid.

That's the whole argument of this post, stated as practice before theory. Machines extend and execute; they don't originate. A machine can vary and scale a creative act, but the act itself begins with a person deciding that something is worth making and why. This isn't a temporary limitation waiting for a better model; it describes what the two parties do.

The language around AI tends to blur this. We talk about machines that write and compose, as if authorship had moved. It hasn't; what has moved is the cost of production.

What a machine actually does well

A machine is good at the parts of creative work that are bounded. Given a clear specification, it'll produce many candidates quickly. Given a pattern, it'll continue it. Given a body of existing work, it'll find the regularities and apply them. These are useful capabilities, and dismissing them helps no one.

Each of those tasks, though, starts from something a person supplied: the specification, the pattern, the body of work. The machine operates on a frame it didn't set. Ask it to set the frame instead, and you get an average of what already exists, because that's the only material it has. The result can be competent, but it can't be a decision, because no one decided anything.

Creativity, in the sense that matters here, is the act of choosing what to make and judging whether it's any good. Both ends of that, the originating choice and the final judgement, rest with a person.

Originating choice

Every piece of creative work answers a question that wasn't obvious before someone asked it. Why this subject. Why now. Why for these people rather than those. A machine can answer a question put to it. It can't notice that one is worth putting.

This is the part that looks like magic and is really just attention. A person sees that something is missing, or wrong, or possible, and decides to act on it. That noticing comes from having lived in a context, a market, a craft, a community, long enough to feel where the gaps are. A machine has read the context. It hasn't lived in it, and it has nothing at stake in it.

Final judgement

The other end is judgement: deciding whether the work is honest and fit for the people it serves. A machine can score an output against a metric. It can't tell you whether that measure was the right one, or whether the work will land badly with an audience for reasons it never captured.

Judgement also carries responsibility. When a person publishes something, they answer for it. A machine produces an output and answers for nothing. If the work is misleading, or tone-deaf, or simply wrong, the accountability sits with whoever decided to release it. Keeping a person in that position isn't caution for its own sake; it's the only arrangement under which the work can be trusted.

Convergence principle, seen from this angle

MX builds on the convergence principle: interfaces built well for machines tend to be better for humans too, because clarity and honest naming serve both. The principle is usually stated as a claim about interfaces. It's also a claim about roles.

If you design for machines as the audience and forget the humans, you push everything toward throughput and lose the point of it. If you build for humans and treat machines as an afterthought, you produce work the new distribution layer can't read or carry. The principle holds the two together. Human creativity sets what the work is; machine capability extends how far and how fast it travels. Neither substitutes for the other, and an approach that respects both beats one that picks a side.

This is why MX keeps a person in the loop as a requirement rather than a courtesy. Not because machines are untrustworthy, but because the loop is where the originating choice and the final judgement live. Remove the person and you haven't automated creativity; you've removed it and kept the production line running.

What this means in practice

The useful question is where to place the human effort so it counts, not whether to use machines at all. Place it at the start: the brief, the angle, the decision that this work should exist. A weak brief produces weak output no matter how capable the model, because it faithfully extends a weak starting point.

Place it at the end: the review, the judgement, the willingness to reject work that's competent but wrong. That's harder than it sounds, because work that reads well is persuasive and invites you to approve it. Judgement means reading for whether it's right, not whether it's smooth.

In the middle, let the machine do what it does well. Drafts, variants, scaling, the patient application of an agreed pattern. That's real help, and treating it as beneath notice wastes it. This is the arrangement that produces work I'm willing to put my name to, not a compromise I've settled on.
