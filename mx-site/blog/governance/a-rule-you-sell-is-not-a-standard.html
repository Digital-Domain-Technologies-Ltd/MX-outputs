A Rule You Sell Is Not a Standard

3 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

A standard decides who passes and who fails. The moment the company that profits from the verdict also writes the rule behind it, what was a standard becomes a sales instrument. It bends, slowly and reasonably, toward whatever the company sells. Not through malice, through gravity. People protect the thing that pays them.

This is the hinge on which the whole case for an independent standards body turns. Whose Standard Is It Anyway? opened this series on it and named the ways the bend happens: capture by infrastructure ownership, capture by sponsor concentration, and capture by founder dependency. This post takes the infrastructure and sponsor modes out of the abstract, into the clearest cases the web has produced, and then shows what the opposite looks like when it's built properly.

The open label, the private steering wheel

The usual move is to wear openness rather than reject it.

Google's AMP arrived dressed as an open project for a faster mobile web. It was open source, anyone could contribute, and for years a Google engineer held the final say on what went in. AMP pages were served from Google's cache and given prominent placement in search, which meant publishers couldn't easily decline. When the criticism grew, Google announced open governance and later moved AMP to the OpenJS Foundation. Critics called that step window-dressing, since Google funded the foundation; an advisory member said on resigning that AMP remained a Google product in all but name. A later antitrust complaint alleged the format had been used to steer publishers away from ad-selling methods that threatened Google's own revenue. Whatever a court eventually makes of that allegation, the structure is the lesson: the party that owned the rule was the one that gained from it.

Microsoft's Office Open XML is the older, cleaner case. In 2008, after an ISO process marked by committee-stuffing allegations and a failed first vote, OOXML was fast-tracked to international-standard status as ISO/IEC 29500. A committee member observed that the specification read less like a standard than the technical documentation of a commercial product. That's close to what it was: a way to encode Microsoft Office's existing formats so the company could claim conformance without loosening its hold on the people locked into them. An openly developed format, ODF, already existed. A second one was minted because the incumbent needed a standard shaped like its product.

Different shapes, one pattern. AMP is capture by infrastructure ownership: the rule mattered less than the cache and the search slot that Google alone controlled, mirroring the WordPress.org case that opened this series. OOXML is capture by sponsor concentration: the specification was waved through by committees the incumbent had packed. In both, the move is the same - adopt the language of openness, keep the steering wheel. Embrace and extend, where the extension is the part only you can implement.

What independence actually looks like

Open governance is possible, but it has to be real, and realness can be recognised.

The internet runs on standards made by the IETF, whose culture David Clark caught in a line from a 1992 talk that has been on T-shirts ever since: "We reject kings, presidents and voting. We believe in rough consensus and running code." Participation is open, no one buys a veto, and a proposal earns its place by working rather than by who put it forward. The protocols that carry this sentence to you were set that way. No vendor owns TCP/IP, and that's exactly why anyone can build on it.

Foundations can supply the same neutrality - OASIS held ODF, the OpenJS Foundation holds a shelf of web projects - but a logo isn't a guarantee. AMP shows that a project can be housed in a foundation while one company funds it, staffs it, and gains from it. The test isn't where the rule is filed. It's whether those who profit from the outcome can change the rule, and if those who live with it can be heard. Independence is a structure, not a press release.

When capture has already happened, the structural repair is to delete the single switch rather than build a nicer one. After one company seized a rival's plugin slug on the WordPress directory, the answer was federation rather than a better catalogue. The Linux Foundation's FAIR project distributes cryptographically signed packages across independent repositories, so trust travels in the signature and no single registry can cut a participant off. The format is platform-agnostic by design, and after its WordPress effort stalled it has been carried into TYPO3 - a federated system can move between ecosystems precisely because no one owns it. The Spec Was Never the Fragile Part follows that mechanism through to how MX uses it.

Why this is the hinge for MX

Here is the part it would be dishonest to skip.

CogNovaMX is a vendor. It sells an audit, and it runs REGINALD, a registry. If CogNovaMX kept the MX standard permanently, and with it the definition of a good score, every result it issued would be worthless - the seller grading its own homework and charging for the rubric. A regulator would be right to ignore it. So would a buyer.

That's the whole reason the standard isn't ours to keep. The position is written down in one sentence: "CogNovaMX stewards the common MX vocabulary today and dogfoods it first; ownership and governance pass to the Gathering after donation." The vocabulary is the specification text and the mx: namespace: what a record must declare, and what its fields are allowed to mean. Once donated, it belongs to The Gathering, and The Gathering owns the conformance criteria, what conformant means, while selling no audit of its own. The definition is open. Anyone may implement it; REGINALD is one implementation among those that can exist, not a vendor specification wearing a standard's clothes. The separation is the only arrangement under which our numbers can mean anything to someone outside the company.

A standards body earns trust by selling nothing it standardises. That line is easy to write and costly to hold, because the pull this post began with acts on every organisation, ours included. The defence against it isn't good intentions. It's keeping the rule somewhere we can't quietly move it - and writing the limits down where a sceptic can check them. Not the Main Sponsor sets out the concrete version. It caps how much of The Gathering's funding may come from my own company, rules out a founder veto, and names three checkable conditions for the donation.

The same test, in short

The opening post set out the questions a buyer should ask of any standard: if the sponsor went bankrupt, would the standard survive; if its commercial interests shifted, would the standard bend; and if the registry operator blocked a participant, would they have recourse. The compressed version fits in a breath. Who profits if the rule says what it says, and who's able to change it. If one company answers both, you're looking at a product with a certificate stapled to it.

The Gathering exists so that, for MX, neither answer is us.
