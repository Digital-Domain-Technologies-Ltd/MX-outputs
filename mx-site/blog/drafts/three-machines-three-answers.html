Three Machines, Three Answers

17 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 3 min read

Scrunch's Agent Experience Platform (AXP) does something technically impressive. It intercepts requests at the CDN layer and identifies the type of machine making the request. Then it serves each machine a tailored version of your page.

Three machine types. Three optimised responses.

That is the problem.

The Three Machines

AXP distinguishes:

  1. Training crawlers — building the next generation of LLM datasets from your content.
  2. Search indexers — building the citation stores that retrieval systems query.
  3. Real-time retrieval agents — answering live questions right now, using your page as a source.

Each gets a different HTML response. Different structure. Possibly different emphasis. Plausibly different facts if the transformation layer rewrites, summarises, or reorders content.

Same URL. Three versions. Three places where your content now lives.

The Compounding Problem

This matters because these three machines do not talk to each other.

The training crawler archives its version. In six months, an LLM trained on that archive will answer questions about your product using what it saw.

The search indexer stores its version. A retrieval system will cite that.

The real-time agent reads its version today.

Three versions. Three archives. Three answers about you — potentially contradicting each other.

And the divergence grows. Every time you update your page, each machine type gets a fresh transformation of the new content. The training dataset never catches up to the index. The index never catches up to real-time. The gap is structural, not a bug to fix.

Where Provenance Goes

There is a deeper problem. Once AXP transforms your content and sends it to a crawler, the provenance chain is severed.

What lands in the training archive is not your document. It is a CDN-generated derivative. When that content resurfaces in a model response, there is no traceable path back to your intent, your authorship, or your version.

You cannot attest to it. You cannot correct it. You cannot prove it was yours.

REGINALD cannot sign what it never saw. Your AI Usage Declaration cannot travel with content that left its origin in a different form.

One Source. Declared. Readable by Any Machine

The MX approach is structurally different.

A COG — a Community Owned Governance Standards document — is structured at the point of authorship, not at the point of delivery. The governance travels inside the document. Every machine type reads the same declared content. What changes is the projection, not the source.

A training crawler reads the COG. A search indexer reads the COG. A real-time agent reads the COG. They all get the same structured facts, the same declared intent, the same cryptographic provenance from REGINALD.

No transformation layer. No derivative. No three answers.

The Vendor Bet

AXP is a subscription over your existing content. It assumes your content is structurally inert and needs a vendor layer to make it readable to machines.

That is the bet. You pay for the layer. The layer holds the structure. You never own it.

MX is a design decision. Structure your content once. Declare its intent. Let any machine read it directly.

When the CDN goes dark, the COG still travels.
