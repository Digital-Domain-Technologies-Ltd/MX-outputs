Cogs and DMOZ: Two Ways to Organise the Web for Machines

27 April 2026 · 6 min read

Two models, opposite ends of the spectrum

Cogs and DMOZ both tried to organise information so machines could find it, but they sit at opposite ends of the spectrum on almost every design choice. The contrast sharpens further once you look at what each actually claims about the content it carries. That second question is the one that matters most, and it is where most discussions of machine-readable web infrastructure get the framing wrong.

A short DMOZ recap

DMOZ, the Open Directory Project, was a centralised, hierarchical web directory. Volunteer editors classified websites into a single taxonomy: Top, then Arts, then Music, then Bands, and so on down. The intelligence lived in the directory itself, in the categorisation, the editorial judgement, and the tree structure. Individual sites being listed didn't have to do anything; they were passive entries. DMOZ ran from 1998 until AOL shut it down in 2017, partly because keeping a hand-curated tree current at web scale became impossible, and partly because search engines had absorbed the job.

Cogs invert that model

A cog is a portable, self-describing markdown document with YAML frontmatter. The intelligence lives in the artefact, not in a central registry. Each cog declares its own purpose, audience, stability, relationships, and update instructions. There is no single tree it must fit into. A cog can be moved, copied, signed, and consumed by any machine that understands the format, without asking a central authority for permission.

That single change of locus, where meaning moves from container to content, is what makes the model durable. The hard work of saying what something is happens once, by the author, at publication time. Every reader, human or machine, gets the same answer.

The contrasts that matter

Locus of meaning. DMOZ put meaning in the container, in which branch of the tree a site sat in. Cogs put meaning in the content itself, in the frontmatter declaring what the document is and who it is for.

Authority model. DMOZ relied on editorial gatekeepers deciding what belonged where. Cogs rely on cryptographic signing and a registry to attest authenticity, but anyone can author a cog. The trust question shifts from "did an editor approve it?" to "is this signed by who it claims?".

Failure mode. DMOZ's failure mode was staleness. Humans could not curate fast enough. The cog format's failure mode is fragmentation. Without shared vocabularies and validation, a pile of self-describing documents that do not agree on terms. Both are real risks; the cog risk is the easier one to design around.

Audience. DMOZ was built for humans browsing categories, with machines as a secondary beneficiary. Cogs are designed for both, with the explicit MX premise that optimising for machines improves the human experience too. Convergence rather than compromise.

Lifecycle. A DMOZ entry was a pointer that aged silently. A cog carries its own update instructions, so the document tells you how to keep it current, and a fresh signature proves the update came from the same owner.

What the registry actually claims

This is where the framing question becomes load-bearing. DMOZ editors made a soft truth claim every time they classified a site: that the site belonged in the category, that it was what it said it was, that it was worth listing. The claim was implicit, never spelled out, and it aged badly because verifying it at scale meant keeping pace with the entire web by hand.

An MX registry like REGINALD makes a much narrower claim, and the narrowness is what makes it tractable. When REGINALD attests a cog, it is not saying this content is true. It is saying this is the content the owner of the source wants you to know. The cog genuinely came from that owner, has not been hallucinated downstream, and has not been altered in transit.

Andris Reinis raised the point that "verify" is the wrong word for this, and he is right. "Verified" implies a truth claim. If a site owner publishes a cog claiming World War II did not happen, REGINALD can confirm the cog genuinely came from that owner and has not been tampered with. Whether the claim is factually correct is a different problem, one no registry could solve at web scale, and one we deliberately do not take on. The registry attests provenance and integrity. It does not verify truth.

The right vocabulary, used consistently, is attestation: the registry attests that a cog matches what its named publisher signed. Truth is the publisher's responsibility, and the publisher's reputation. Attestation is the registry's responsibility, and a problem the registry can actually solve.

Why the smaller claim is the right one

DMOZ's collapse is the lesson. The directory tried, implicitly, to do two things: organise the web and make a soft editorial claim about what was worth listing. The second job is the one that scales badly. Editorial truth-curation at web scale is a problem with no clean technical answer, and DMOZ chose, by structure, to take it on.

Cogs and the MX registry split those concerns. Discovery is one job: which cogs exist, and how do agents find them? Attestation is a separate, narrower job: did the named publisher really sign this exact content? Truth, accuracy, and editorial judgement remain with the publisher and the reader. The registry stays out of the truth business and is correspondingly easier to operate, faster to query, cheaper to run, and more honest about what it claims.

This is why the language matters. If you say "REGINALD verifies cogs", listeners reasonably hear "REGINALD vouches for the truth". They will then expect REGINALD to do something it cannot do, and they will, eventually, conclude that REGINALD has failed when an obviously false but well-signed cog appears in the registry. Saying "REGINALD attests cogs" puts the right boundary in the right place. The publisher is responsible for what the cog says. The registry is responsible for confirming the cog came from the publisher unaltered. Two jobs, two parties, one boundary, no overload.

Takeaway

DMOZ tried to solve discovery by imposing structure from above, and quietly took on a truth-adjacent editorial burden it could not sustain. Cogs push structure down into every artefact and confine the registry's job to a question it can actually answer: is this what the owner published?

The smaller claim is what makes the model work. Use the language that reflects it. The registry attests. Publishers and readers handle truth.
