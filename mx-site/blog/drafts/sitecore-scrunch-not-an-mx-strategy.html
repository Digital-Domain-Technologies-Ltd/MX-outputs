Sitecore Bought Scrunch But They Didn't Buy an MX Strategy

17 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

On 3 June 2026, Sitecore announced the acquisition of Scrunch for approximately $225 million. Scrunch runs the Agent Experience Platform — AXP — which intercepts content at the CDN layer and serves AI-optimised HTML to training crawlers, indexers, and real-time retrieval agents. The deal also includes brand monitoring: a dashboard that tells you how often your name appears in LLM answers.

Sitecore CEO Eric Stine put it plainly: "Companies must rethink traditional digital strategies and accept that the internet must be written for machines to understand."

He is right about the problem. He is wrong about the solution he just bought.

Intercepting content at the edge is not the same as writing it for machines. A transformer at the CDN is a coat of paint applied to walls the building was never designed to hold up. The walls look fresh. The structure is unchanged.

The Edge Is Not the Origin

Scrunch's AXP works by sitting between the origin server and the requesting agent. It reads the request, identifies the machine type, and serves a tailored version of the page. Training crawler gets one payload. Real-time retrieval agent gets another. The monitoring layer watches for brand citations downstream.

This is clever engineering. It is not governance.

The content that AXP transforms was not written with machine intent. It was written for a human CMS workflow, published to a web server, and intercepted on the way out. The transformation happens at the exit, not the source. The document itself carries no record of what was changed, who authorised the change, or what the original author intended a machine to do with it.

Strip away the edge layer — kill the subscription, change CDN provider, move to PDF, email a whitepaper, push a file into a RAG pipeline — and the optimisation vanishes entirely. The content reverts to whatever it always was: undeclared, unattested, and without governance.

A Pipe That Cannot Follow the File

AXP has a hard scope limit: HTTP requests it can intercept. That boundary excludes everything outside it.

PDFs downloaded and ingested by an enterprise RAG system. Whitepapers scraped into a training corpus. Slides emailed to a prospect. Spreadsheets shared via Drive. A document pooled into a vendor knowledge base and queried three months later.

For all of those, AXP has zero reach. The content arrives at the machine with no record of what it is, who wrote it, or whether it may be acted upon. The monitoring dashboard cannot tell you what the model concluded from that whitepaper. The edge transformer was never involved.

Scrunch solved the problem it could solve: web content, at the edge, while the CDN is paying attention. The rest of the estate is on its own.

Pooling Breaks Provenance

There is a deeper issue. Once content is pulled from its source document and aggregated into a vendor knowledge base, the provenance chain is severed. The machine reading that aggregated store cannot verify what it is reading. It cannot confirm the original author, check for amendments, or establish that the fact it is about to act on was not silently updated last Tuesday.

Under the EU AI Act, deployers of AI systems must be able to demonstrate the lineage of the information those systems acted on. "It came through our CDN optimiser" is not that demonstration.

Any LLM sitting over a shared data lake — whether for generation, retrieval, or internal governance reporting — breaks the provenance chain unless the model's role is made explicit in a signed AI Usage Declaration attached to the content itself.

Sitecore has bought a visibility tool. Visibility and verifiability are not the same thing.

What Travels With the File

COGs — Community Owned Governance Standards — are portable, self-describing documents. Governance is embedded at the point of authoring, not applied at the point of delivery. A COG carries its own provenance record: who wrote it, when it was certified, what a machine may do with it, and how it connects to the wider estate.

That record travels with the file. Into the PDF. Into the RAG pipeline. Into the email attachment. Into the training corpus. The CDN does not need to be involved because the document already knows what it is.

REGINALD — the Registry for Genuine Information, Notarised Authentication, and Legitimate Documentation — provides the signed attestation layer. Instead of trusting that the content the agent received is the content the publisher intended, an agent queries the registry and confirms it. Not a guess. A lookup against a signed record.

AXP provides optimised HTML for the current HTTP transaction. REGINALD provides proof of origin for the lifetime of the asset. These are not competing tools. They solve different problems. Only one of them travels with the file.

The Pattern

Salesforce bought Contentful because it needed structured text delivery for Agentforce. Sitecore bought Scrunch because it needed something to show at the next board meeting on AI visibility. Both acquisitions follow the same logic: buy the capability you cannot build, bolt it on, and call it a strategy.

Neither bought governance that travels with the file. Neither bought portable provenance. Neither bought a standard their clients can own.

Owning a CDN transformer is not a machine readiness strategy. It is a subscription.

Machine Experience is not a layer you add at the edge. It is a property the document carries from the moment it is written.

MX is an open standard governed by The Gathering. REGINALD is the proprietary CogNovaMX implementation that runs on it. Neither grants regulatory compliance. Both produce verifiable, queryable evidence — the documentation a team must keep anyway, made structured and machine-checkable.
