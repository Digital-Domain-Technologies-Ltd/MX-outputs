Skills Execute. Meaning Has to Be Governed.

26 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

Darlene Newman has been making a point I keep coming back to. Every vendor she has spoken to in the past two weeks has a skills library: legal review skills, procurement skills, contract assessment skills. The pitch is always the same, and it is a good one. Package how your best people work into something an agent can apply consistently, at scale.

Then she asks the question that takes the floor out from under it:

Every AI skill contains a definition of "good." The question is whose.

A liability cap of twelve months' fees is standard for one company and an automatic escalation trigger for another. Net-60 terms are acceptable to one business and a non-starter to the next. There is no objectively correct good, better, best. There is only yours, built from standards your legal and commercial teams developed over years of deals. And if the skill ships with those thresholds baked in, then, in her words, "you're buying someone else's risk tolerance. Labeled as an assessment of your contracts."

She is right about all of it. I want to add the half her argument is already reaching for: what the fix actually looks like when you build it.

Her diagnosis is exact. A skill is an execution layer. It handles how work gets done. What it cannot do is define what those things mean inside your organisation. Her conclusion is the sentence I would frame on a wall:

That has to be governed separately and referenced at runtime, not embedded inside the skill itself.

Embed the meaning in the skill and you lose the ability to govern it. You cannot update it when your risk tolerance changes. You cannot audit it when an assessment looks wrong. It scales silently across everything in the queue, applying a definition nobody agreed to. The skill is not wrong. The assumption underneath it is invisible, and invisible is the problem.

So the real question is the one she leaves open: if meaning has to be governed separately and referenced at runtime, what is the thing that holds it? What is the shape of a separately-governed definition that an agent reads while it runs?

That is the layer I work on, and it has a name.

We call the practice MX, machine experience. Where UX asks whether a human can understand and act on something, MX asks the same of a machine. And the unit MX produces is a cog: a small, portable, versioned artefact that carries meaning and travels with the work. Not buried in a tool. Not a constant compiled into someone's skill. A separate object the agent reads at runtime, that answers the questions governance actually asks. Who set this definition. When. Which version. Is it still current. Is it still the one we agreed to.

Put Darlene's example through that lens and the difference is concrete. The skill that assesses a statement of work stays exactly as it is, an execution layer, doing the reading. But "good" - your liability cap, your payment terms, your negotiating position on this deal - lives in a cog the skill references when it runs. Change your risk tolerance and you update the cog, once, and every future assessment moves with it. An assessment looks wrong and you can open the cog and see precisely which definition produced it, who set that definition, and when. The meaning is no longer an invisible assumption. It is a governed object with an owner and a history.

That separation is the whole game, and it gives you three things the baked-in skill cannot:

  • Updatable. Meaning changes. When your tolerance shifts, you change the referenced cog, not fifty skills you have to hunt down and re-certify.
  • Auditable. When an answer looks wrong, you can walk from the answer to the exact definition that produced it. That is the difference between "the tool decided" and "here is the rule, here is who owns it, here is the version it ran under."
  • Attributable. A definition that travels needs to prove whose it is and whether it is current. That is the trust layer, and in our work it is Reginald, the registry that makes a cog's meaning signed, dated, and verifiable on the reader's own side.

There is one more move her argument makes for free. She says, near the end, that "reusable expertise requires reusable meaning." Exactly. And reusable meaning cannot be a private constant inside one vendor's skill, because the moment it is, every team that reuses the skill inherits a definition none of them set. Reusable meaning has to be a shared, open, governed object. Which is the last reason this layer cannot belong to any single vendor: if the platforms get to own the definition of "good" the same way they are racing to own the skills that apply it, you have simply moved the invisible assumption one level up. That is why the standard for these objects has to be community-owned. It is what The Gathering exists to do.

Darlene is right that the industry is building skills faster than it is building the meaning beneath them. Everyone is packaging execution. Almost nobody is governing the definitions that execution applies. Her instinct is the correct one: pull the meaning out of the skill, govern it on its own, reference it at runtime.

Build the skills. Then make sure the meaning they apply lives somewhere you can see it, change it, audit it, and prove it is yours.
