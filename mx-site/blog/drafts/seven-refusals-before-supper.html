Seven Refusals Before Supper

3 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

One evening this week I tried to ship a day's work, and my own system told me no. Then it told me no again. It said no seven times in a row, and every single time it was right.

I want to walk you through what happened, because "self-healing" has become one of those phrases that sounds like magic and usually means marketing. What we run is not magic. It is a loop, and the loop is made of three dull, dependable parts: gates that refuse, errors that instruct, and a ledger that remembers.

The gates do not negotiate

Before anything ships from our estate, it passes through a series of quality gates. Each one checks a single promise: links resolve for the reader, published pages match their sources, records carry the metadata they claim to carry, nothing arrives without its provenance. A gate is deterministic. It does not weigh intent, it does not accept context, and it has no mood. The work conforms or the door stays shut.

Stiffness is the point. A gate that can be talked round is not a gate; it is a suggestion with extra steps. On the evening in question, the gates caught, among other things, a page whose published copy had quietly fallen behind its source, and a genuine bug in a change we had made that same afternoon. Both would have shipped. Neither did.

The error message is the repair instruction

Here is the part most systems get wrong. A refusal that just says "failed" punishes you twice: once by stopping you, once by making you go and find out why. Our gates refuse loudly and helpfully. Each error names the exact thing that is wrong, where it is, and what to do about it - not a stack trace, an instruction.

That design choice matters more now than it ever did, because the worker reading the error message is usually an AI. My assistant hits the gate, reads the refusal, does the repair the message describes, and presents the work again. Another gate fires, another instruction, another repair. Round and round it goes. Seven laps, that evening. My involvement was two decisions the machine was not entitled to make on its own; the rest of the arguing happened between the system and the assistant, which is exactly where arguing belongs.

The friction ledger

Every refusal gets written down as it happens: what blocked, what fixed it, and whether the fix was a one-off repair or a permanent change. Clean runs get written down too, because a ledger that only records disasters cannot tell you whether things are improving.

At the end of the run, the rule is that someone - or something - reads the ledger back. Each entry has to end one of two ways: it becomes a permanent improvement to the system, or it becomes a decision put in front of a human. What it may not do is sit there as sediment. That reading-back step is the difference between a system that heals and a system that merely scars.

This is an old idea wearing new clothes. Toyota built its production system around stopping the line the moment a defect appears, on the understanding that the stop is not the failure - shipping the defect is - and that the line does not properly restart until the cause is fixed at the source. Google's reliability engineers make the companion argument about automation: its job is to eliminate the toil, not to hide it somewhere a human will trip over it later. Our loop is those two ideas holding hands: stop hard, repair at the source, and make the repair permanent.

What self-healing is not

It is not the machine quietly rewriting whatever it likes. Every automated repair in our estate works inside boundaries a human set, and the decisions that matter - what gets published, what gets deleted, what a fix should mean - stay with a person. A system that "heals" by silently changing things you never authorised has not healed anything. It has just moved the damage somewhere you cannot see it.

It is also not softness. By the end of that evening, shipping took one attempt, first try, every gate green. The gates had not relaxed. The estate had become the kind of place where the gates find nothing to refuse - and three of the problems they caught that night can now never happen again, because their fixes went into the system rather than into a to-do list.

Seven refusals before supper. I would take that trade every single time, because the alternative is not zero refusals. The alternative is shipping seven defects and finding out from your readers.

That practice - content and systems that explain themselves, machines that can act on the explanation, and humans who stay in command - is what the MX books teach. Start with one gate and one honest error message. The loop will teach you the rest.
