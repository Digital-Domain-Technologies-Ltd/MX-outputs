The Question AI Can't Answer

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 3 min read

Janus Boye sent me a message this week that I've been turning over ever since.

He runs Boye & Company and convenes the CMS Experts community - summits in London, Frankfurt, Florida, the kind of gatherings where people who run digital teams talk honestly to each other. He said this:

While technology has evolved dramatically (especially with AI), the fundamental questions remain unchanged - how to adopt new tech without creating complexity, maintain clarity amid change, and build confidence amid uncertainty.

Then the part that caught me:

Access to expertise is abundant, but an honest peer perspective is harder to find. Scandinavian learning traditions emphasise learning from one another rather than seeking the smartest individual.

He's right, and it's not a small point.

You can ask AI anything now. Any architectural question, any framework comparison, any regulatory interpretation - you'll get a competent senior-level answer in seconds. The question is no longer "how do I find someone who knows?" The question is "how do I find someone who has been there?"

These are different questions. Expert knowledge is correct. Peer knowledge is tested. When you're trying to adopt something new without making a mess of it, tested usually beats correct.

The Scandinavian model Janus is drawing on is straightforward: you learn from the person next to you, not from seeking out the wisest person in the room. Communities of practice work this way. Apprenticeships work this way. The best technical writing - the kind that actually helps - tends to come from someone who just got through the thing and remembers where it was hard.

What AI has done is flood the zone with correct. There's more expert-grade analysis available right now than any team can read. What hasn't scaled is the honest account. "We tried that. Here's where it fell apart. Here's what we'd do differently."

This is why The Gathering exists.

Not to produce another expert opinion. To produce the thing AI structurally cannot: a standard ratified by people who've deployed it. That weight is different. A group of practitioners who all independently arrived at the same conclusion is not the same as an authority who designed it from first principles. At the actual decision point, the former is more useful.

The Gathering's governance model reflects this directly. I founded it, but I don't own it. The community does. Drafts publish openly. Review is open. Ratification requires rough consensus - the W3C model, not a founder's signature. Joining is "worth being up close to; not bought into" because what you're close to is a network of honest peers, not a vendor's verdict.

The fundamental questions, as Janus puts it, haven't changed. How to adopt without creating complexity. How to maintain clarity. How to build confidence when everything is moving. Those are still the questions.

What's changed is that expertise is now a commodity. Honest peer perspective is not. And that gap is exactly where community-owned standards become valuable - not because they're cleverer than what a single expert could produce, but because they're tested.

That's what we're building.
