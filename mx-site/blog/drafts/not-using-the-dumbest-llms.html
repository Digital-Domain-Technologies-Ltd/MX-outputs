No, You're Not Using the Dumbest LLMs

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Someone asked this on Reddit, and it is one of the sharper questions about GEO I have seen, so I want to answer it properly.

The gist: GEO case studies keep reporting that citations went up 5%, 7%, 10%. This tactic works, that one works better. But when you actually use the models, you watch them recommend companies for services they do not offer, confuse general fame with specific expertise, and state easily-checked facts that are simply wrong. So which is it - are you using uniquely stupid models, or are we all crediting these systems with judgement they do not have?

You are not using the dumbest LLMs. You have spotted the trick, and almost nobody in the GEO conversation will say it out loud.

The Model Is Not Judging You. It Is Guessing

There is no expertise-recognition step inside a language model. That is the whole thing. When you ask "who is the best firm for X", the model does not weigh credentials, read your case studies, or assess whether you are genuinely the specialist. It predicts the most probable continuation of the text "the best firm for X is...", based on how often words have travelled together in its training data and whatever got pulled into context.

So it returns whatever co-occurs most strongly with the topic. That is a measure of presence. Of mentions. Of footprint. It is not a measure of being right, and it has no idea whether you offer the service it just recommended you for. A well-known generalist with ten thousand mentions beats a sharper specialist with forty, every time, because the machine is counting the wrong thing on purpose - counting is all it does.

You did not find the dumb models. You found the mechanism.

Why the Case Studies Are Both True and Hollow

Here is the uncomfortable part. The 5-10% citation lifts are probably real. GEO works, at exactly the thing the model actually uses. Feed the machine more co-occurrence, more presence, more text that sits your name next to the topic, and yes, your name turns up more often in the answers. The number moves.

But look at what moved. You raised a visibility signal. You did not teach the model that you are the specialist, because there is no part of the model that holds that belief. The case study measures citations and quietly lets you read it as recognition. As you put it: the discipline is measuring visibility and authority, and calling it expertise. That is not a rounding error in GEO. That is what GEO is.

GEO Inherited SEO's Worldview, and the Worst Case Got Worse

GEO mostly assumes the LLM is just a new ranking engine - feed it signals, climb the results. But the failure mode is not the same, it is worse.

A search engine that gets it wrong hands you a link at position eight, and you click through and judge for yourself. A language model that gets it wrong hands you a confident sentence it generated, with no link, no source, and no obligation to be correct. SEO's worst case was a bad ranking. GEO's worst case is a fabricated fact, stated with total composure, about a service you have never sold. Optimising harder for visibility does not fix that. It just makes the wrong answer more fluent.

What You Are Actually Missing

Only this: the model hallucinates because you gave it nothing to check against.

It is guessing because guessing is the only move available when the content it reads makes no clear, checkable, machine-readable claim about what you do. Your services are implied in marketing prose the model has to interpret. Your relevance is something it has to infer. Nothing in your content states, in a form a machine can read without guessing, "we do X, we do not do Y, and here is who says so." So the machine fills the gap the way it always does, with the most probable-sounding thing. More visibility signals do not close that gap. They pour confidence into it.

The fix is not to win the hallucination. It is to give the machine content it does not have to hallucinate about. Three properties do that, and none of them are GEO tactics.

Readable. The content states what you do, and what you do not, in structure a machine can parse without interpretation, instead of prose it has to second-guess. This is what MX (Machine Experience) is for: metadata that travels with the file and tells a machine what it is looking at, who it is for, and how it may be used.

Attributable. The claim carries an attestation - who published it, when, verifiable against a signed record. So an agent can confirm "this firm offers X" by looking it up, instead of inferring it from how often your name appears near the word. That is the job REGINALD does: it makes the content machine-trustworthy, not just machine-readable.

Corroborated. Your load-bearing claims point to authoritative outside sources that independently state them. Attestation proves you said it; corroboration is what lets an evaluator prefer the specialist who can prove relevance over the generalist who merely has reach. Readable, attributable, corroborated - that is the actual readiness, and it is the opposite of buying more footprint.

The Honest Caveat

None of this makes today's consumer chatbot behave. Ask it "best firm for X" and it will still answer off its weights and still not query anyone's registry. I am not going to pretend otherwise.

What this builds is the groundwork for the layer that is coming: agents that retrieve before they answer, that can check a claim against a record, that operate in settings where "the model sounded sure" is not an acceptable answer. GEO optimises for the model's worst habit, confident pattern-matching with no ground truth. The work I am describing optimises for the thing that replaces it, verifiable retrieval. One is a treadmill that runs faster every time a competitor outspends you. The other is an asset you own.

So, to Answer the Question

You are not missing anything about the models. You have them pegged. What is missing is in the method. GEO is a visibility discipline wearing an expertise costume, and you noticed the seams.

The question worth asking is not "how do I rank inside the hallucination." It is "how do I make my content the thing the machine does not have to hallucinate about." Make it readable, make it attributable, make it corroborated. Then it stops mattering how loud the generalist is, because the machine has something better than a guess to reach for.
