Game the Signals, Lose the Engine: GEO's Self-Defeating Advice

5 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

Generative Engine Optimization promises a clean payoff. Tune your content, and the AI answer engines start citing you. Add the schema. Publish a "best" list. Refresh the date. Claim the superlative. Stack the structured data, and watch your brand surface in ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, and Google's AI answers.

Most of those moves game a signal. And the signals GEO games are the signals search engines have started to demote. That is the problem. Treated as surface gaming, GEO walks you straight into the demotion that makes you invisible to the very systems it was hired to win.

This is a companion to GEO is a tactic, MX is the specification. That post sets GEO as surface tuning and MX as the foundation underneath. This one goes further: the surface tuning, done the way the playbook teaches it, is self-defeating.

The Tactics and the Demotions Are the Same List

Look at what GEO recommends. Then look at what search engines now punish. It is one list, read twice.

GEO says add FAQ markup. Google deprecated FAQ and Q&A rich results because publishers bolted the markup onto pages that held no real questions. The tactic GEO still recommends is the tactic that already lost its rich result. The same pattern runs through Schema.org keeps growing, the provenance layer does not exist yet.

GEO says publish a ranked list and put your own product at the top. Search engines are demoting self-promotional "best of" pages whose ranking is rigged in the publisher's own interest.

GEO says signal freshness. So a future year gets bolted onto the title while the body stays untouched. That year-swap reads as a quality flag now, not a ranking boost.

GEO says assert authority. So the page fills with "leading", "world-class", "the number one", and cites nothing. Unverifiable first-party superlatives are exactly what the helpful-content systems bury.

Each tactic optimizes a surface. Each surface is one a search engine has learned to distrust. The full taxonomy sits in the provenance gap. The gain is real for a quarter. Then the system catches up, and the gain reverses.

The Engine Is the AI's Eyes

Here is the part the GEO framing misses.

The AI answer engines do not read the open web from scratch. They retrieve through search. Google's AI Overviews and Gemini ground their answers in Google's index. ChatGPT and Perplexity ground theirs in web search and their own crawls. The retrieval layer is a search engine, every time.

So a demotion is not a separate cost you can trade away for AI reach. It is the loss of AI reach. When the search engine drops your gamed page down the results, the AI reading those results does not surface you near the top. You did not keep the citation and lose the ranking. The citation rode on the ranking, and it went down with it.

That is why the advice eats itself. GEO chases the citation by gaming the signal. The gaming triggers the demotion. The demotion removes you from the index the AI reads. The tactic forfeits the visibility it was bought to deliver.

The Target Moves Without Warning

Even when gaming works, it works briefly. The lever that governs AI citation is the system prompt: the hidden instruction a vendor runs before any query, telling the model what to search, when to cite, and which sources to prefer. Those prompts are not published, and they are rewritten without notice.

When one major assistant changed its underlying model in early 2026, independent monitoring across tens of thousands of responses found the count of cited domains fell by about a fifth, overnight. Nothing about the web had changed. The prompt had. Every site tuned to the old pattern started again.

Gaming compounds that fragility. You are not only tuning to a target that moves. You are tuning with signals the search layer is actively learning to discount. Two clocks run against you: the platform's next prompt rewrite, and the search engine's next quality update. The gamed page loses to whichever fires first.

Google's Advice Is Not Google's Tooling

GEO descends from SEO, so it takes its compass from search-engine advice. That compass is broken, and Google broke it itself.

In May 2026, Google's guide to optimizing for AI search told publishers they do not need to create machine-readable files, AI text files, or Markdown to appear in generative results. The Search team had dismissed llms.txt for months before that, comparing it to the old keywords meta tag.

In the same month, Chrome shipped Lighthouse 13.3.0 and moved its agentic-browsing audit into the default configuration. That audit checks for an llms.txt file at your domain root, flags the file when it carries too little to be useful, and wants its links in Markdown. One arm of Google calls the file pointless. Another audits it as an agent-readiness signal.

The lesson is the one this whole post turns on. The surface advice shifts and contradicts itself, because it tracks one product team's quarter. The machine layer underneath does not. A machine-readable summary of your site earns its place because agents can use it, not because a Lighthouse panel happens to reward it this release. I set out the working version of that file in why llms.txt is not working, and how to fix it, and you can read this site's own at /llms.txt.

The Format Was Never the Problem

None of this argues against structured data, or lists, or fresh content. The opposite holds.

Ranked, comparative lists are among the most-cited shapes in AI answers. Schema markup is what lets a machine read your page as data instead of a wall of text. Genuine freshness matters. The format is an asset.

What fails is the gamed version of each. The list that ranks you first because you published it. The FAQ markup on a page with no questions. The year-swap with no new substance. A machine cannot tell a genuine question-and-answer service from a landing page wearing FAQ markup, because the markup carries no proof of which one it is. The distance between describing a claim and evidencing it is provenance. Gaming a signal is the act of describing without evidencing.

Gaming now has an automated form, and it fails the same way. Set a tool loose to "add structured data" and it decorates indiscriminately, because it cannot weigh one claim against another; the page ends up so cluttered that machines stop reading the marks, the demotion arriving by saturation rather than by a quality update. The fix is the one this whole post argues for, moved up a level: mark what is genuinely material, and keep a human deciding what that is.

Earn the Citation, Do Not Game It

The durable move is the unglamorous one. Build the list because the ranking is real, and let the entries earn their places. Add the schema because the page genuinely is what the schema says. Cite your sources. Carry your provenance: who published this, when, and whether it has changed since.

A page built that way is cited because it deserves to be, and it stays cited when the next prompt rewrite reshuffles everyone tuning for last quarter's patterns. A page built by gaming is rented visibility, on a lease the platform can cancel without telling you.

GEO is worth running on content that can survive it. Run it on gamed content and it accelerates the demotion. The signal you game today is the signal the engine demotes tomorrow, and the engine is what the AI reads.
