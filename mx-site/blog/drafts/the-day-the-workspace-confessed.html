The Day the Workspace Confessed

6 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

This morning's job was about as routine as they come: extract my biography from a chat export and tidy up the record. By the end of the day, my hosted workspace had confessed to hiding nearly all of my repository's history, two book chapters existed that didn't exist at breakfast, and every loophole the day surfaced had been turned into machinery. I want to walk you through how that happened, because the sequence is the lesson.

A routine job hits a suspicious silence

The biography work needed one supporting search: mine the commit log for a phrase I knew had been used. The answer came back fast, confident, and nearly empty.

I've learnt to treat that combination as a smell. A confidently thin answer to a question that should have a rich one isn't an answer; it's a finding. So instead of shrugging, we probed. The workspace, it turned out, had been handed two days of a repository whose history runs back months. The platform clones shallow so containers start fast, which is reasonable, and git never volunteers that anything is missing, which is not. One command fetched the whole history in seconds. Every history-shaped answer before that moment had been quietly wrong.

Ask one confession, get several

Once the workspace had confessed to one omission, the obvious move was to ask for the rest. What else had nobody mentioned?

Plenty. The safety hooks the repository relies on, the ones that block a broken push or a leaked secret, were not installed, because git installs none of them on clone; every one of our carefully built guards was a text file that never ran. The sibling repositories the project composes with were absent. The document toolchain was missing. Some of those gaps healed with one command each. Some were walls. And one, the missing credentials, deserved to be read as a compliment: an environment that withholds secrets from a fresh workspace is doing least privilege properly.

The probing turned "a limited environment" from a vague feeling into an exact map. That map now lives in the repository, where the next reader, human or machine, inherits it instead of rediscovering it.

The part that matters: same-day machinery

Finding gaps is easy and a little theatrical. The discipline that matters is what happens next, and here's where I'll happily show off, because the loop worked.

Every blocked operation during the day went into a friction ledger: what ran, what blocked it, what fixed it. At the end, the ledger gets read back and every entry becomes an offer: here's the mechanical fix, accept or dismiss. By the close of play the day's ledger read like this. The session now arms its own hooks at start, so the guards actually run on a fresh workspace. It fetches its own missing history, so no more confident near-empty answers. A whole class of merge conflict, two sessions bumping the same version number, ended permanently with a small merge driver. The healer that writes folder metadata learnt to see brand-new directories. A blocked push caused by a merge is now impossible, because the merge hydrates what the push checks. Seven fixes delivered as working code; one queued for a machine that has the tools this one lacks.

One offer even closed itself by being investigated: what looked like a missing permission turned out, on inspection, to be a transient platform fault. The fix there was refusing to add dead configuration for a gap that didn't exist. Not building things is also engineering.

Why I'm telling you this

Because the loop is the product, not the war stories. Friction, logged; log, inspected; every entry, an offer; every accepted offer, machinery, ideally the same day. The human decides, the machine does, and the repository ends the day knowing more about itself than it did at breakfast. That's what "self-healing" actually means in practice; it isn't magic, it's bookkeeping with follow-through.

And because your workspace, whoever hosts it, is quietly making decisions about what you get. Ask it what it's holding back. Treat thin answers as findings. Write what you learn where your tools and your agents will read it, because the machine is an audience now, not just a runtime.

I'm writing the book on this facet of MX, MX: The Repo, and two of its chapters were written today, from this. The companion piece to this post is The Clone You Are Given Is Not the Repository, which goes deeper on what hosted environments hand you. Today was the day the theory earned its examples.
