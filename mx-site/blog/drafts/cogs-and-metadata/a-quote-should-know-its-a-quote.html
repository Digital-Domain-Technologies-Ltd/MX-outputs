A Quote Should Know It's a Quote

14 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

A growing pile of tools now grades your writing. Some hunt for the tells of machine-written prose, some score readability, some check house style, and more of them arrive every month. They all share one blind spot: they cannot tell your words from the words you quoted. So when your article carries a paragraph from a court ruling, a rival's blog, or a colleague's email, the grader reads that borrowed paragraph as if you wrote it, and marks you down for a sentence that was never yours to fix.

I hit this on my own writing. A piece of mine quoted another author at length, faithfully, the way you are supposed to. A style pass flagged the quotation for the very habits I had spent the rest of the piece avoiding. The tool was not wrong about the sentence; it was wrong about who wrote it. And there was no way for it to know better, because nothing in the document said "these words are borrowed."

The oldest courtesy in writing, made machine-readable

When you quote someone, you mark it. Quotation marks, a block indent, a citation. Those signals work for a human reader, and for a careful one they work well. They do not survive the trip to a machine that has stripped the formatting, or that never saw the rendered page, or that is grading prose a sentence at a time with no sense of where a quotation starts and ends. The courtesy is there for human eyes and absent for everything else.

The fix is small and, once you see it, obvious. A governed document carries a short machine-readable list of the passages it has quoted, and that list travels with the file. Now any system that reads the document, a style checker, an AI summarising it, an auditor walking its claims, can see which spans are the author's own and which are carried in from elsewhere, and treat each correctly. The author's voice gets judged on the author's words. The borrowed words are left to the standard of whoever wrote them.

This is the whole MX idea applied to something very ordinary. Metadata that records what a thing is should travel with the thing, so a machine meeting it out of context never has to guess. "Which of these words are mine?" is about as basic a question as a document can answer about itself, and until now it mostly could not.

Why it is worth the bother

Three things get better at once, and none of them needs anyone to be more careful.

The grading gets fair. A tool that knows where the quotations are stops penalising you for them, and stops you having to argue with a score that was measuring the wrong author. The machine reading gets accurate. An AI that summarises or answers questions about your page can keep your analysis separate from the material you were analysing, which is exactly the distinction it most often blurs. And the record gets defensible. When an auditor or a partner asks where a claim came from, a document that already marks its quotations can show that the borrowed lines are borrowed, and point at their source, instead of leaving everything in one undifferentiated wall of text. That is the kind of documentation organisations are increasingly expected to be able to produce on request, and it is far cheaper to capture as you write than to reconstruct under pressure.

The dream: every sentence knows where it came from

Marking a whole quotation is the first step, not the destination. The direction this points is a future tool, a skill I would love to build, that does the same job at the grain of the single sentence and phrase. Give it a document and it would resolve, span by span, whose words these are: the author's own, a quotation carried in from a named and verifiable source, or a passage a model generated. It would write that provenance into the document so it travels with the file, and keep it current as the document changes, without anyone maintaining it by hand.

Picture what that buys you. A style tool judges only your prose and leaves quotations and generated passages to their own standards, automatically. An AI reading your page never confuses your argument with the thing you were quoting. And an auditor can follow any sentence back to its origin: this line is the author's, this one is quoted from here and the source resolves, this one was drafted by a model and reviewed by a person. The document stops being a flat surface of words and becomes a record that knows its own authorship, line by line.

That tool is not built yet. I am describing the dream so the direction is on the page. The distance from "this paragraph is borrowed" to "this sentence is borrowed, from here, and the source checks out" is a matter of how fine-grained you go, not a change of principle. The principle is the same one worth ending on: a quote should know it's a quote, and so should every other sentence you publish.

If you build or buy tools that grade writing, ask them a simple question before you trust their verdict: do they know which words the author actually wrote?
