The Pudding That Was Always a Sausage

18 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

I was in France last week, reading a menu, and I stopped on boudin noir. I know what it is. Blood sausage. The English call it black pudding, and I've eaten it my whole life without once thinking it strange that we file a length of congealed pig's blood under the same word as the thing we have for afters at Christmas. A menu in another language is good for that. It holds your own language up at an angle and lets you see the join.

Because here's the thing that should bother us more than it does: black pudding is not a pudding. Except it is. It's the original pudding, and everything you picture when you hear the word - the sweet, the set, the steamed, the spotted dick and the sticky toffee - is the newcomer.

Go back far enough and "pudding" meant a sausage. A gut, an intestine, stuffed with minced meat and fat and oats and blood, tied off and boiled. That's it. That's the word. Black pudding and white pudding are the last survivors of the old meaning, standing in the cooked-breakfast section like two veterans who never got the memo that the word had moved on without them. Somewhere along the centuries "pudding" slipped its moorings and drifted from the savoury gut to the sweet bowl, and the new sense grew so fat and dominant that the old one now looks like the odd one out. The impostor is the trifle, not the sausage.

Where did the word come from in the first place? Here it gets pleasantly murky, which is my favourite state for a word to be in. The old dictionaries reached straight across the Channel and pointed at boudin itself - the very word on my menu - tracing it back through Old French to Latin botellus, "a little gut, a little sausage." (The same botellus, marvellously, gives us botulism, the disease named after the poisoning you got from a bad sausage. Etymology does not flatter its subjects.) On that account "pudding" and "boudin" aren't cousins; they're the same word, weathered differently on two sides of the water.

The modern view is more cautious and reaches the other way, to a Germanic root meaning "to swell" - the same swelling you hear in Low German puddek, a lump, and in German Pudding. A stuffed, swollen casing. Which, you'll notice, describes the sausage just as well. My suspicion is that the truth is a collision: two roots that meant roughly the same thing, leaning on each other until they became one English word. Words do this. They're not tidy. They arrive at the same meaning down different roads and shake hands in the dark.

Now put three languages side by side and watch what happens, because this is the part I can't leave alone.

French kept boudin meaning the sausage, and only the sausage. No drift. That's because the French didn't borrow the word - they inherited it, raised it at home from the Latin, and it grew up with one job.

English lived through the entire story, which is why "pudding" is the only one of the three that contradicts itself. It carries the old sausage and the new dessert in the same six letters, and the only people who notice are the ones standing at a French menu having a small private crisis.

Spanish borrowed budin (and the even more English-looking pudin) late - so late that "pudding" had already become the sweet thing by the time it crossed the Pyrenees. So Spanish only ever received the dessert. It never needed the sausage sense, because it already had a perfectly good word of its own: morcilla, the blood sausage, the true Spanish cousin of boudin noir. You don't import a word for a thing you can already name. Italian does the identical trick - budino for the pudding, sanguinaccio for the blood.

And there's the rule, the one this whole plate of offal turns out to be teaching. A borrowed word arrives frozen at the meaning it had on the day it crossed the border. The home word keeps ageing; the loan does not. So if you line up the same word in several languages, you're not looking at variation - you're looking at a row of stopped clocks, each one showing the time at the moment of the loan. French boudin shows the oldest hour. English "pudding" is the clock that kept running and now shows two times at once. Spanish budin shows the newest.

The lovely final twist: French has since borrowed pouding back from English, for the sweet sense it never had. So the word has crossed the Channel in both directions, leaving a sausage on one shore and a dessert on the other, and a man with a menu standing in the middle, no longer hungry, just thinking.
