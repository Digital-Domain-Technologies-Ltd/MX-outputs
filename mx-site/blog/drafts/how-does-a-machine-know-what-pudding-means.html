How Does a Machine Know What Pudding Means?

18 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

I've spent two posts now enjoying the word pudding - how it once meant a sausage, how black pudding is the original and the dessert is the upstart, how the same word froze at different ages in different languages. That was the fun. Here is the part that keeps me up, because it turns out to be the whole of my day job.

When I write "pudding", how does a machine know which one I mean?

Stop and feel how easily you did it just now. You didn't even pick. The context did it for you, below the level of noticing: a sentence about breakfast and you saw the sausage, a sentence about Christmas and you saw the bowl. You hold both senses at once and the surrounding words tip you, frictionlessly, to the right one. You have no idea you're doing it.

A machine has none of that. A machine has no senses of the word at all. What it has is statistics - a vast tally of which words tend to sit near which other words, built from whatever text it was trained on. So when it meets "pudding" it does not know; it infers. It reaches for the most probable meaning given everything around it and everything it has ever read. And in modern English text, the dessert has so thoroughly won that "pudding", unqualified, pulls hard toward the sweet bowl. Write "black pudding" and a good large model will lean back toward the sausage, because it has read enough butcher's prose and breakfast menus to have seen the phrase. But the leaning is a guess. A confident, well-calibrated, usually-right guess - and still a guess.

Now here is the thing that should give everyone pause. That guess is different in different languages.

Run the same job through a model steeped in Spanish and the ambiguity isn't reduced, it's absent. As I wrote in the first post, Spanish borrowed budín late, after "pudding" had already become the dessert, so in Spanish the word means the sweet thing and only the sweet thing. The sausage lives under a completely different word, morcilla. A Spanish-leaning model has therefore never seen budín used for a sausage, not once, and so it cannot even consider the reading. It isn't choosing dessert over sausage. Sausage is not on the menu it can see.

Sit with what that means. The thing in the world - blood, fat, oats, a length of gut, boiled - is identical on both sides of the Pyrenees. But the machine's understanding of it is not a fact about the thing. It is an artefact of which language's text the machine happened to eat. Change the training language and you change what the machine believes the word can possibly mean. The world didn't move. The data did.

And then make the model small.

This is not hypothetical for me - the default for the audits we run is a local model on the operator's own hardware, because client content should not leave the building, and local means small. A small model with limited training is the worst possible reader of an ambiguous word. It has seen fewer examples, so its priors are blunter. It has less room to hold a rare sense alongside a common one, so it tends to collapse the word to its single most frequent meaning and march on. Give a small English model "I fried the black pudding" and there is a real chance it pictures a scorched dessert and quietly builds the rest of its answer on top of that, never flagging a doubt, because nothing in its machinery represents doubt. It doesn't know less loudly. It hallucinates confidence. The small model is not a worse version of the big one's caution; it has no caution to be worse at.

So line the readers up the way I lined up the languages last time. A large English model: leans right, usually. A Spanish model: can't see the sausage at all. A small on-device model: grabs the commonest sense and runs. Three machines, three different answers, and none of them is reading the word wrong - each is reading it exactly as faithfully as its data and its size allow. The variance isn't a bug in any one of them. It's the inevitable shape of meaning-by-inference.

Here is where it stops being a language post and becomes the reason MX exists.

Every one of those machines is straining to reconstruct something I already knew and threw away. When I wrote the sentence, I knew whether I meant the sausage or the bowl. There was no ambiguity at the source - it appeared the instant my words left me and arrived at a reader who didn't share my context. I published the word and binned the meaning, and now every machine, in every language, at every size, has to spend effort and risk error guessing back the thing I could simply have told them.

MX's answer is almost rude in its simplicity: stop making the machine guess. Don't ship the bare word and hope inference lands. Ship the word with the meaning attached - structured, carrier-neutral, travelling with the file wherever it goes. In an MX-governed document the "pudding" on the page can carry, right beside it and readable in one pass, the plain declared facts: this is a blood sausage; it is a savoury dish, not a dessert; here is the tradition it belongs to. That is the whole of MX in one bite - metadata that records what a thing is, the context it sits in, and how to use it, and that does not get separated from the thing.

And watch what it does to my three divergent machines. The large English model no longer needs to lean - the fact is stated. The Spanish model no longer needs the sausage reading to exist in its training, because it isn't inferring, it's reading a declaration. The tiny local model, the one with no room for doubt, needs no room for doubt, because there is nothing left to disambiguate. The variance that language and model size forced into the system collapses to zero, not because the models got better, but because the question got removed. You cannot guess wrong about a thing you were simply told.

When the word is pudding and the stakes are a breakfast order, a wrong inference is a funny story. But the same machinery, with the same blind spots, reads dosage instructions, allergen lines ("contains blood" is not a small thing to a kosher, halal, or vegetarian reader, or to the agent ordering on their behalf), legal clauses, safety steps. There the language a model was trained in, and the size it was shrunk to, quietly decide whether it understood you - and you have no way of knowing in advance which machine, in which tongue, at which size, will be the one that confidently gets it wrong. That uncertainty is not a problem you can train your way out of, because it lives in the gap between the word and the meaning you didn't send.

So that is what I actually think about, standing in front of a French menu reading boudin noir. Not just the lovely fossil history of the word. The fact that I know exactly what it means, the French waiter knows, and the moment that word travels to a machine - especially a small one, especially in the wrong language - the knowing falls out and the guessing begins. My whole job is to make the knowing travel with it.
