WordPress's Update Delay and the Provenance Gap

16 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

A post doing the rounds on Reddit this week put its finger on something real, even if it reached for the wrong villain. WordPress.org has launched an initiative called Protect The Shire, and the headline change is a 24-hour cooldown before plugin and theme updates reach sites through auto-update. The window exists so an AI-assisted reviewer can check each update before it ships, after a run of supply-chain attacks where backdoored updates slipped straight into live sites.

The Reddit author's worry, stripped of the heat, is an economic one. Attacks now move faster than the gate. Patchstack's 2026 report puts the weighted median time from disclosure to first exploit at five hours, with roughly 45% of high-impact vulnerabilities exploited inside 24 hours. Meanwhile Wordfence's free tier sits 30 days behind on firewall rules and malware signatures; real-time protection is the paid product. Stack a 24-hour update gate on top of a 30-day signature lag and the only door that stays open quickly is the one with a price on it. You can see why someone concludes the squeeze is deliberate.

I do not think it is deliberate. But I do think the delay is a tell, and it points at something MX has been saying for a while.

A delay is what you build when you cannot verify

Ask what the 24-hour window is actually for. It buys time to answer one question: is this update genuine, or has someone slipped something into it? WordPress is answering that question with time and review, because there is no faster way to answer it. The platform has no machine-checkable record, travelling with the update itself, that says who built it, what is in it, and that nothing changed in transit.

That is the gap. A cooldown is the workaround you reach for when you cannot verify provenance on the spot. If every update arrived with a signed, tamper-evident record of its origin, a machine could check it in the time it takes to read a file, and the choice would stop being "wait 24 hours" against "ship blind". You would verify, then ship. The delay is a confession that the supply chain cannot do that yet.

This is the whole reason MX exists, and it is why we pair it with REGINALD. MX makes a thing machine-readable: it carries metadata describing what it is, where it came from, and how it may be used, and that metadata travels with the file rather than living in a database someone has to be trusted to query. REGINALD makes it machine-trustworthy: a signed attestation of who published it, verifiable by anyone with standard libraries, no trust in the registry required. Readable, then attributable. That pair is the instrument missing from the WordPress story. Not a slower pipe. A verifiable one.

The other half of the Reddit complaint

The author also vented about search inside the plugin directory: that big plugins surface ahead of small, relevant ones, and that size wins over fit. I cannot speak to how WordPress ranks results, but the underlying mechanic is familiar, and it is the same problem from a different angle. A machine ranks on the signals it can read. When the only legible signal is how big you are, big wins.

MX's answer is a principle we call corroboration over assertion. A small publisher whose content is machine-readable, attributed, and corroborated by authoritative external sources can be as trustworthy to a machine as a giant, without buying the position. The fix for "size decides" is not a fairness policy bolted on later. It is giving the machine better signals to read, so that relevance and provenance can outweigh raw size.

What MX does not claim

To be straight about scope: MX does not fix WordPress, and it does not make anything secure. Attestation proves who shipped an update; it does not prove the update is safe, and an attested backdoor is still a backdoor. What attestation removes is the doubt about origin and tampering, which is the specific doubt the 24-hour delay is paying for with time. The review still has to happen. It just stops having to happen blind, and it stops having to happen slowly.

The pattern is bigger than one content platform. Any registry that manages supply-chain risk by adding a delay, whether a package index, an app store, or a model hub, is paying in latency for a problem that provenance solves in verification. WordPress is the example in front of us this week, handed over by a Reddit thread. The lesson travels.

WordPress bought itself a day. Fair enough; given the tools on the table, a day is a reasonable thing to buy. But the bill comes due as latency, and latency is what the attackers are counting on. The durable fix is not a longer queue. It is content that can prove what it is the moment a machine looks at it.
