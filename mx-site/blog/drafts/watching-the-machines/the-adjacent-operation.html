The Adjacent Operation

2 November 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

An account reached me in November 2026 of an assistant going wrong three times in one sitting, each time in a way that looked like competence from a distance. The person who sent it had already stripped every amount and every name, along with the product names, the filenames and the links, before it left their hands. I keep that boundary here. The mechanism is what matters, and it survives the redaction.

Three turns

The assistant was working inside a mail application. It had retrieved a draft and knew the job was to check it against a source document. A different message happened to be open on the screen.

Asked to read the document and check the draft, it inspected the message on screen instead, reported that no attachment was there, and answered about the wrong subject. Corrected, it returned to the draft, searched for a related source document and read it. Finding a related document isn't the same as establishing that it was the one attached to the draft, and the account is careful to say so.

Asked to revise the report it had just written about that mistake, whose full text and every revision were sitting in the conversation, it searched the mailbox for a stored draft. The search returned nothing useful. It said the report couldn't be located and offered to write a replacement. The requester pointed out that the report was in the chat.

Asked to depersonalise the report, it rewrote the prose in an impersonal third person and kept the financial values and case details through several revisions. The requester had to explain that depersonalise meant remove what could identify or trace the case, figures included.

One failure, three surfaces

In each turn the assistant performed an operation adjacent to the requested one. Inspecting the visible message instead of the active draft. Locating a stored copy instead of revising known text. Changing the tone instead of removing identifying detail.

What the three share is where the assistant looked for its answer. It resolved each request against the nearest signal: the thing on screen, the place a report normally lives, the register the word "depersonalise" suggests. The conversation had already established the task, the object and the text. None of that was consulted first.

The account's author is precise about what the evidence does and doesn't show. It shows failures of context selection and task interpretation. It doesn't show that the chat was inaccessible, that the application lost information, or that a platform defect caused any of it. I hold to that. The column's own standard, set out in How Watching the Machines Works, is that a claim gets no further than its evidence.

The second and third substitutions deserve a sentence each, because people make them too.

A missing storage identifier limits saving in place. It never limits access to text that's in the conversation. "I can't find the draft" and "I'm unable to revise the report" are different statements, and only the first was true.

Third-person wording doesn't anonymise a document. Exact values, and combinations of ordinary facts that only one case has, identify it as surely as a name. The repair was to remove the values, not to invent replacements and not to stamp them confidential, and to hold the examples and appendices to the same line as the body. The author's own closing note is the model: it describes the verification outcome without reproducing the figures, and it declines to claim that the result is impossible to recognise.

What the machine needed

The useful part of the account is its list of what a task record would have to carry, in a form a machine reads rather than infers. I reproduce it because it's better than anything I would have written from the outside:

  1. The active object and its current revision.
  2. The source message or document supporting it.
  3. The requested operation and what counts as done.
  4. The intended audience and what may be disclosed.
  5. What must be removed, generalised or kept.
  6. Evidence already read, and claims not independently verified.
  7. Content available in the conversation, as distinct from content saved elsewhere.
  8. Actions allowed without a further confirmation.

Unrelated material on screen is background state and should be marked as such. When the target becomes unclear, the recovery is to go back to the conversation, find the latest relevant text and apply the most recent correction. A search elsewhere answers a specific missing fact. It doesn't stand in for context already in hand.

This is Machine Experience as I have been describing it for two years, arriving from the other direction. MX makes context explicit in machine-readable form so an asset carries its identity and provenance, its relationships to other assets, the authority behind it and the use it permits, beyond the environment it was made in. For a source document, that means where it came from, which version it is, what it covers and what it assumes. For a report, it means which sources it was built from, how it was revised, who it's for and what it may disclose. A public report derived from a restricted source can declare that relationship and still leave the contents out.

The author adds the limits, and they're the right ones. The application still has to expose live task state and enforce its permissions. Source material can't grant itself authority over the requester's privacy instruction. Metadata helps only when the tool exposes it and the assistant reads it. And a document marked anonymised is a claim until someone checks it, which is the whole distance between machine-readable and machine-trustworthy. These are workflow requirements, not an assertion that any field name here is established MX vocabulary.

What to do

For anyone building an assistant into a working application, or deciding whether to trust one that's already there:

  • Before any search, refer to the conversation. The latest relevant text and the most recent correction are the task. A retrieval elsewhere is for a named missing fact.
  • State the transformation before applying it: a factual check, a wording revision, or the removal of identifying information. Each is a separate operation with its own acceptance test.
  • Run the privacy check over the whole output, the examples and appendices as well as the body, and the metadata too, and against the purpose rather than the register.
  • Keep agreement with a source document separate from independent verification, and never report a proposed revision as a confirmed save or send.
  • Say what remains uncertain, and never promise anonymity.

Better prompting adds safeguards. The architectural goal is explicit context carried by the asset, exposed by the application and used consistently by the assistant. Nothing in that moves responsibility for these three turns onto the person who asked.

The Palimpsest, the browser-with-an-assistant I'm building on MX, takes the list above as a requirement from this account onward: every Ask carries its active object, its source, the operation and the disclosure limit across turns, so the nearest signal is the task itself.

Your content in the same exchange

The declared context that would have told that assistant which draft was active, which text was current and what "depersonalise" meant is what your pages need as well. It decides whether the machines reading them on your buyers' behalf act on what you published, or on the nearest thing to it.
