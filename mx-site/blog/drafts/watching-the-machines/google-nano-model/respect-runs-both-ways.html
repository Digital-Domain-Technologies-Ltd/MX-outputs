Respect Runs Both Ways

29 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 9 min read

Part of the series The Silent Install, a cluster on what a program may do to your device without asking. This post is the principle.

MX asks publishers to do work for the machines that now read their content: make it legible, declare where it came from, structure the facts so a reader that is not human can follow them without guessing. That is one signature on a contract. A contract with one signature is not a contract. It is a demand.

The second signature belongs to the machine, and to the company operating it. If we ask people to open their content to machine readers, those readers, and the firms behind them, owe the person something back. Consent over what runs on their device. Honesty about what is happening on it. Restraint about what they take. The plain word for the whole of that is respect, and right now some of the largest machine operators on the web are not offering it.

The half-contract

Read the MX material on this site and a single instruction runs through all of it: write for both audiences. Put provenance in a data file the machine can read. Make the structure honest so the machine does not have to infer it. Reduce the work the reader has to do, whether that reader is a person with a screen reader or an agent fetching the page on someone's behalf.

The Convergence Principle says this effort pays off in both directions at once. Content built to be read cleanly by a machine tends to be clearer for a human too, and the accessibility outcomes follow. That claim holds. But it has always had a quieter assumption underneath, which is that the machine on the other side of the exchange behaves itself. We design content to serve the reader. The reader is supposed to behave as a guest.

A guest who lets themselves in, rearranges the furniture, and leaves a four-gigabyte crate in the hall is not a guest. They are a problem you now have to manage. That is the situation more people are in than realise it, and the clearest documented example arrived this month.

What the missing signature looks like

Alexander Hanff, writing on his That Privacy Guy blog on 4 May 2026, set out a forensic account of what Google Chrome does to the device it runs on. His investigation, which he corroborated against the macOS kernel's own filesystem event log on a profile that had received no human input at all, describes Chrome writing the weights for Gemini Nano, an on-device model of roughly four gigabytes, to the user's disk with no prompt, no checkbox, and no first-run dialogue. The file lands in a directory whose name gives no hint of what it holds. Delete it, and Chrome writes it back when the next eligible window opens. Hanff reports the install completing in under fifteen minutes on an idle machine, with no person touching the keyboard. You can read his full account, with the evidence chain and the legal argument, at thatprivacyguy.com.

A few details earn their place in any MX discussion of the episode.

The device gets profiled before anything is offered. Hanff found Chrome reading the machine's processor class, graphics, and memory to decide whether it was a worthwhile target, and only then pushing the model. The person was assessed as a delivery surface, not asked as an owner.

The visible feature and the silent install do not even match. The "AI Mode" control Chrome shows in its leading piece of screen, the address bar, routes the user's typing to Google's servers, while the on-device model that was quietly placed on their disk powers smaller features buried in menus most people never open. The local model sits there as a cost the user carries, offering no obvious benefit where they might reasonably expect one.

And the user's own decision is treated as a fault to be corrected. A person who finds the file, understands what it is, and removes it has expressed a clear preference about what runs on their property. Chrome overrides that preference on the next launch. The software has decided it knows better than the owner of the machine what belongs on the machine.

Hanff makes the legal case himself, and frames it as his professional opinion rather than a settled ruling: that storing this on a person's device without consent engages Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, and that the fairness and transparency principles of the GDPR are in play. I will leave the law to him and to the regulators who have so far declined to act on it. The point for MX is simpler and does not need a courtroom. This is a machine, operated by a company that wants the web's content opened to its readers, refusing to extend the most basic courtesy back to the people whose devices its readers run on.

Where this sits against MX principles

Three of the principles this practice is built on speak directly to the episode, and all three are violated by it.

The first is that the human stays in the loop, and that outputs must be readable and reviewable by both audiences. A person cannot review or refuse what they were never told about. There was no moment of review. The model arrived during an idle afternoon while a tab waited for a timer. Removing the human from the loop is not a side effect of the design here. It is the design.

The second is the principle that, faced with a choice, we pick what is better for the planet: reduce compute, reduce inference, reduce energy, build on what people already have. A silent push of four gigabytes across the qualifying share of an install base counted in billions is the opposite move. Hanff puts the one-time delivery cost, on his mid-band estimate, in the tens of thousands of tonnes of carbon-equivalent, before counting the re-downloads triggered by people trying to delete the thing, the embodied cost of the disk space held in perpetuity, and the future model refreshes. For users on metered mobile connections, much of which is how households without fixed broadband reach the internet, four gigabytes is on the order of a month's data allowance, spent on their behalf without being asked. An approach that respected the energy budget would pull the model only when a person chose a feature that needed it. This pushes it on a contingency, to everyone eligible, and pays for that convenience in atmosphere and other people's data caps.

The third is the Convergence Principle read in reverse. The claim is that optimising for the machine improves outcomes for the human. The honest corollary is that optimising for the vendor's plans at the human's expense fails the human. That is what this is. The arrangement serves Google's option to run on-device features later without a server round trip. It costs the user disk, bandwidth, and the trust that their browser does what they tell it. Convergence held up as a benefit only works if the operator is actually optimising for the reader. When the operator optimises for itself and calls the leftovers a feature, convergence becomes the cover story.

What two-way respect looks like in practice

None of the alternative is difficult, and Hanff lays out what it looks like. Translated into the language used here, it amounts to a few things an operator that respected the human would simply do.

Ask before you write to someone's device. A single dialogue at the point the feature is first needed, with a genuine option to decline, turns an imposition into a choice. Pull rather than push: let the act of choosing a feature be the event that triggers the download, instead of pre-staging on the guess that the person might want it one day. Surface what you have placed there, in the settings the person actually opens, with the size, the purpose, and a control that removes it and keeps it removed. Respect deletion as an instruction, not a glitch. And account for the cost in public, as the real energy and carbon line it is, rather than leaving an independent investigator to estimate it from a filesystem log.

These are not constraints that break the product. They are what the relationship looks like when both parties have signed.

MX makes respect into evidence

Here is where this stops being a complaint and becomes work this discipline can do.

MX already makes the human-side obligations legible. A page or a file can carry, in a record a machine can read, who made it, when, what it refers to, and what permissions attach to it. That is provenance, and it is half the exchange.

The same discipline can make the machine-side obligations legible too, because a consent event is a fact, and a decision a machine takes on someone's device is exactly the kind of thing a decision record exists to hold. When did the person agree. What were they shown. What policy authorised the action the software then took. Was a human in the loop at the point it mattered. These are not feelings. They are recordable, they can be attested, and they can be audited by someone who does not have to take the operator's word for any of it.

That reframes the whole argument away from sentiment. Respect is not a stance a company adopts in a values statement. It is something an operator can either show or not. If a vendor placed a model on your device with your agreement, it can produce the consent and the record of the decision. If it cannot produce them, it did not have your agreement, and no amount of published language about responsible practice changes what the absent record says. MX gives both sides of the relationship a place to leave evidence, which means the question of whether a machine respected a person stops being a matter of opinion and starts being a matter you can check.

A diagnostic question

One test does most of the work, the same way it does for content. Looking only at what the software told the person, before it acted, could they have known this was going to happen and said no?

If yes, the relationship is two-way, whatever else you think of the product. If no, it does not matter how the behaviour is described afterward, how small the file is relative to the disk, or how useful the feature might one day be. The person was acted upon rather than asked. That is the line, and Chrome's on-device push falls on the wrong side of it.

The bottom line

MX was never only about teaching humans to serve machines. That framing was always half the picture, and the missing half is the one the largest operators would rather not discuss. A web where machines read everything people publish, and give nothing back in the way of consent or restraint, is not a relationship. It is extraction with better manners.

The Convergence Principle cuts both ways or it does not hold. Designing for the machine is supposed to serve the reader. When the machine, and the company behind it, treats the reader's own device as territory to occupy, the principle has been turned into a one-way demand and the second signature is missing from the page. Ask people to open their content to machines, by all means. That is the work. But the operators on the other side owe the same courtesy in return, and until they offer it, the contract is not signed.
