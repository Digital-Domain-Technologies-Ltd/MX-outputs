The Signature Covers Who Shipped It, Not What It Fetches

29 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 10 min read

Part of the series The Silent Install, a cluster on what a program may do to your device without asking. This post is the mechanism.

A companion to Respect Runs Both Ways. That post argued the relationship between a person and the machines reading their content has to run in both directions. This one asks a narrower question that follows straight from it. When a trusted program on your Mac quietly downloads a few gigabytes that have nothing to do with the job you installed it for, what exactly vouched for that, who answers for it, and is the answer a job for attestation and COGs.

The precedent is now general

The detail worth holding on to from the Chrome case is not that Chrome did it. It is that Chrome did it openly, at the scale of a browser counted in billions of installs, and met no consequence. Once an operator of that size establishes that a trusted application may reach onto the device and write a large payload the user never asked for, the question stops being "can Chrome do this" and becomes "can anything do this". The honest answer, on a normal machine, is close to yes.

A program you have installed and granted permission to run is running with your authority. Within the access its sandbox and entitlements allow, it can open a network connection, fetch a payload of any size, and write it to its own storage or your folders, on its own schedule, with no further prompt. Nothing about that is a flaw being exploited. It is the ordinary capability of a trusted application, which is exactly why it is a governance problem rather than a security hole, and exactly why "it was technically allowed" is not the defence the operators seem to think it is.

It is not only the operating system's trust that works this way. Any channel a person has agreed to trust can be used to deliver something they did not choose, and open source has already had its own version of the shock. In October 2024, WordPress.org took over the Advanced Custom Fields plugin, used on millions of sites, and reissued it under a new name. The mechanism is the part that matters here: sites that had automatic updates from WordPress.org switched on were moved to the replacement through the same update channel they had trusted for ordinary patches, without being asked at the moment of the swap. The operator framed it as a security fork made necessary by a legal dispute; the plugin's maintainers and much of the community called it a forced takeover, and a court later ordered the plugin returned. Set the commercial fight aside and the structural point is the same as Chrome's: a channel a person trusts for one thing was used to push a different thing onto their installation, and the trust granted once became standing permission for a decision taken later by someone else. The browser does it with a bundled binary; the CMS did it with the update feed. The pattern is identical.

What the trust model actually checks

It helps to be precise about what the Mac's protections cover, because the gap is in the precision. Apple's platform security documentation is clear that Gatekeeper checks downloaded software for a valid developer signature and Apple notarisation the first time it is opened, confirming the software comes from an identified developer and has not been altered since it was signed. Independent analysis adds the part the documentation leaves implicit: that check happens at first launch, not continuously while the program runs.

Read those two facts together and the limit comes into focus. The signature attests identity and integrity at a single moment: this is who shipped it, and this is the unmodified thing they shipped. It does not attest behaviour over the program's life, and it does not attest anything about what the program fetches afterwards. A model-weights file pulled down later is not a new executable being launched, so the launch-time check never looks at it; it is data handed to code that was already signed and already trusted. The trust you extended to the application at install becomes a standing permission for everything the application later decides to do in your name. The notary vouched for the bouncer. Nobody vouched for who the bouncer lets in after the doors open.

The thing that is neither

It is worth stopping on that phrase, data handed to code, because in the Chrome case the data is a model's weights, and a model's weights sit nowhere on the line this article has been drawing. They are inscrutable as content: you can read every byte of the file and learn nothing about what the model will say, because the meaning is not held in any place you can point at. They are unexecutable as code: inert numbers that do nothing until a separate runtime feeds them, which is the precise reason the launch-time check never looks at them. And they carry a property neither code nor content has, which is that the weights are the behaviour. Content is read; code can be read to recover its logic; weights decide what the program does while disclosing none of it. It is the first carrier where the logic and the opacity are the same object.

The open-source world reached this wall before the rest of us and declined to climb it. When the Open Source Initiative set out to define open-source AI, the question that stalled the effort was this one in another form: what is the preferred form in which a person would modify the thing. For software the answer is settled, the source. For a model it was not, because a machine-learning system is not simply a program; it blends data, configuration, and artefacts like weights and biases that fit no existing box. The Initiative would not call weights source code and could not call them ordinary data, so it minted a separate term, open weights, to mark the difference. The beast was given its own word because it belonged to neither category.

This is the strongest argument MX has, not an awkward exception to it. Trust is available by reading a thing and judging it, or by relying on a verifiable record wrapped around it. Code and content both, in principle, allow the first route: source can be inspected, facts can be checked. Weights allow neither, so reading is off the table and only the record is left. The inscrutability that makes the weights a beast is the very thing that makes attestation the only honest way to trust them. You cannot certify the payload by looking at it, so you certify the claims that travel with it: what it is, where it came from, what it is permitted to do, and that the person agreed. You cannot make the beast readable. You can make its papers readable, and decline to trust a beast that turns up without them.

What we already have, and where it stops

This is not a plea to reinvent code signing. The identity-and-integrity problem is solved well, and MX has a standing rule to defer to the mechanisms that already work rather than build a rival. Code signing establishes the developer. Notarisation adds a malware scan and a revocable ticket. A software bill of materials lists the components inside the artefact. The build-provenance work coming out of the supply-chain security world, the kind of signed build attestations associated with efforts like in-toto, SLSA, and Sigstore, records how the artefact was produced and from which source. All of it is good, and all of it answers the same family of questions: who made this, from what, and has it been tampered with.

The WordPress shock produced a direct response in the same spirit, and it is worth pointing at because it is the right kind of answer. The FAIR Package Manager, launched under the Linux Foundation in June 2025, stands for Federated and Independent Repositories. Its purpose is to remove the single controlled choke point through which WordPress plugins, themes, and updates have flowed, and replace it with signed packages served from a federation of independent repositories, so that no one operator can decide unilaterally what lands on a site. That is the open web defending itself by decentralising trust and attesting authenticity across more than one source, and it sits squarely inside the values MX works from: defer to the standard others are building, prefer the federated answer to the centralised one, keep control with the site owner.

MX defers to FAIR, the same way it defers to the cryptographic and identity standards it builds on rather than reinventing. The division of labour is clean enough to put in a line: FAIR carries trust in code as it travels, MX carries trust in content as it is read. Different artefacts, different jobs, no overlap. The beast in the section above shows the axis underneath that line. Code and content could always, in principle, be trusted by reading them; weights cannot, and neither can anything that arrives faster than a person can inspect it. What FAIR and MX share is that both trust by attestation rather than by reading: FAIR vouches a package is authentic across a federation without anyone reading the package, MX carries provenance and behaviour declarations without anyone reading the payload. They are the tools you reach for once reading is off the table, which by now is most of the web. They are complementary, the relationship is a handoff and not a contest, and MX has no reason to duplicate FAIR and every reason to point to it.

What FAIR settles is where a package comes from and that it is genuine across a distribution that no single party controls. What it does not set out to settle is the question this article is about: once that genuine package is installed and running, what is it entitled to do, what may it fetch, and did the person agree to the thing it pulls down next. That is not a criticism of FAIR, which is solving the distribution-trust problem it set out to solve. It is the boundary of what distribution attestation can reach, and it is where MX picks up. A declaration of what the genuine package is allowed to do, and a record that it did so with consent, is content about the code rather than the code itself, which keeps it on MX's side of the line. The two meet at the binary and still do not overlap: FAIR vouches the package is authentic; MX declares what the authentic package may do and records that it did it with the person's agreement.

None of it answers the question the Chrome case raises. What is this program entitled to do once it is running. What may it fetch, from where, and why. Did the person agree to the thing it is about to pull onto their disk. A bill of materials describes the crate. Build provenance describes the factory the crate came from. A federated repository decides which crates are genuine and breaks the monopoly on shipping them. None of them describes the crate letting itself into your hall and deciding to leave a larger crate behind. That behaviour, and the consent that should gate it, is the part no signature in the chain currently carries.

The missing layer is behaviour and consent

So the answer to whether we need attestation and COGs for binaries is yes, but not the attestation people assume, and not in place of the signing that already works. The executable's identity is covered. What is missing is an attested declaration of what the executable may do, travelling with it, and a record of what it actually did, produced as it happens.

A COG is a carrier-neutral container, and a program is just another carrier that arrives somewhere and acts. The declaration uses the same form MX applies everywhere else, written for an executable instead of a document. What network destinations may this program reach for payloads beyond its own updates. What categories of file may it write outside its own storage, and how large. What requires a fresh, explicit agreement from the person rather than a one-time grant at install. Signed by the developer, attested, and readable by the operating system, a security tool, or the person, so it can be checked against what the program then does rather than taken on faith.

The second half is the decision record, which is where this meets the audit problem from the companion piece. When the program crosses one of those lines and fetches a payload, that act is exactly the kind of event a decision record exists to hold: what was fetched, when, from where, under which declared policy, and whether a person was asked at the point it mattered. Written at the moment, attested so it cannot be quietly revised, and available to be read afterwards. The signature on the binary tells you the program is genuine. The declaration tells you what genuine behaviour is meant to look like. The decision record tells you what the program actually did. A platform holding all three could let a download like Chrome's happen only inside a declared, consented, recorded envelope, and flag the moment a program stepped outside it. None of that requires touching the code-signing that already does its job.

Who answers the auditor

The responsibility question answers more cleanly once the records exist, and barely at all while they do not. The person running the program is not the responsible party in any regulatory sense; for personal use they are the one with rights, not duties, and they did not choose the download. Responsibility sits with whoever decided the program would behave this way, which in the browser case is the operator that shipped both the application and the payload, and chose to push the second without asking.

But naming the responsible party and being able to hold them to account are different things, and only a record closes the gap between them. A regulator can say Google is responsible for what Chrome wrote to a user's disk. Whether that responsibility can be examined depends entirely on whether there is an attested declaration of what Chrome was permitted to fetch and an attested record of what it did. Without those, the investigation looks like the one we actually got: an independent researcher reconstructing the event from the operating system's own filesystem journal, after the fact, because the responsible party kept nothing an outsider could read. Responsibility without a record is a name with nothing attached to it.

A diagnostic question

One test, in the spirit of the others on this blog. For any program on your machine right now, could you, or a tool acting for you, produce a checkable answer to what it is permitted to download and what it has downloaded, without taking the vendor's word for it?

If yes, the program operates inside something you can audit. If no, then the signature on it told you only that it is the genuine article from a known developer, which is worth having and is not the same as knowing what the genuine article does once it is running on your device. For almost every program on almost every machine today, the answer is no, and the Chrome case is simply the instance where the consequences of that no became visible.

The bottom line

Can any program on your Mac now download binaries unrelated to what you installed it for. In practice, a trusted program running with your authority largely can, the platform's checks were built to confirm who shipped it and that it is unaltered, and a quiet later download falls outside what those checks were ever designed to see.

Do we need attestation and COGs for binaries. Not to redo the signing, which works. We need the layer above it: a declaration of what a program is allowed to do that travels with the program and can be checked, and a record of what it did that is written as it happens and cannot be silently rewritten. That is the same two-way respect the companion piece argued for, expressed as architecture instead of complaint. The first signature says the program is genuine. The web still has no second signature for what the genuine program is entitled to do in your name, and the Chrome case is the bill arriving for its absence.
