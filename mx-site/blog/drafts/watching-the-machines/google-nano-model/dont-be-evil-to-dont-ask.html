From "Don't Be Evil" to "Don't Ask"

29 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

Part of the series The Silent Install, on what a program may do to your device without asking. This post is the moan, and the fix.

Respect Runs Both Ways set out the principle. The Signature Covers Who Shipped It, Not What It Fetches set out the mechanism. This one is allowed to be a moan, because it is about a company whose code of conduct opened, for the better part of two decades, with the words "Don't be evil," and which then deleted them without telling anyone.

The promise, and how it left

"Don't be evil" was in Google's code of conduct from 2000 and turned up in the 2004 prospectus when the company went public. It was never a binding rule, only an unofficial motto, but it did real work: it told users and engineers alike that this company held itself to a line, and the line was higher than the law's floor. When Alphabet was formed in 2015, the parent took the gentler "Do the right thing" while Google held on to the original. Then, sometime in late April or early May 2018, the phrase came out of the preface of the code of conduct, leaving a single trailing mention at the very end that tells employees to speak up if they see something that isn't right.

The way it left is the part worth dwelling on. There was no announcement. The change was noticed by a reporter checking archived copies of the page against each other. The company that would, a few years later, make a practice of writing things to people's devices without telling them began by quietly removing the promise not to. The motto did not fail in public. It was edited out in private, which is its own small tell about how the place had come to treat the people it had once made the promise to.

What "evil" turned out to look like

If you were waiting for evil to arrive as something cinematic, you will have missed it, because it arrived as a default. The companion post covers the mechanics; the short version is that Chrome writes a multi-gigabyte on-device model to the user's disk with no prompt, gives the file a name that hides what it is, profiles the machine's hardware to decide whether it is a worthwhile target, and rewrites the file if the user deletes it. None of that is the work of a villain. It is the work of a product team that decided the user's device is theirs to provision, and that asking would only slow the rollout.

That is what the old motto existed to catch. Not grand wickedness, which large companies rarely commit, but the steady, banal kind that says we decided, you'll cope. Evil, in the sense a code of conduct can actually govern, is mostly a long series of small defaults set in the company's favour and against the person's, each one defensible on its own and indefensible in aggregate. "Don't be evil" was a promise to keep setting those defaults the other way. Deleting it did not change the behaviour; it just stopped pretending the behaviour was held to a line.

The fair version, because the moan should earn it

A moan that will not concede anything is just noise, so here is the strongest case for the defence. On-device processing can be the privacy-respecting choice, because a prompt answered on your machine need never travel to anyone's server. The features the model powers are useful to real people. A shared, efficient model that every page can call beats the alternative of each site shipping its own. And a browser that handles the model so the developer does not have to is, in pure engineering terms, the right division of labour. All of that is true, and none of it is the complaint.

The complaint is the missing sentence. Every defence above survives the addition of a single dialogue box that says: Chrome would like to download a model to power these features; allow, or not. The good version of this costs almost nothing to build and would have cost the rollout a few percentage points of uptake. They chose not to show it. The failure is not the AI, the file size, or the ambition. It is the decision that the person did not need to be asked, taken on behalf of billions of people who were never given the chance to disagree.

Not a slip, a posture

One incident is an incident. The reason this is worth a post rather than a shrug is that it is not one incident. The same move, a trusted channel used to push something the user did not choose, turned up in open source when WordPress.org moved millions of sites from one plugin to a renamed replacement through the auto-update feed; the operator framed it as a security measure forced by a legal fight, the maintainers called it a takeover, and a court ordered the plugin returned. It turns up again in the layer of apps and extensions now built on the silently installed model, software that assumes the model is already there because Chrome put it there. When a behaviour appears in a browser, a content platform, and a hackathon's worth of products at once, it is not a slip. It is how the industry has quietly decided your device works.

Seen that way, the motto did not fail. It was retired because it had become inconvenient to the business it was meant to govern. A promise that constrains nothing is a marketing line; a promise that constrains the plan is a cost; and the second kind is the kind that gets edited out of the preface some quiet week in spring.

The only fix that does not need anyone to be good

So I am not going to ask Google to be good. Mottos are cheap, this one proved it, and a company's conduct should not depend on whether it currently feels virtuous. The point of the other two posts is that there is a fix which does not depend on anyone's character.

First, the law that already exists should be enforced. As the earlier analysis set out, storing things on a person's device without consent already engages the ePrivacy rules, the fairness and transparency principles of data-protection law are already in play, and the AI Act's transparency duties arrive this August. None of that is new legislation waiting to be written; it is standing law waiting to be applied, and Alexander Hanff has already done the forensic and legal work of showing where it bites. That is the regulatory half, and it is his lane more than mine.

Second, and this is the half MX owns, the durable answer is attestation and provenance. Make consent a record rather than a feeling: a declaration of what a program is permitted to fetch, travelling with it, and a log of what it actually did, written as it happens and impossible to revise quietly. Then "don't be evil" stops being a slogan a company can delete in private and becomes a record an outsider can check. Virtue you can audit needs no motto. That is the whole of the case the set has been building: where you cannot trust the actor, you trust the evidence, and you make sure the evidence exists.

A diagnostic question

Here is the test that decides whether any of this matters. If a company's good behaviour depends on its stated values, you have exactly as much protection as those values command on the day someone finds them inconvenient, which 2018 showed can be none, removed without notice. If its behaviour instead depends on a record that anyone can check and that the company cannot rewrite, the values are decoration and you are covered either way. Which would you rather your browser ran on, the promise or the receipt?

The bottom line

"Don't be evil" was the easy part. Easy to say, easy to print in a prospectus, easy to delete one quiet week when it started getting in the way. The hard part was always the unglamorous one: ask first, and keep a record. It is the only part that was ever going to protect anyone, because it is the only part that still works when the company has stopped wanting to be held to the higher line.

Google removed the slogan quietly. The fix is to make the conduct loud and checkable, whether or not the company feels like being good that week. That is not a motto. It is a signature, a declaration, and a log, and unlike a motto, nobody can take it out of the preface when it becomes inconvenient.
