Text Without a Writer Needs a Name on It

3 November 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

Between 1915 and 1925, Cory Doctorow writes, Thomas Edison toured a show in which audiences listened to music behind a curtain, then watched the curtain pull back on a phonograph (Doctorow, 2026). People were fooled by a scratchy recording. Doctorow's explanation is the part I keep coming back to: "before the phonograph, every live performance had a performer."

His essay, "Separable", published in Pluralistic on 3 November, is about what happens when AI splits apart things that always used to arrive together. It's one of the clearest accounts I've read of a problem I work on every day, so I want to set out what he argues before I say where it leads.

What Doctorow Argues

The core idea is simple. If one thing has always meant another, a performance meant a performer, your brain doesn't bother learning to tell them apart. In his words, "the ability to detect a phenomenon always post-dates your encounter with that phenomenon." The first phonographs fooled people; after a few, the hiss was impossible to miss.

He applies that to AI. Before chatbots, we'd never met writing without a writer, or paintings without a painter. So we filled in the missing person: someone who chose those words and meant something by them. "No one is home inside the AI," he writes. "The intentionality we imputed to these works was the actual 'AI hallucination'." And as we've had more exposure, the effect has worn off: "The chatbots haven't gotten worse at impersonating humans, we've just gotten better at spotting the difference between a human and a bot." He treats that as healthy. Learning "to separate the formerly inseparable is the first step in our societal metabolization of these novelties."

Then he turns to mathematics. Drawing on Fenner Stanley Tanswell and Giordano Scarciotti, he argues that when mathematicians solve a hard problem, the value is often in the method, the new idea that connects the result to the rest of the field. Euler's solution to the Konigsberg bridges problem gave us graph theory, not a walking route. "Once we separate mathematics from mathematicians, we start to get solutions without insights."

He's careful not to say the tools are useless. He describes Terence Tao working with a chatbot as a centaur, a person assisted by a machine, and notes that "it's Tao who drives the research agenda." His worry is the reverse centaur, the person reduced to checking the machine's output at the machine's pace. His example is radiology: "Centaur radiology requires more radiologists, not fewer." And he reads the big announcements from AI companies as more than marketing: "The pronouncements of AI companies aren't just marketing hype, they're attempts at self-fulfilling prophecy."

That's his argument, and he makes it better than my summary does. What follows is my reading of it, not his.

The Inference We Used to Get for Free

Doctorow's towel and toothbrush example (he borrows it from Douglas Adams) is the one I'd put in front of anyone who publishes. A hitchhiker with a towel is assumed to have all the other kit as well, because everyone before them did.

A page of text used to be a towel. If it existed, someone had written it. That someone meant it, had probably checked it, and could be asked about it, or held to it. None of that was declared anywhere. It didn't need to be. Having the text implied the writer.

That's the inference AI has broken. A page can now arrive with nobody behind it, or with someone who skimmed it once and pressed publish. It reads exactly the same.

People Learn the Hiss, Machines Need a Label

Doctorow's hopeful point is that people adjust. We learned to hear the needle; many of us are already learning to spot chatbot prose.

I agree, and I don't think it's enough, for two reasons.

First, more and more of what reads your page each year isn't a person. An agent comparing suppliers, booking a table or summarising the week's news reads your page as text. It never sat through an Edison show, so it has no hiss to learn and no reason to assume a writer either way.

Second, "was this written by a person?" turns out to be the wrong question. I made this case when Anthropic described how Claude marks its output (A Watermark Answers the Wrong Question): detecting machine authorship tells you what tool was involved, at best. It doesn't tell you who stands behind the words, whether they're current, or whether anyone else agrees. A careful human can publish nonsense. A machine-drafted page can be accurate and owned by someone who checked it.

So I wouldn't try to rebuild the old inference with better detection. I'd do what Doctorow describes, and separate the parts on purpose.

Separate on Purpose

Everything the towel used to imply can be written down instead. That's most of what Machine Experience is.

  • Who stands behind it. An attestation records who made a statement, whether they still stand behind it, and whether the record is current. It doesn't prove the statement true. It does give you someone to hold to it, which is what the writer used to be.
  • How each claim is known. MX gives a claim a basis: asserted, inferred, observed, corroborated or contested. A page that says "the publisher states this" and "an outside source confirms that" lets a person or an agent tell the two apart, instead of assuming a careful writer behind both.
  • The method behind a result. Doctorow's "solutions without insights" is the same gap I found in early November 2026 in a viral AI result (The Repair That Got Retold as a Breakthrough). The number travelled. How it was reached, and what it didn't show, stayed behind. A result that carries its task, its method and its number of runs can be weighed. One that carries only the score can only be believed or not.

None of this asks whether a machine was involved. It asks who answers for the page, and what they're claiming to know.

Who's the Head

Doctorow's centaur is a human head on a machine body: the human decides, the machine carries. The reverse centaur is the person bolted to a machine that sets the pace.

Declarations decide which one you get. Hand someone an agent's confident output and nothing to check it against, and they're a reverse centaur, approving at speed. Hand them output that says where each claim came from, who stands behind the sources and how the result was reached, and they can do the job a person is for: deciding what to trust. I've argued this before in the books. Doctorow's essay is a good reminder of why it matters to the person at the desk, not just to the machine.

What I'd Do About It

If you publish anything that people or agents will act on:

  • Put a name on it. Say who published it and who stands behind it now, not just who wrote it first.
  • Date it, and say when it was last checked. A page that was right in March can be wrong in November without a word changing.
  • Say how each claim is known. Your own statement, someone else's measurement, an outside source that agrees. Link that source.
  • When you share a result, share the method. Or at least point at it. An answer without its reasoning is the gap Doctorow warns of.

He's right that we'll learn to tell the difference. The page can make that easier by saying what it is.
