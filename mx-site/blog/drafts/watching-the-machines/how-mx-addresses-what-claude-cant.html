How MX Addresses What Claude Can't

11 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

This is the sixth post in a series about what Claude actually is, rather than what its marketing implies. If you've read the earlier posts, a pattern should be visible.

Claude doesn't behave consistently across its own product family. It doesn't reliably preserve context or instructions. It can be confidently wrong. Its pricing mechanics are opaque. Its safety commitments have been revised under commercial pressure.

These aren't problems unique to Claude. They're problems with how AI tools are built and sold. And they all share a common root: the tools don't carry their own declaration of what they are, what they can do, what their limits are, or who is responsible when they fail.

That root problem is exactly what MX is built to address.

The content has no declaration

When a Claude implementation reads a piece of your content - a document, a policy, a product description - it doesn't know what that content is allowed to do. It doesn't know if the policy is current or retired. It doesn't know if the document was written by a verified author or a draft intern. It doesn't know if it can share the content externally or whether it's internal-only.

It guesses. Plausibly, fluently, and sometimes incorrectly.

MX adds the declaration the tool is missing. A COG - a machine-readable metadata record - attached to an asset states what the content is, when it was last verified, who produced it, and what a machine may do with it. The tool doesn't have to guess. It has something to read.

The COG format is published under the MIT licence, so anyone may implement it. CogNovaMX stewards MX while we run it live as a test, and it will be donated to The Gathering, an independent community body, as an open standard. No one needs a vendor's permission to use it. A COG works whether the consuming tool is Claude, another model, or something that doesn't exist yet.

Trust needs a record, not a brand

Claude's safety brand asks users to trust Anthropic's judgement about what the model will and won't do. As the previous post examined, that brand has been revised. Trust based on brand positioning is fragile because it can be revised.

MX takes a different approach. REGINALD - the registry CogNovaMX operates - provides attestation: a signed record that content is what it claims to be, produced by who it claims was produced by, and unchanged since it was attested. An agent querying REGINALD doesn't trust the content on the basis of the publisher's brand. It checks a signed record.

This is the difference between an agent that guesses at provenance and one that can verify it. The attestation doesn't make a publisher trustworthy. It makes the claim checkable - which is a different, more durable thing.

Affordances replace assumptions

The vocabulary problem between Claude implementations - the "question tool" that one Claude knows and another doesn't - is a symptom of something deeper. AI tools are built to be flexible general-purpose systems. That flexibility makes them useful. It also means they bring no fixed understanding of what they're allowed to do in your specific context.

Machine affordances in MX close that gap. They're explicit operational limits written into the content itself: declarations of whether an asset may be used to answer an external query, whether it has expired, whether it may leave the organisation. These rules travel with the content regardless of which tool reads it. The tool's vocabulary doesn't matter. The declaration is in the asset.

Accountability needs a name

Every agent operating in an MX environment has a named human owner. When an affordance fails - when the rule is wrong, or the content it applies to is outdated - there is a person responsible for correcting it. Governance without named accountability is policy with no author.

This is the principle Claude's product family currently lacks. When Claude Code behaves differently from Claude on the web, there is no named person accountable for the user experience gap. When a compaction event loses your instructions, no one owns that failure. The inconsistency is a structural property of how the products are built, and the accountability for its consequences is diffuse.

What this means for users of Claude

MX doesn't fix Claude's inconsistency, memory limits, or pricing opacity. Those are Anthropic's problems to solve.

What MX does is give you a layer of governance that doesn't depend on Anthropic solving them. Your content carries its own declaration. Your instructions travel with the asset rather than living in a context window that can be compacted away. Your accountability chain is named, not implied by brand.

The AI tools will keep changing. The products will be revised, the policies updated, the pricing restructured. MX is built on the premise that the content - not the tool - should carry what needs to persist.

The tool changes. The COG doesn't.
