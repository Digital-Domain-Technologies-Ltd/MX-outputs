The Adobe Story That Explains Why Provenance Isn't Optional

4 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

A post has been doing the rounds this week from a creative-ops leader with close to 25 years managing teams across dozens of factories, all running Adobe because Adobe is the industry standard. The story: a team member loses access to Illustrator overnight, no warning. Support says the account is past due. He is holding the payment receipt while they say it, and their own screen shows the same payment, processed that morning. Nobody disputes the money moved. Nobody can explain why the account is still locked.

The fix Adobe offered was to build an entirely new account, mirror the licenses across by hand, migrate the users, and cancel the old one later. Then the new account was built wrong: extra licenses nobody asked for, assigned to the wrong person because the original owner's email couldn't be reused, and the promised follow-up call never came. He mentions, almost in passing, that the same thing happened in 2024.

The rep isn't the problem

The post is careful about this, and it's the right instinct: the individual support agents come off as courteous and genuinely trying. The team lead escalated to sounds like he wanted to help and couldn't. That rules out the easy explanation. This isn't a training problem or a bad-attitude problem. It's that everyone on that call, including the people paid to resolve it, was working from a different partial view of the account and had no way to reconcile them.

Billing saw a cleared payment. Support saw a suspension flag. Nobody had a single record either side could point to and say "this is what happened, in order, and this is why." So the only tool available was the most drastic one: start over with a new account, and hope the mirroring holds. It didn't.

This is a provenance failure, not a support failure

Strip the specifics away and the pattern is one we spend most of our time on for a different reason: an AI regulator trying to verify a safety claim runs into exactly the same wall. Not "is the claim true" but "is there one authoritative, checkable record of what happened, that everyone with a legitimate reason to look can read and get the same answer from."

An enterprise account is not so different from a model card. It accumulates events over time: a payment, a suspension, a license grant, an account migration, a support escalation. When those events live in separate systems that don't share a timeline, nobody, not the customer, not the rep, not the team lead, can reconstruct the sequence well enough to fix it. The workaround becomes "build a new one and start clean," which is what you do when you've given up on the record and decided to overwrite it instead.

That's the same failure mode REGINALD exists to close, just wearing a different jacket. An attested, append-only account of state changes, queryable by anyone authorised to ask, isn't a nicety for AI safety disclosures. It's the missing piece in every system that has ever told a paying customer "our records show" something their own receipt contradicts.

What "attested" would have looked like here

Not a fix that requires Adobe to rebuild their billing stack. Just this: one record, per account, that every system writes to and every authorised party can read - payment received, suspension applied, suspension lifted, license granted, account created - each entry timestamped, signed, and impossible to contradict without someone noticing. The support rep pulls it up and sees the same sequence the customer sees. The team lead doesn't need to escalate to reconcile two different truths, because there's only one.

None of that requires new technology. It requires treating "what happened to this account" as a record worth making trustworthy on purpose, the same way we've started treating "what did this AI system do" as a record worth making trustworthy on purpose. The pattern generalises past AI entirely: any relationship where one party can say "our records show" and the other party is holding evidence to the contrary is a relationship missing a shared, attested source of truth.

The second time this happens to the same customer, in the same shape, two years apart, it stops being an incident. It's what a system looks like when nobody built the record that would have prevented it.
