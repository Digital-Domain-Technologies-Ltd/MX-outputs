Bridging the Machine Partnership

10 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

Linda Hill has spent her career studying why good ideas stall on the way to becoming products. In a Harvard Business Review piece written with Emily Tedards and Jason Wild, she argues that the answer often lies not in the idea itself, but in the partnership around it.

Innovation now depends on collaboration across functions and across firms. Product teams are paid to experiment. Compliance is paid to adhere. IT is paid for reliability. Executives need a business case. When startups and mature companies work together, the gaps widen. Hill's answer is a particular kind of leader she calls a bridger, someone who uses contextual and emotional intelligence to align partners who would otherwise talk past each other. Her line bears repeating: people do not take risks with those they do not trust.

The article is about humans. I want to extend it one step further, because the cast of partners has changed.

The partner nobody named

Not long ago, the participants in a digital initiative were largely human. That is shifting. Large language models help decide which sources to cite when answering a customer's question. Agents can execute purchases on behalf of buyers who may never visit your site. Crawlers shape what enters the models that follow. Assistants often mediate the first interaction a person has with your brand.

These machine participants have their own language: structured data, provenance signals, machine-readable intent. They have their own failure modes: hallucination, stale context, missing metadata, ambiguous authority. They are increasingly partners in your value chain, whether the org chart acknowledges them or not. And like any partner, they tend not to take risks with sources they cannot trust.

An LLM may hedge when provenance is absent. An agent may decline to transact when intent is ambiguous. A machine reader may pass over a source it cannot parse cleanly. The mechanism differs from human hesitation, but the principle Hill identifies still applies. Trust tends to precede participation.

Bridging across the machine boundary

Hill's bridgers do uniquely human work. I am not suggesting otherwise. What I am suggesting is that the work has a new edge. A bridger is helping a Director of Compliance and a Head of IT understand each other, and is also making sure that when their joint output reaches a machine consumer, the machine can read it, trust it, and act on it the way the humans intended. The contextual intelligence Hill describes now extends to context the machine can read.

That second half is what Machine Experience addresses. MX is the discipline of designing the signals that let machines participate as legible partners. A few of the moving parts:

  • Assets that carry their own metadata, including purpose, audience, history, authority, lifecycle, provenance, relationships, and other signals, declared in a format the human author writes once and the machine reads forever.
  • A coordination layer (we call it the Intent Data Lake) that maintains the relationships between assets so context survives when content is reused.
  • Cryptographic provenance: what we call REGINALD, the Registry for Genuine Information, Notarised Authentication, and Legitimate Documentation, so a machine consuming your content has proof, not inference, of where it came from and who stands behind it. Inference is what hallucinations are made of; proof is what trust is made of.
  • A clean separation between what humans see and what machines need. This is the Convergence Principle: interfaces designed so machines can genuinely use them tend to improve accessibility for humans as a consequence. The two audiences are not in tension. They are served by the same discipline.

None of this removes the human bridger. It gives them something to bridge with.

Why this matters

Companies likely to do well as machines take on more of the value chain are not necessarily the ones with the most aggressive AI strategy. They tend to be the ones whose content, products, and services are legible to machine partners by design, with provenance, and with humans accountable at each step.

That work calls for bridgers. It also benefits from the infrastructure that lets bridging cross the human-machine boundary without losing fidelity. Hill has named the leader. MX offers a name for the discipline.

A test for any asset

Pick one important asset on your site or in your product, a price, a policy, a product description, a piece of guidance. Ask whether a machine could answer, from the asset alone:

  • What is this?
  • Who is it for?
  • Who stands behind it?
  • When was it last changed?
  • Is it still current?
  • May it be cited?

If the asset cannot answer those questions about itself, it is not yet ready to be a partner. That is the gap MX exists to close.

If your team is thinking about how it participates in a value chain that includes machines, the MX community is where that conversation takes place.
