How Long Does It Take a Machine to Read Your Site?

20 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

When we run an audit, we measure two kinds of time. The first is network time: how long it takes an agent to fetch your pages. The second is analysis time: how long it takes to understand them once the bytes have arrived.

Network time is about your infrastructure. Analysis time is about your content.

The first question is already answered a hundred different ways by your existing monitoring. The second question has no number attached to it. Until now.

What Machine Processing Speed measures

After the audit crawler has fetched and cached your pages, we re-run the analysis with no network in the loop. Every page is read from local storage. The only clock that runs is the time it takes Pa11y to parse your DOM, Cheerio to extract your content, and our metrics engine to score what it finds.

That time — averaged across your pages — is your Machine Processing Speed (MPS).

A fast site has clean, structured markup. The parser finds what it needs quickly. A slow site has accumulated complexity: layers of JavaScript-assembled DOM, nested elements that carry no meaning, markup that was built for a browser to render rather than a machine to read.

Network hides this. Two sites can have identical Lighthouse scores and wildly different MPS figures, because Lighthouse measures what your browser renders, not what a machine can parse.

Five bands

We express MPS as a band, not a raw number. A number alone carries no meaning without a reference point. A band tells you where you stand.

Band Name Time per page What it means
A Machine-Lean Under 100ms Clean, structured markup — agents process this without effort
B Machine-Ready 100 to 300ms Standard complexity — agents handle this without friction
C Machine-Workable 300 to 800ms Moderate overhead — agents cope, but may miss context
D Machine-Heavy 800ms to 2 seconds Dense DOM or heavy JS layers — agents slow down and risk missing information
E Machine-Dense Over 2 seconds Parsing cost exceeds a practical agent budget

Most sites do not know which band they are in. Most sites have never had a reason to find out.

Why this matters now

Agents do not read your site the way a human does. They do not scroll past the noise, infer missing context from visual cues, or try again when the first parse produces something incoherent. They read what the markup gives them, once, and they move on.

A site in Band D takes twenty times as long to analyse as a site in Band A. That overhead compounds across every agent that reads you: every search crawler, every AI retrieval system, every automated buyer researching your product category. Slower analysis means higher inference cost for the agent, more chance of a partial or incorrect reading, and lower probability of appearing in an AI-generated answer.

The site owners who will benefit most from the agent economy are the ones whose content is easiest to process. MPS is the number that tells you whether you are one of them.

What changes

MPS appears in every CogNovaMX audit report alongside the existing MX Stack metrics. It is computed automatically from the timing data the audit already captures. No additional instrumentation required.

If you are in Band C, D, or E, the report tells you which pages are driving the overhead and what structural patterns are causing it. The fixes are not performance fixes — they are structural ones. Flatter DOM trees. Semantic markup. Content in the document, not assembled by JavaScript. Things that help machines and humans alike.

The band will change as you make those changes. That is the point of tracking it.
