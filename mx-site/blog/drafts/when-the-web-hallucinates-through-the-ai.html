When the Web Hallucinates Through the AI

5 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

I asked an assistant a simple thing. Get me from Rovinj to Malaga on the sixteenth of June.

A person with a browser solves this in an afternoon. Check the ferries. Check the buses. Check the flights. Stitch the legs into something that holds.

The assistant gave me a ferry that does not run on that date, a bus to the wrong airport, and a flight from an airport that bus does not serve. The timings overlapped. The whole thing read like an itinerary. None of it would get me to Spain.

This was not a hallucination in the poetic sense, but a structural failure dressed as an answer. It is the same failure that will keep happening, on bigger models, until the web itself becomes readable to machines.

The Answer That Could Not Happen

Look at what the plan actually stitched together. A ferry that was out of season. A bus that ended at one airport while the flight left from another. Connection times that ran backwards. Each fact, on its own, sounded plausible. Together they described a journey no passenger could take.

There was no lie in it. The assistant did what these systems do: predict the next plausible token, fill the gaps, and present the result with the even confidence of a system that cannot tell a true schedule from a likely-looking one.

The Second Question: Malaga to Leeds

Then I asked the harder one. Get me home. Malaga to Leeds, all the way by rail.

Here a clean answer exists, and it is not the one a model reaches for. You cannot do it in a day. Not in 2026, not with any combination of high-speed services. The reasons are physical, not matters of taste.

The exit from Spain into France is a single bottleneck. The fast trains north run a long leg, and the last departure lands in Paris late in the evening. Eurostar has stopped for the night by then. Even leaving Malaga on the first train at dawn, you reach the French connection too late to cross to London the same day. London to Leeds is two more hours on top of that, against a UK network that shuts before midnight.

So the fastest all-rail route is two days, not one:

  • Day one: Malaga north to Madrid, across to Barcelona, then the long leg to Paris, arriving late. Stay the night.
  • Day two: Paris to London in the morning, London to Leeds by early afternoon.

Notice what I just did. I gave you a timetable in prose. That is the precise move this whole post warns against: an assistant asserting schedules it cannot prove. Treat the outline as an outline, not a booking. The real times live with the operators, and today you cannot fetch them in a form a machine can trust. That gap is the whole problem.

Why the AI Failed, Even With Web Access

Models do not browse the web the way a person does. They pull fragments, infer patterns, and guess. They read text. They do not see systems.

A transport network is a system. Timetables, route constraints, airport catchments, seasonal services, last-train cutoffs, the transfer window between one platform and the next. A person reads "operates Monday, Wednesday, Friday" or "seasonal service from 20 June" and understands the consequence. A model sees the words and guesses the consequence. When it guesses wrong, it guesses with the same fluency it uses when it is right.

The web is the reason. Transport sites are heavy with script, built around tables, hidden behind forms, inconsistent from one operator to the next. The meaning a person extracts at a glance is nowhere stated. It has to be inferred. Inference is where it breaks.

Why Bigger Models Will Not Fix This

Scaling the model does not fix the web it reads.

More parameters, more training data, more access to the live web: none of it changes what the content is. If the underlying facts are implicit, visual, incomplete, and never declared, a larger model will still misread them. It will reach past the evidence, stitch incompatible facts together, and fill the silence with something that sounds right. The defect is architectural, not in the model. The architecture is the web.

The Data Already Exists. It Is Not Declared.

Now the part that matters. The facts the assistant needed are not exotic. Many of them are already described by open standards. Schema.org can express a flight, a bus trip, a journey, a schedule. Public transit has carried machine-readable timetables for years. The vocabulary to state "no service on this date" or "this leg does not connect" already exists.

What is missing is operators publishing those facts in declared, verifiable form, and a reader having any way to trust them once published. A schedule buried in a rendered table is not declared. A claim with no provenance is not trustworthy. The machine is left to scrape and hope.

This is the gap MX fills, and it fills it without reinventing what already works. MX is metadata that records a file's provenance, its context, and how it is meant to be used, and travels with the file. Where Schema.org, transit feeds, and the other standards already cover a need, MX defers to them rather than duplicating them. It makes the file say, plainly and in portable form, what it is and how to use it, so the next system to read it does not have to guess.

What MX Changes

Two pillars. MX makes content machine-readable. REGINALD, its registry, makes content machine-trustworthy. Together they turn the assistant's job from guessing-from-prose into reasoning-over-facts.

With declared, verifiable constraints, the ferry's out-of-season date is a stated fact, not an inference. The last northbound train is a published constraint, not a gamble. The airport a flight leaves from is written down, next to the bus that does or does not reach it. The assistant stops stitching plausible fragments because it no longer has to. It reads what the operator declared, checks that the declaration can be trusted, and reasons from there.

That is the difference between an agent guessing from text and an agent working from structured, verifiable, machine-declared facts. One produces confident itineraries that collapse on contact with a real platform. The other produces a journey you can take.

The Mess Was a Demonstration

Call the broken itinerary what it is: a live, unarguable demonstration of what happens when a machine is handed a web built for human eyes and asked to act on it.

The model did what models do. It predicted, inferred, approximated, and filled the gaps. The gaps were there because the web is implicit, visual, and machine-hostile. Until that changes, the best assistant available will keep producing confident nonsense, not because it is stupid, but because the page it read was never written to be understood.

If your content has to pass through an agent before it reaches a person, that is the question worth asking this week: can a machine read what you publish, and can it trust what it reads?
