How Watching the Machines Works

29 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

The editorial standard for Watching the Machines. The column names companies, products, and developments, which means it owes the reader fairness and accuracy, and it means a reader is entitled to hold it to the same bar it holds others to. This is that bar, written down so it does not drift.

What qualifies

The column is not a general AI news feed. An entry earns its place only if it turns on something Machine Experience has a particular claim to: consent over what runs on a person's device, the provenance of content and software, attestation, accountability and the evidence that supports it, accessibility, or the principle that a human stays in the loop. The working test is whether the piece is one only an MX reading could write. A model release, a benchmark result, or a funding round, on its own, is not for this column.

The evidence bar

Every factual claim cites a primary source, named, dated, and linked, so a reader can go and check it. Where the column relies on someone else's investigation, that investigator is credited and the reader is sent to the original. Quotation is kept short and sparing, and the column paraphrases by default. Where a claim is contested, it is given as contested.

Legal claims are argument, not verdict

The column will often touch law, because consent and provenance are legal questions as much as technical ones. Unless a court has actually ruled, a legal claim is presented as argument and attributed to whoever is making it, not stated as settled fact. The column is not a law firm and does not pretend to be one. Where a ruling exists, it is reported plainly, including when it cuts against the column's own view.

Fairness to the actor

A named company is given its own rationale before it is criticised. If an operator says a behaviour was a security measure, or a necessary trade-off, that case is put in its strongest form first, and the criticism then has to survive it. The column criticises behaviour, design, and decisions, never persons, and it does not invent quotes or attribute words to anyone who did not say them. When an actor corrects course, that is recorded too.

The constructive close

This is the rule that keeps the column from becoming a grievance log. Every entry closes on the answer to the problem it raised, never the grievance. Across the column as a whole, the failures are balanced by entries on what the right answer looks like when someone builds one, and by entries on the enforcement landscape, so the reader leaves with something to do rather than only something to resent.

Corrections

Mistakes are fixed in place, dated, with a short note of what changed, rather than quietly edited away. The irony of a column about silent changes making silent changes of its own is not lost on anyone here. Each entry's own update-instructions record how it is maintained.

A declared interest

CogNovaMX sells Machine Experience services, and the column argues for attestation and provenance, which is the discipline CogNovaMX practises. That interest is stated plainly so a reader can weigh it. The column's defence against the obvious objection is the evidence bar above: the claims stand on sources a reader can check, whatever the column happens to sell.

The column holds itself to the same rule

A watch that argues content should carry its own checkable evidence has to carry its own. Each entry ships with provenance in a record a machine can read, cites its sources, and is written to be read by humans and machines alike. If the column cannot meet the standard it sets for others, it has no business setting it.

Where your own content stands

The bar above is the bar the column applies to others, and the bar your own content meets the day an agent picks it up on a buyer's behalf. Evidence the reader can verify is the difference between a page a machine can act on and a page it can only summarise.
