Meet Maxine and the Joymaker

23 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Open almost any website today and you're rarely the guest. You're the thing being measured. A cookie banner, a newsletter pop-up, a carousel, and the lot again on the next click, and somewhere underneath it all the one thing you actually came to read. You do the searching, and the site does the watching.

I want to introduce what turns that around: Maxine, and the Joymaker. Between them, they let a web page rearrange itself around you, show you only what matters to you, and do it without sending your data anywhere at all.

Maxine is the partner; the Joymaker is the body

Maxine is an AI partner. Not a chatbot you interrogate and forget, but a working partner that carries your context from one day to the next. She knows what you care about, what you need, what you'd rather never see again, and she reads the web on your behalf with all of that in mind. The model underneath her can change over time; what stays constant is the partnership and the memory.

The Joymaker is what you actually open and use. If Maxine is the intelligence, the Joymaker is the body she acts through: the app on your desk and the companion on your phone. It's where all of this becomes visible, and where you talk to Maxine in plain language. Maxine thinks and decides; the Joymaker acts and shows.

A name borrowed from 1969

The name isn't ours. In 1969, the science fiction writer Frederik Pohl described a device he called the joymaker: a slim thing you carried that connected you to the intelligence around you. It knew your preferences. You talked to it and it answered. It acted on your behalf. Pohl imagined one per person, a personal companion to the world.

The smartphone got us halfway there and then stopped. It put most of our lives into our pockets, but it's still a screen you stare at, and what fills it competes for your attention rather than serving it. The Joymaker picks up where the phone left off: not one more thing demanding you look at it, but a companion that reads the world for you and hands back only the part that's yours.

Their page, and then your page

Here's what it feels like in practice. You visit a site the ordinary way and you get their page, built for everyone, shaped by whatever the owner wants you to notice.

When that page comes with a machine-readable description, Maxine reads it, matches it against what she knows about you, and builds you a fresh one from the same facts. The restaurant's menu, filtered to the dishes you can actually eat. The product, seen through your budget and your needs. The venue, with the step-free route already found. No advertisements, no banners, no dark patterns, no infinite scroll. Just what matters to you, with the option to ask follow-up questions in plain language and act on the answer.

That page is written for two readers at once: you, and the machine working on your behalf. The same description that lets Maxine lay it out for your eyes is what a machine needs to act on it, so a layout that's clearer for you is also one an agent can actually use.

When a page carries no machine-readable description, and most don't yet, the Joymaker does the next best thing. It strips the noise out and gives you a clean, calm, readable version. No personalisation, because there's nothing structured to personalise from, but no clutter either. A clean page is the gateway: once you've read the web this way, the old way starts to feel like wading.

Privacy is the architecture, not a promise

Every personalisation system you've met until now asked for the same thing in return: your data. It watches what you do, builds a profile of you on someone else's computer, and sells a version of the web shaped by their interests, not yours.

The Joymaker inverts that completely. What Maxine knows about you stays with you, on your own device. A site's machine-readable description is public, just information on a page. The matching, the part where your needs meet the site's facts, happens on your side, for your eyes only. The result is deeply personal and the amount of data that leaves your device to produce it is none.

That's worth saying plainly, because it's the opposite of how this normally works. The personalisation isn't private because we promise to behave. It's private because there's no step in which your preferences are sent anywhere to be matched. Surveillance isn't forbidden here; it's simply not possible by the way the thing is built. When Maxine does need to share something to get a job done, like booking you a table, she shares only the piece that job needs and tells you exactly what she handed over.

Two halves of one idea

Underneath the experience are two simple halves that fit together.

The first half is the world describing itself. A menu, a product, a building, a document can each carry a small, honest description of what it is, in a form a machine can read in an instant. The thing introduces itself, so nobody has to guess.

The second half is an agent that knows you. Your needs, your preferences, your context, held by you and carried into every encounter. On its own, a self-describing world is just tidy data. On its own, an agent that knows you has nothing solid beneath it. Put them together and you get the thing Pohl was reaching for: a companion that understands both you and the world well enough to stand between them and make them fit.

Where this is going

The everyday version is a phone app and a desktop companion you can use now. You point your phone at a code on a poster, a label, or a menu, and the object's description loads and gets matched to you. You talk to Maxine and she talks back. Over time the objects around you will introduce themselves without you lifting the phone at all.

None of this depends on you trusting one company with your life. The descriptions are open, the standard behind them belongs to everyone, and the part that's about you stays with you. That's the quiet promise: a web that works for you because it's built to be read, by you and by the machine you send ahead of you, with your privacy intact because nothing about you had to be given away to make it happen.

Maxine is the partner. The Joymaker is her body. Their page. Your page. The Machine Experience.
