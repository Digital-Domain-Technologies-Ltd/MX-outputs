Swap the Model, Keep the Veteran

15 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Satya Nadella wrote something this week that is worth reading slowly, because it states a problem precisely and then stops just short of the answer. He is thinking about the future of the firm in an AI economy, and he lands on a distinction that every board is about to have to make. He splits what it now builds in two. Human capital is the knowledge and judgement of its people, the relationships they hold and the patterns only they notice. Token capital is the AI capability it builds and owns.

His argument is that the two compound together, and that the human contribution gets more valuable as the machine capability grows, not less. I agree with all of it. What interests me is where he describes the test, because that is the question MX exists to answer.

The line that is Human-in-Command

Before the test, the foundation. Nadella writes:

Without human direction, you have compute running in circles.

That is the EU's Human-in-Command principle, arrived at from a completely different direction. The principle says the human holds authority over when and how a system is used, and the machine executes inside a declared boundary; it does not set direction or own outcomes. MX treats this as load-bearing rather than decorative. Every automated thing in our estate writes inside a stated scope and answers to a named person. It is reassuring to see the same conclusion reached by someone counting compute rather than reading governance papers. Direction is the scarce input. The machine is the amplifier.

The test he names

Here is the sentence that does the work:

A company should be able to switch out a 'generalist' model without losing the 'company veteran' expertise built into their learning system. This is the key 'test' of your control and sovereignty in the era ahead.

Read that again, because it is exactly right, and it is the question we get asked. A firm spends a year teaching a model how it works, what its customers want, which judgements it has learned the hard way. Then a better model ships, or the price changes, or the terms change. Can the firm move, and keep the veteran? Or has it quietly poured a year of institutional memory into an asset it rents and cannot take with it?

Nadella frames the answer as a stack of private evaluations and reinforcement-learning environments. That is real, and it is heavier engineering than most firms have. But it sits on top of something he leaves implicit, and that unanswered piece is what decides whether any of the rest is sovereign.

Where the veteran actually lives

If the company veteran lives inside the model's weights, you have already failed the test. You cannot extract it, you cannot inspect it, and you cannot carry it to the model that replaces it. The expertise has to live somewhere you own, in a form the next model can read on arrival.

That somewhere is your content. Not the model, the content: the workflows, the decisions, the domain knowledge, the accumulated judgement, written down in units that travel. This is the whole of what MX does. MX is metadata that records a file's provenance, the context that produced it, and how it is meant to be used, and it travels with the file. A unit of that knowledge is a cog: carrier-neutral, the same record whether it lands as markdown, as a row in a database, or as a comment in a PDF. It is owned by you and readable by any model, this year's or next year's.

So the veteran is not a fine-tuned checkpoint. The veteran is your cogs. Swap the generalist model and the veteran stays, because it never lived in the weights in the first place. It was always sitting in content you control, waiting to brief whatever reads it next. That is sovereignty made concrete rather than aspirational, and it is buildable today with tools you already have.

Models that eat everything they see

Nadella is blunt about the failure mode:

The last thing any of us want is a world where every company across every sector is ceding value to a few models that eat everything they see.

This is the same shift I keep describing from the publishing side. Your content is read by machines now, away from the page that produced it, and if it arrives with no provenance, no attribution, and no way to verify what it claims, it gets absorbed anonymously. The knowledge is commoditised out from under you, exactly as he says, and the value flows to whoever did the absorbing.

The counter is to make the content both readable and trustworthy. MX makes it machine-readable. REGINALD, the registry that runs on the same open standard, attests the facts so any party can verify them with their own tools. An agent that can both read your file and check its claims cites your attested fact and attributes it to you, instead of inferring something and keeping the credit. That is the mechanism by which the value stays home. It is also, not by coincidence, what makes an agent hallucinate less and cost less to run.

A frontier ecosystem, not a frontier model

The part of Nadella's essay I most wanted to see in print is the conclusion:

building a frontier ecosystem, not just a frontier model, so value flows broadly across every company, every industry, and every country.

He wants the platform ethos where, in his words,

platforms enable more value on top than is captured inside.

That is the architecture MX is deliberately built as. The metadata standard, the containers that carry it, and the public registry layer are open and governed in the community, through a standards body we call The Gathering. The proprietary registry CogNovaMX operates, REGINALD, is one implementation on that open standard, and anyone can build another. An operator can adopt the standard without adopting any one vendor. That separation is the point, not an accident of packaging, and it is the stable equilibrium Nadella is asking the industry to choose.

The honest seam

I will not pretend MX is the whole of what he describes. Token capital, as he frames it, includes the training apparatus: the private evaluations against business outcomes, the reinforcement-learning environments that improve a model on the firm's own traces. MX does not train a model and does not improve weights. What MX does is sit underneath that loop and keep it ownable. It keeps the loop's inputs and outputs portable and attributable, and it makes the model swappable. It is what keeps token capital from quietly becoming someone else's, and his test turns on exactly that.

He puts it best himself:

You can offload a task, or even a job, but you can never offload your learning.

Quite. The learning is the asset. The only question is whether you wrote it down somewhere you own, in a form the next machine can read, or left it inside a model you rent. Write it down in content that carries its own meaning and its own proof, and you can swap the model and keep the veteran. His test has an answer.
