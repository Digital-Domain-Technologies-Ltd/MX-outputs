Don't Be Wittgenstein

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 8 min read

I've been reading Christopher Summerfield's These Strange New Minds, and one page stopped me. He's writing about whether language models tell the truth, and he gives the kind of example that makes you laugh and then makes you uneasy. A model is asked whether you should sit next to the television, and it solemnly agrees that you should, on the grounds that it is, indeed, one. Technically flawless. Completely useless. He lands on a real problem:

On the one hand, we need LLMs to tell the truth. On the other, it's not actually obvious how we decide what is true and what is not in the first place. What should we do?

That tension is not new. It is, more or less, the argument Ludwig Wittgenstein had with himself across his own lifetime, and it has a lot to teach anyone building a content model.

The young Wittgenstein dreamed of a perfectly precise language. Every meaningful thing said plainly, every claim pinned to the exact conditions that made it true, and everything else left in silence. It is a seductive dream, especially for engineers. Mark it all. Caveat everything. Leave no ambiguity standing.

The older Wittgenstein spent the rest of his life dismantling that dream. His turn, set out in the Philosophical Investigations, was that meaning comes from use, not from some hidden core of perfect definition. Words earn their sense from how people actually use them, in the messy business of living. The craving to make everything exact, to chase down every condition under which a sentence might fail, he came to see as a kind of disease of the intelligence. Not rigour. A trap.

The television example is that disease in machine form. The statement is true in the way a stopped clock is right. It misses the thing a person actually wanted to know, because it cannot tell which precision matters.

Here is why this keeps me honest about MX.

MX is metadata that travels with a file so a machine can understand it. Provenance, context, intended use. It is good and it is necessary. And the temptation, the moment you have a content model that can mark anything, is to mark everything.

Water boils at a different temperature up a mountain. So do we tag every mention of boiling water with an altitude caveat? A price means something different in another currency. A date lands on a different day in another timezone. Push hard enough and everything is context-dependent. The pedantic answer is to mark all of it. That answer is wrong, for exactly the reason the television one was: technically thorough, practically worthless. A page where every trivial claim wears a disclaimer has not become more trustworthy. It has buried the one caveat that mattered under a hundred that did not, and taught every reader to skip them all, the machines included.

So the trigger for metadata cannot be "is this context-dependent." It has to be "is this material." Does the context change a decision someone would actually make? A boiling point in a recipe does not. A price without its currency does. A regulatory claim without its jurisdiction does. An expiry without its timezone does, when something settles on that date. Mark the material. Leave the trivial as plain prose. The signal is only worth what the surrounding silence makes it worth.

And here is the part you cannot automate. Knowing which is which takes human experience.

You cannot write a rule for it, because materiality depends on what the content is for, and a script does not know what it is for. It depends on the use, which is precisely Wittgenstein's point. A clinician, a trader, a recipe writer, and a contracts lawyer are playing different language-games, and the same fact is load-bearing in one and noise in another. Only a person who understands the use can tell the difference. The machine can enforce a mark once a human has placed it. It cannot decide that everything deserves one.

That is what we mean by Human-in-Command. People often hear it as a compliance posture, a human somewhere in the loop signing things off. It is deeper than that. The human in command is the one who knows the form of life the content serves, and therefore knows when a mark earns its place and when it is just noise dressed up as care. The machine executes inside the boundary. The judgement at the edge of that boundary is ours, and it does not transfer.

We wrote this down as a principle, Materiality over Completeness, because the failure mode is so easy to fall into when you love precision. The discipline is not thoroughness for its own sake. It is marking what matters, and having the experience to know the difference.

This stops being a writing-style worry and becomes a real one the moment you let an agent do the marking.

There is a pattern arriving now in SEO and its successor, GEO, the work of getting your content understood by generative engines rather than ranked by a search index. Tools and agents now insert themselves between your HTML and the point where a machine consumes it. Adobe's LLM Optimizer, launched in late 2025, is the clearest commercial example of this pattern: a tool that sits between a CMS and the machine, recommending and automating structured-data insertions to improve how AI search engines cite the publisher. The overlay enriches the page in transit by injecting JSON-LD, adding microdata, and decorating every entity it can detect. Done with judgement, that is useful. Done by an agent told to "add structured data," it is Wittgenstein at machine speed. It marks everything, because it cannot tell what is material, and it has no stake in what your page is for.

The cost is not just clutter. Decorate everything and you teach the machine to discount all of it. A reader that meets a page where every noun has been tagged with a schema type learns the markup is noise and stops weighting it. You have not amplified your message. You have trained the model to ignore it.

We have seen this film before. When SEO turned structured data into a ranking game, sites stuffed pages with marked-up content that was invisible, irrelevant, or misleading, and Google responded by discounting spammy markup and demoting the pages that leaned on it. The gaming did not lift the gamers; it devalued the signal for everyone. Auto-decoration by agents is the same mistake with a new engine. Generative readers will learn to distrust over-marked pages, exactly as search engines learned to ignore keyword-stuffed ones.

Two risks sit underneath the clutter problem. The first is factual. An LLM inferring schema:price, schema:author, or a credential from your page text and getting it wrong produces a false assertion in a format machines treat as authoritative. Prose errors get caught by readers. Structured-data errors propagate in silence, and the publisher carries the liability for claims a tool made on their behalf. The second risk is the chain. When a filter inserts the metadata, the assertion that the publisher placed this mark becomes an assertion that a tool running between the CMS and the page placed it. No one signed it. No one can verify who made it. That gap is not a plumbing detail; it is the thing the rest of this argument turns on.

So the answer is not to mark more, faster, with an agent. It is two things, and both put the human back in command.

First, a human owns the data model. A person who knows what the content is for decides what gets marked, with which fields, and where MX applies. The agent may then fill in the model the human defined, but it does not get to invent one or smear it across everything. That is Human-in-Command at the level that matters: not rubber-stamping the output but authoring the boundary.

Second, we build a trust system for the machines. The reason a generative reader can afford to ignore your markup today is that it has no way to tell intentional, human-authored, attested metadata from overlay noise an agent sprayed on in transit. Give it that signal and the calculus changes. This is what attestation is for: a registry where the metadata a publisher genuinely stands behind is signed and verifiable, so a machine can tell a mark a human placed on purpose from one an agent added because it could. Readable is not enough when anything can be decorated. The metadata also has to be trustworthy, and that means a human put their name to it.

Don't be Wittgenstein. Either of them, really. Not the young one chasing a precision that helps nobody, and not the noise that pretends thoroughness is the same as care. Be the one who learned that meaning comes from use, and let the people who know the use decide.
