The New Participant Is the Machine

17 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

You launch something and hope people will pitch in. A wiki, a forum, an open-source project, a comments section. More often than not they don't, and it's tempting to blame the crowd. Twenty years ago Tim O'Reilly looked at the same problem and drew the opposite conclusion: the systems that thrive on contribution aren't the ones with the keenest users. They're the ones built so that contributing is the easy, default move.

He called it the architecture of participation, and the idea has aged better than almost anything else written about the web in 2004.

Architecture, Not Goodwill

O'Reilly's starting puzzle was Unix. It collaborated brilliantly for decades under a proprietary licence, while other projects with spotless open-source licences went nowhere. If the licence were the thing that drove collaboration, that should be impossible. His answer was that the licence grants permission to contribute and nothing more. What actually produces contribution is the architecture: small tools, pipes, standard interfaces, so you could add your part without negotiating with everyone else first.

He borrowed Mitch Kapor's line, "architecture is politics," by way of Larry Lessig. How you build a thing decides what behaviour it invites and what it quietly forecloses. The web itself was the proof. Open protocols, a low bar to adding a page, no central permission desk, and value that grew with every new participant.

The sharpest part came at the end, where he cited Dan Bricklin's "cornucopia of the commons." There are three ways to fill a large shared resource: compel people, pay them, or build a system where they pitch in as a by-product of using it for their own selfish reasons. Bricklin's example was Napster, where downloading a track also, by default, shared it. The default setting was participation. A year later that same insight became the headline of O'Reilly's "What Is Web 2.0?": collective intelligence, with users adding value just by showing up.

There Is a New Participant Now

Here is what has changed since 2004. The participant O'Reilly was designing for was a person. Today, the participant arriving at your content often isn't.

It's an agent acting on someone's behalf. A model summarising your page for a question its user asked. A scraper building an index. A validator checking a claim. It visits once, reads what it can, and leaves. It never sees your visual design, never follows your carefully built path from landing page to pricing page. It takes one read and acts on it.

And most content gives that participant nothing it can use. It can see the pixels and the raw markup, but the meaning, who published this, when, on what authority, how it may be reused, never makes it into anything the machine can read. So it does what any reader does when the text is unclear. It guesses. Multiply that by every agent that meets your page, and you've built a system the new participant can't join.

An Architecture of Participation for the Machine

This is the gap MX is built to close, and the lesson is exactly O'Reilly's. You don't get the machine to participate by hoping it interprets you generously, any more than you get people to contribute by appealing to their goodwill. You get it by architecture.

So the metadata that says what a file is, where it came from, and how it may be used travels with the file, in the file's own format, readable in a single fetch. Contribution to the shared record, the provenance of who did what and the attestation of who stands behind a claim, is captured as a by-product of normal work rather than as a separate chore someone has to remember. That's Bricklin's third way, pointed at the machine: the default setting is participation.

The pleasing part is that none of it costs the human reader anything. The page still looks the way it looked. A well-built MX page is also a well-built page for people, for search, and for a screen reader, because all of them want the same thing the agent wants: explicit structure instead of inference. The visible artefact is for eyes. The metadata behind it is for the machine. Both say the same thing.

Twenty years ago the move that mattered was designing so people would join. The move that matters now is designing so the machine can. It's reading your content already. The only question is whether you've given it something to read, or left it to guess.

The concept here is Tim O'Reilly's "The Architecture of Participation" (2004). The extension to the machine as a participant is MX's.
