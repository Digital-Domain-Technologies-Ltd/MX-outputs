Google Just Added an Agentic Browsing Score to PageSpeed Insights

2 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

I saw this in a screenshot doing the rounds on LinkedIn this week, so I went and checked it myself. Open PageSpeed Insights on a live URL and, alongside the familiar Performance, Accessibility, Best Practices and SEO scores, there's now a fifth: Agentic Browsing.

The description Google ships with it's blunt about where it stands: "These checks ensure high-quality, browsable websites for AI agents and validate the correctness of WebMCP integrations. This category is still under development and subject to change." The screenshot I saw showed a site scoring 2 out of 3 on it.

That's a small line of text doing a lot of work.

Why this is not just another score

Performance told you whether a page loads fast for a human. Accessibility told you whether a screen reader can use it. Agentic Browsing asks a new question of that same page: can an AI agent browse it, understand it, and act on it? Google building that question into its own flagship audit tool, rather than leaving it to third-party checkers, is the clearest signal yet that "readable by an agent" is becoming a first-class property of a well-built page, on the same footing as "fast" and "accessible".

That's exactly the question MX exists to answer. MX is to machines what UX is to users: MX asks whether a machine can understand and act on a page, the way UX asks the same of a human. Seeing that question turn up inside PageSpeed Insights, unprompted, from Google, is the kind of external corroboration that's worth more than any amount of us saying it ourselves.

What WebMCP actually is

The other half of the description names WebMCP specifically, and it's worth being precise about what that is. WebMCP is a proposal, incubated at W3C with Microsoft's Edge team leading it, that lets a web page act as its own MCP server, exposing a set of tools an agent can call from right there, under the user's existing authenticated session and the browser's existing security model. It's the missing piece in the story I've told before on this blog, the assistant that can read your prescription renewal form perfectly and still can't act on it, because the session belongs to the browser and nobody handed the agent a way to press the button.

WebMCP is the action layer. It answers "what can an agent do here, and how does it ask permission." MX sits one layer over from that. MX answers "what is this, where did it come from, and how should it be interpreted." A page can have a flawless WebMCP integration and still be unreadable if its content carries no provenance, no context, and no signal of who stands behind it. The two are complementary, not competing: WebMCP without MX gives an agent a lever to pull with no idea what it's attached to; MX without WebMCP gives it a trustworthy description of a page it still can't act on.

Early, unstable, and worth tracking anyway

I want to be straight about the caveat in Google's own text: this category is still under development and subject to change. A 2 out of 3 score today tells you almost nothing about what a 3 out of 3 will require in six months. Whatever checks survive to general availability will most likely become the de facto benchmark for agent-readiness, the same way Core Web Vitals quietly became one for performance. Nobody voted for that outcome either time. It just happened because the tool was already sitting on every developer's desk.

That's worth building towards now, not after the rubric settles. If your site isn't yet thinking about what an agent sees when it browses your pages, this is as good a prompt as you'll get to start.
