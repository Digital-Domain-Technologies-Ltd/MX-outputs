Verify and Reuse Are Machine Verbs

25 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

There is a good piece of AEO advice doing the rounds, and the best version of it I have seen this week comes from Avidly: Why AEO won't fix your average content. The argument is sound and I agree with almost all of it. Stop chasing volume. A tool that surfaces your visibility problem does not solve it. Brands that win in answer engines are not the ones publishing the most; they are the ones making their expertise easy to understand, verify, and reuse. Google says much the same thing in its own guide to optimising for generative AI features: create valuable, non-commodity content for your audience, and the rest follows.

I want to take that advice seriously enough to find the one place it stops. It is hidden in three small words: understand, verify, reuse. Those are presented as a single instruction to the writer, three flavours of the same "write well" command. They are not. One of them is a human verb. The other two are things a machine does to a file, and you cannot deliver them with prose alone.

Understand is the writer's job

Start with the one the advice gets completely right. Understand is a human verb, and it is also the one a language model is best at. If your page clearly states what you know, who you help, and why your view is worth holding, a model reading it will understand. This is the part where "write better content" is the whole answer. Clear claims, plain sentences, expertise stated up front rather than buried three paragraphs down. No file format helps here and none is needed. The Avidly post is right that this is where most content fails, and right that no AEO checklist rescues a page that has nothing to say.

So if the advice only had to cover understanding, it would be complete. It does not stop there, and the next two words are where it quietly changes subject.

Verify is something done to a claim, not a page

Watch what happens when a model moves from understanding your content to acting on it. A buyer asks it to compare options and recommend one. Now the model is not reading your page; it is weighing your claim against everyone else's and deciding which to stand behind. To do that it wants to verify, and verification is not a property of how well you wrote the sentence. It is a property of what the sentence can be checked against.

Two questions sit under "verify", and a model asks both. Who published this, and when? And does anyone authoritative independently agree? Neither is answered by clear prose. The first is attestation: a machine-checkable record of authorship and date that travels with the claim. The second is corroboration, and it is the one writers skip because it is the most work. Google's own quality framework draws the line precisely: in the Search Quality Rater Guidelines, authoritativeness is external recognition, other credible sources citing you, never anything you can assert about yourself. A confident, beautifully written sentence with nothing behind it is exactly the kind of claim a model discards at the decision turn. Writing it more clearly does not make it more verifiable. Linking it to someone authoritative who says the same thing does.

So "make your expertise easy to verify" is real advice, but it is not writing advice. It is an instruction to anchor your claims and to carry a record of who made them. The clearest prose in the world fails it if the claims float free.

Reuse is something done to a file after the page is gone

The third verb travels the furthest from the keyboard. When a model reuses your content, your page is usually nowhere nearby. The text has been lifted into a training corpus, a retrieval index, or an agent's working memory, and what came with it is whatever the file carried on its own. The byline is gone. The footer is gone. The careful "according to our research" framing you wrote is gone. If who-said-it and under-what-terms were facts about the page rather than facts about the file, they did not survive the trip.

This is the gap a search guide structurally cannot close, because it concerns the content after it has left the page entirely. Reuse without provenance is just text. An agent that reuses a paragraph it cannot attribute will either drop the attribution, which harms you, or decline to use it at decision time, which also harms you. Either way the expertise you wrote so clearly does not get the credit, because the thing that carries it was never attached to the words.

Making content easy to reuse, then, means making the provenance ride inside the file: the publisher, the date, the version, the terms of use, the licence. That is metadata that travels, and it is a structural choice made once, not a quality of the writing. This is the part MX puts in the file and Reginald, currently in beta, makes verifiable. This post marks where the line between writing and infrastructure falls.

The advice is right. It is also half the job

Lay the three verbs back down next to each other and the division is clear. Understand is craft: write expert content, clearly, and a machine reads it. Verify and reuse are infrastructure: carry the provenance and the corroboration so the claim survives being checked and survives being lifted off the page. The AEO advice treats all three as one instruction to the writer because, from where they sit, they feel like one job. From where the machine sits, they are two different jobs that happen to share a sentence.

This is why I think the "stop chasing volume, write better content" message is correct and incomplete at the same time. It is the right answer to the question it is asking, which is "why does my average content fail in AI search". The answer is: because it is average, and AEO tooling will not fix that. True. But the next question, the one a brand asks after it has finally written something genuinely expert, is "why does the model still not stand behind me when it matters". And the answer to that one is not "write even better". It is: because the model could not verify the claim and could not safely reuse the page, and those were never things your prose was going to deliver.

There is an old line that the post I started from lands on, and it is worth keeping: focusing on what people want to know is still the answer, even after all these decades. I would add one clause for the readers who are not people. Focusing on what people want to know gets you understood. Carrying what a machine needs to check and to credit gets you used. Write the expert content. Then make sure it can be verified and reused by something that arrived without your page, because that something is now the reader you were not writing for.
