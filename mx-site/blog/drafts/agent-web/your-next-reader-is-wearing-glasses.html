Your Next Reader Is Wearing Glasses

25 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

On 23 June 2026, Meta put a camera and an AI model on a pair of $299 glasses and dropped the Ray-Ban name off the front. The cheap price is the headline. The thing worth your attention is quieter: the new glasses run Muse Spark, Meta's own model, and the whole pitch is that you look at something, say "Hey Meta, what am I looking at," and the model answers from what the camera sees.

I have been writing in this series about the machine that visits your website once, reads what it can, and leaves. That machine just got a face. It is a person, in a shop, looking at your product on a shelf, asking a model to tell them about it. And the model is not reading your page. It is reading the world, and then, if it needs more, it is fetching whatever it can find about you and reading that.

That is the shift. Machine-readability used to be a thing that happened in a data center, on a crawl, weeks before anyone bought anything. Now it can happen at the moment of decision, with your customer standing in front of the product.

What the glasses can read, and what they can't

A model behind a camera sees roughly what every other machine visitor sees, which is to say: not your design.

It sees pixels and it hears speech. It does not see your brand color, your hero image, your careful layout. When it wants to know more than the camera shows, it reaches for whatever is machine-readable about you: your structured data, your plain text, the metadata that travels with your content. If your price lives only inside a product photo, the glasses can read it off the shelf tag but not off your site. If your specifications live only in a styled table that looks right but carries no structure, the model gets a wall of pixels where you meant to put facts.

Here is the part that should land. The customer is not going to scroll your site through the glasses. There is no journey. There is one question, asked out loud, answered in one breath, from whatever the model can read in that instant. Every page you own has to stand on its own for that moment, because the moment is all you get.

This is the audit we already run

None of this is a new problem. It is the same problem this series keeps circling, arriving through a new door.

When we audit a site, we read each page the way a machine reads it: the structure, the accessibility tree, the metadata, the plain-text projection. The defects that surface are the ones that fail a model behind a camera too. Data locked inside images. Controls that are styled boxes with no meaning underneath. Facts that only exist in the visual layout and vanish the moment you strip the layout away. A page can look finished and still carry none of its meaning in a channel the glasses can read.

The fix has not changed either. You carry the same meaning in more than one channel, so that whichever channel a visitor can read, the meaning survives. Do that and you serve the screen reader, the crawler, the chatbot, and now the model on someone's face, all at once, with one piece of work.

The point of decision moved

The glasses are not the story. The story is where the machine now stands. It used to read you in the background. Now it can read you while your customer is deciding whether to buy, with the product in their hands and a model in their ear.

If you want to know what that model can actually learn about you in that moment, the honest way to find out is to read your own pages the way it does. That is what an MX audit is for. The machine that visits once is now looking through your customer's eyes, and it still only visits once.
