The Language You Ask In Is Not Neutral

23 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Ask for a legal letter about a house purchase in English and you get common law. Solicitors handle the transaction, the Land Registry holds the title, Stamp Duty Land Tax falls on the buyer, and the parties exchange contracts before a completion date. Ask for exactly the same letter in Spanish and you get civil law. A notario - a state-appointed public official - must sign the escritura pública. The buyer pays Impuesto de Transmisiones Patrimoniales. The seller pays plusvalía municipal, a tax on the land value increment that has no equivalent in English law. Specific Spanish legislation is cited by article and decree number, as is standard in civil law drafting.

Neither letter mentions the other's taxes. Neither describes the other's professional roles. A translation of either into the other language would not produce the other letter - it would produce a letter that is legally incorrect in the target jurisdiction.

The prompt language was the only variable. It changed everything except the date.

How training data becomes cultural memory

The reason is structural. Common Crawl, the open archive that most large language models train on, sits at roughly 41% English. No other single language exceeds 6%. English-language legal content describes common law systems: Land Registry, freehold, solicitors, SDLT. Spanish-language legal content describes civil law systems: Registro de la Propiedad, notario, escritura pública, ITP, plusvalía.

When a model generates text in a given language, it draws preferentially from training data in that language. That data carries a legal, institutional, and cultural world with it. The prompt language is not merely a translation parameter. It is a jurisdiction selector. It activates a different world model.

The same mechanism explains why a German juice bottle photographed through a translation app reads "Orange with Pump" rather than "Orange with Pulp." The German word "Fruchtfleisch" - literally fruit flesh, meaning pulp - maps to a statistical neighbourhood where "pump" and "pulp" sit close together, and the cultural grounding that would immediately rule out a pump in a juice context is absent. The model filled a grounding gap with the most probable English approximation. It was wrong by one letter and one world.

This is not a flaw in language models. It is how language works. Languages are not neutral transport layers for abstract meaning. They are carriers of context, embedded assumption, and institutional knowledge accumulated over centuries of use.

Register as world model

In linguistics, a register is the variety of language used for a particular purpose or in a particular context - legal, medical, technical, conversational, formal. Registers differ not just in vocabulary but in how obligations are stated, how authority is performed, and which data files are treated as foundational.

LLMs have registers too. Without explicit instruction, instruction-tuned models converge on a default: measured, clear, slightly formal, hedged where appropriate, moderately enthusiastic. That default is baked in by fine-tuning on human preference data, which rewards a particular tone regardless of the domain. It sounds helpful and professional but belongs to no specific discipline. Practitioners recognise it immediately.

Spanish makes the register decision visible in a way English does not. The T-V distinction - tú versus usted, vosotros versus ustedes in Castilian - encodes formality into grammar itself. A model producing Spanish must choose a register on every second-person sentence. When it defaults to Latin American neutral usted for a Castilian audience, the register mismatch is grammatically audible. The same invisible choice happens in English; it just leaves no grammatical fingerprint.

The same divide appears within a single language. American English and British English share vocabulary and grammar but carry different register conventions for professional contexts. American business register defaults to direct, action-oriented prose: short sentences, imperative recommendations, low tolerance for circumspection. British professional register performs confidence through understatement: "It might be worth considering..." is not diffidence - it is authority expressed in a different key. A model generating professional content for a British audience without explicit instruction defaults to American conventions, because American English accounts for the larger share of English training data. The output will be grammatically correct and register-wrong in ways that are invisible to anyone who only knows one convention.

The developer case

The effect is not limited to legal writing. A coding prompt in English draws on training data that skews toward US conventions: MM/DD/YYYY date formatting, American variable naming patterns, libraries dominant in English-language developer communities, and a problem-solving idiom shaped by the communities that write predominantly in English. A coding prompt in German draws on different conventions - more verbose, explicitly commented code, different library preferences, and a culture of formal precision in technical documentation.

A developer building systems that operate across locales may write prompts in English and deploy code that silently carries American date handling, US timezone defaults, and ASCII assumptions about text encoding. None of those choices appear in the prompt. All of them are inherited from the cultural ground the prompt language activates. A function generated from an English prompt and deployed in a German product will format 6 January 2026 as 01/06/2026 - which a German user reads as 1 June, not 6 January. The error appears in no log file.

Declare, do not infer

The fix is declaration. If a data file carries machine-readable metadata that states its language, locale, jurisdiction, and the specific meanings of domain-specific terms, the model has no need to infer those facts through an English-weighted lens. The declaration travels with the content and survives every system that handles it.

Three posts on this site trace the same problem from different angles. "Orange with Pump" shows how "Fruchtfleisch" - standard German for juice pulp - became "pump" through a one-letter gap in cultural grounding. "The Crawl Still Speaks English" covers the 41%/6% training data split and the English-frame reasoning loop in detail. "Strip the Marks, Lose the Word" shows what happens when diacritics are stripped before a model reads the text - in Vietnamese, a single tone mark separates mother from ghost.

The prompt language is not a neutral choice. It is already a decision about which world the machine draws on. Making that decision explicit - in metadata the machine reads before it starts reasoning - is the point of Machine Experience.

MX addresses this through two instruments. COGs (Community Owned Governance Standards) are data files that carry their own reasoning instructions: a COG-equipped legal letter declares its jurisdiction, its legal system, and the meanings of its domain-specific terms in machine-readable frontmatter. REGINALD, the provenance registry, signs that declaration so any machine reading the data file can verify it has not been altered. Together they mean a Spanish property transaction data file does not rely on its language to signal which legal system it inhabits - it states the system explicitly, and any machine that reads it knows before it starts reasoning. That is the gap the jurisdiction selector effect exposes: the machine had to guess because the data file had not declared. Declaration closes the gap.
