The Joymaker Walks Into a Museum

25 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

In Frederik Pohl's The Age of the Pussyfoot, written in 1965, a man wakes up centuries in the future and is handed a joymaker: a small personal device that answers any question, books his travel, watches his health, and connects him to everything the world knows. Pohl saw the smartphone, and the personal assistant, and the always-on agent, decades early. He also saw the catch, and built the plot around it. The joymaker is only ever as good as the information it can reach. When it cannot reach a true answer, it gives a confident one, and the confident wrong answer is the dangerous one.

Sixty years later the joymaker costs $299 and sits on your face. Meta's new glasses look at what you look at and answer out loud. Which brings me to a museum.

The Invited Machine

Everything I have written in this series so far has been about the machine that arrives uninvited. It lands on your page, reads what it can, and leaves, and your job is to not lose your meaning on the way. Defensive work. Important, but defensive.

A QR code on a museum exhibit label is the opposite posture. The museum is holding the door open. It is saying, out loud and on purpose, read me, and here is exactly where. The visitor in glasses looks at the code, and the code points the agent at a page built to be read by a machine. This is the invited machine, and it changes everything about how you publish, because now you are not defending your meaning. You are offering it.

So the question stops being "will the machine understand my page despite me" and becomes "what do I want the machine to tell my visitor." That is a much better question, and it is the one a museum is uniquely ready to answer.

What the Codified Page Carries

The glasses do not want your marketing copy. Standing in front of a fifth-century BC vase, the visitor asks one question, and the agent has one breath to answer it well.

A codified page gives the agent what it actually needs:

  • Provenance. Where the object came from, how it was acquired, the accession record, the chain of custody. Provenance is a word museums have used for two hundred years. They will not need it explained.
  • Context. The period, the culture, the technique, why this object matters and what it sat next to in its own world.
  • Intended use. How to narrate this to a person standing in a gallery, in a sentence, not a catalogue entry.

With those in hand the agent stops inferring and starts reading. The narration the visitor hears is the museum's own account of the object, not the model's best reconstruction of it.

Why Attestation Is Not Optional Here

Here is the part that makes this the strongest case MX has.

When the glasses say "this is from 480 BC, excavated near Athens, acquired in 1887," the visitor cannot check. They are standing in a gallery with a device in their ear, and a confident hallucination sounds exactly like a fact. Pohl's catch, arriving on schedule.

Attestation is the answer. The museum signs the claim. The page does not just state "480 BC", it carries a record of who stood behind that date and when. The glasses narrate an attested fact, and a curious visitor, another institution, or a researcher can later verify who made the claim and trace it home. In a place whose entire authority rests on being right about the past, the difference between an attested fact and a confident guess is not a nicety. It is the whole job.

This is what makes cultural heritage the natural home for the trust layer. Museums already do attestation by hand: the label, the catalogue, the curator's signature on the acquisition file. We are not introducing a foreign idea. We are giving an old and careful practice a form a machine can read.

Three Things You Get for Free

Build the page properly once, and the rest follows without extra work.

A visitor who cannot see gets the entire exhibit narrated to them, accurately, because the page was built for a machine to read and a machine is what reads to them. The same structure that serves the glasses serves the screen reader. Accessibility stops being a separate project and becomes a property of having done this right.

The glasses translate as they narrate. The museum codifies the label once, and every visitor hears it in their own language. No more rows of translated panels to print and maintain. One source, many tongues.

And the load-bearing claims point at the catalogue and the scholarly record, so the story the visitor hears is not only attested but corroborated. Attested means we know who said it. Corroborated means someone authoritative agrees. A museum can offer both, and few other publishers can.

The Door Was Always Going to Open

The glasses are not the story. The story is that the personal agent Pohl imagined has arrived, it answers out loud, and it is only as good as what it can read. A museum is the place that has the most worth telling and the longest practice of telling it carefully.

Put a QR code on the label. Point it at a codified page. Sign the facts. The joymaker walks in, looks at the vase, and tells your visitor the true story, in their language, for as long as the museum stands behind it. That is what this was always for.
