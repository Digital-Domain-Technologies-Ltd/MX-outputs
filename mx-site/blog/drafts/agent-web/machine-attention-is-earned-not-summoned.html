Machine Attention Is Earned, Not Summoned

14 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 8 min read

There is a lot of superstition around GEO. Generative engine optimization has collected a folklore: a set of rituals people perform in the belief that an AI will notice them. Drop an llms.txt at the root. Stack the page with schema. Chop the content into bespoke chunks. Write a second, machine-flavoured version of every article. Manufacture a few brand mentions to nudge the answer engines. The rituals share a premise, and the premise is wrong: that machine attention is something you summon with the right incantation rather than something you earn by writing well.

This post is the practical version of an argument made elsewhere on this blog. Here I want the craft: what you actually do, at the keyboard, to write a blog post a machine will use.

The rituals that do nothing

The useful thing about superstition is that it can be tested, and this lot has been. On 5 June 2026 Google published an official guide to optimizing for generative AI features, and in the same document told site owners that most of the GEO ritual does nothing for its generative results. You do not need llms.txt. You do not need special schema for AI. You do not need bespoke AI chunking. You do not write a separate machine version of your page. Manufacturing mentions is a thing to avoid, not a thing that works. From Google's seat, writing for generative AI search is still writing for search.

That is the vendor everyone is optimizing for, publishing the list of moves that have no effect on it. It is worth taking seriously, because it is the closest thing to a primary source the field has. The rituals were never the hard part. They were the part you could buy.

The failure the rituals hide

Debunking the folklore leaves the real question standing: a machine has read your post, so why does it ignore you when it matters?

The sharpest answer I have seen from outside our own work comes from AIVO Journal, which ran structured audits across consumer and financial categories and reported a result it calls the Decision Gap (Stop producing content for LLMs). The models knew the brands well. Detailed, accurate, retrievable knowledge. But at the moment a buyer asked the model to compare, recommend, and commit, the audits found mean fact deployment dropped to roughly a quarter. The model discarded most of what it knew and fell back to a category default. The brand it knew thoroughly became the brand it could not decide on.

The mechanism AIVO describes is the part a writer needs to hold. The decision turn is a compression pass. The model is not re-reading everything it holds about you; it is summarising across candidates and constraints and defaulting to whichever option most cleanly resolves the query. Volume without evidence structure adds noise to that compression. It does not add signal. And here is the uncomfortable conclusion for anyone whose plan is to publish more: pumping out unanchored content widens the very gap it is meant to close.

So the superstition is worse than useless. The rituals do nothing, and the volume strategy underneath them actively hurts.

What actually earns machine attention

Strip out the folklore and three things are left. None of them is a trick. All of them are writing.

Write one artefact for every reader. The single most common ritual is the machine-flavoured fork: the human page, and a stripped, chunked, marked-up shadow version for the bots. Do not build it. Google's own guidance is that the same page serves both, and it is right for a deeper reason than Google gives. A page an agent can read accurately is a page a screen reader handles cleanly and a new colleague understands without a handover. Building for the machine and building for the human is the same job done once. When you find yourself writing a second version for the machine, stop: the machine wants the clean version of the first one.

Make the structure carry the meaning. The compression pass keeps what resolves the query cleanly. So write so that a single pass cannot lose your point. State the claim in the heading, not three paragraphs below it. Put the fact in a plain declarative sentence, not buried in a clause hedged on both sides. Keep the relationship between intent and outcome on the surface, because the model discards what it has to reconstruct. This is not dumbing down. It is writing that survives being summarised, which is the only form of writing a decision-stage model ever reads.

Carry your provenance. When your post is lifted out of the page, into a training corpus, a retrieval index, or an agent's working memory, the page is gone and the byline with it. What travels is whatever the file carries on its own: who published it, when, under what version, and whether it has been altered. That carried layer is what makes a claim something an agent can cite rather than infer, and it is what a search guide structurally cannot give you, because it concerns the file after it has left the page. This is the layer MX puts in the file and Reginald, currently in beta, makes verifiable. The argument in full is in Schema.org keeps growing, and the provenance layer still does not exist.

That "makes it verifiable" half is really four separate questions a machine can check. Attestation: who published this, and when. Currency: is this the version that still stands, the newer one superseding it while the old one stays queryable. Freshness: is the publisher still behind it - a liveness signal that holds while they keep their registry account current and freezes if they go quiet, so an abandoned post still resolves but reads as unmaintained. And corroboration: does an authoritative outside source independently agree - which is the writer's job, not the registry's, and the one I give its own section below.

Corroborate, do not assert

There is a fourth thing, and it is the one writers skip, because it is the most work and cannot be automated. Anchor every load-bearing claim to an authoritative source that independently states the same thing.

A signature proves you said it. It does not prove anyone authoritative agrees with you, and at the decision turn that distinction is decisive. Google's own quality framework draws the line precisely: in the Search Quality Rater Guidelines, authoritativeness is defined as external recognition, other credible sources citing and referencing you, not anything you can assert about yourself (E-E-A-T). The grounding research points the same way: a model that conditions on retrieved authoritative sources hallucinates measurably less than one drawing on its own parametric memory (Lewis and colleagues, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, 2020; Retrieval Augmentation Reduces Hallucination in Conversation, 2021). A claim a model can ground in a weighted external source survives the decision turn. A claim it cannot ground defaults to the prior.

The practical instruction follows directly, and it is unforgiving: do not restate an authority in your own words and call it support. Link to the actual statement. If you find yourself writing a confident sentence with nothing behind it, that sentence is unanchored content, and unanchored content is exactly the noise the Decision Gap punishes. The honesty test is simple. For each claim that matters, can a reader, human or machine, follow a link to someone authoritative saying the same thing? If not, either find the source or drop the claim.

This post is written to its own rule. Every load-bearing claim in it points to a primary source: Google's guide, Google's rater guidelines, AIVO's audit, the grounding papers. That is not decoration. It is the demonstration. A post arguing that corroboration earns machine attention that was itself uncorroborated would refute itself on contact.

A short checklist

Before you publish, read your draft the way a compression pass would.

  • One artefact, no machine-flavoured fork.
  • The claim is in the heading and the topic sentence, not hidden below them.
  • Each load-bearing claim links to an authoritative source that says the same thing, in its words, not yours.
  • The file carries its own provenance, so the claim survives being lifted off the page.
  • You are publishing because you have something anchored to say, not to add to a pile.

None of that is a ritual. It is the unglamorous floor Google left standing, plus the provenance and corroboration that a search guide was never going to cover. There is no file to drop, no markup to stack, no pipeline to license. There is only the work, done properly.

Earned, not summoned

The folklore promises that machine attention can be conjured by people who know the right moves. The evidence says the opposite. The rituals do nothing, the volume strategy backfires, and what is left is the oldest advice there is: write something true, make it legible, stand behind it with your name, and back it with sources better than your own opinion. SEO got you found. GEO gets you understood. Being used, read and acted on by a machine that has arrived and now has to decide, is earned by content that was built to be read, not summoned by content built to be noticed.

The standard underneath all this is debated in the open at tg.community.
