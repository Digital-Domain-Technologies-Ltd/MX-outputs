x401 Proves the Agent. MX Proves the Page.

26 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

An AI agent lands on your site to do something on someone's behalf. Book a room, file a claim, sign a form, pull a quote into an answer. Before you let it act, you want two things settled.

Two questions decide it. Who this agent is and what it's allowed to do. And whether the thing it just read is genuine.

One question is about the visitor. The other is about your page. Until recently the web had no clean answer to either one for agents. Now it has the start of an answer to the first, and that's worth understanding, because it tells you exactly which half is still missing.

x402 Pays, x401 Proves

You may have seen x402 go by. It borrows the old HTTP "402 Payment Required" status code, dusts it off, and turns it into a way for an agent to pay for something inline, mid-request, without a human clicking through a checkout. It gave the agent web a payment layer.

x401 is the sibling. The name nods to HTTP "401 Unauthorized", and the job is identity. Launched by Proof with identity teams at Google, OpenAI, Okta, Circle, MATTR and Lightspark, and handed to the FIDO Alliance's agentic-authentication group, it lets a site ask an agent to prove who it represents and what it may do. The agent presents a credential, the site checks the issuer, the claim, the scope, and the action, and only then proceeds.

Sensibly, x401 doesn't invent its own credential format. It builds on machinery the identity world already trusts: OpenID for Verifiable Presentations, the W3C Digital Credentials API, W3C Credential Management. Verifiable Credentials and passkeys travel inside it. That deference matters, and we'll come back to it.

So x402 answers how an agent pays. x401 answers who an agent is and what it's permitted to do. Both are about the actor at the moment it knocks on your door.

Neither one says a word about your page.

Who Proves the Page?

Here is the gap. An agent can now prove it acts for a real, authorised person. It still has no way to know that the page it just read is the genuine thing the publisher stands behind, rather than a stale copy, a spoof, or a fabrication an earlier model hallucinated and a later one is now quoting as fact.

That's the artefact, not the actor. And it's the half MX and Reginald exist to cover.

The two-pillar way we say it: MX makes content machine-readable, so an agent can act on a page without guessing what it means. Reginald makes content machine-trustworthy, so the agent can check who published a claim, whether it's the current version, and that the publisher still stands behind it. Readable, then trustworthy. The actor proves itself with x401; the page proves itself with MX and Reginald.

This is also the documentation regimes like the EU AI Act and the European Accessibility Act expect organisations to be able to produce on request: a structured, verifiable record of what was published and what an agent did with it.

MX and Reginald don't grant compliance with any of those. Compliance stays a legal duty of the organisation. What they do is make the evidence machine-readable and checkable, so producing it later is a lookup, not an archaeology dig.

They Already Sign the Same Way

This is the part that surprised me when I lined them up.

x401 and Reginald were built by separate teams, each chasing its own problem, and they reached for the same crypto. Reginald signs a cog's identity and body with Ed25519 over a content hash, and a verifier checks the signature on the spot, from a small sidecar that carries the public key, without calling any registry. x401's world verifies a credential client-side too, issuer-neutral, no central gatekeeper.

Same primitive. Same posture. The only honest difference is what they point at: x401 signs a claim about an agent, Reginald signs one about a file.

Because they verify the same way, a single piece of software walking up to a page could check both at once. Is the agent who it claims to be? Is the page what it claims to be? One mental model, one verification routine, two answers.

Where They Snap Together

Once you see the shared crypto underneath, the integration points almost draw themselves.

The first is an identity socket. Reginald's provenance record already has a slot for the identifier of each party that touched an artefact. Today that slot is filled by hand, a name typed into a field. An x401 presentation fills it with a credential the agent actually proved. The record stops saying "the system claims this agent acted" and starts saying "this one proved who it was, and here's the receipt."

The second is permission, recorded. x401's real trick is binding identity to authorisation: who the agent is, and what it may do. Reginald's provenance steps can carry that scope as a checked precondition. The trail then proves the whole sentence: this verified agent, allowed to do this, acted on this page, under this regime. That's a far stronger thing to hand a regulator than any of the three pieces alone.

The third is the single verifier from a moment ago. One library, checking the actor and the artefact with the same key math.

The One Thing That Has to Change

I'll be straight about the catch, because it's small and it's real.

"Same primitive" isn't yet "same bytes." The Verifiable Credentials world canonicalises its JSON with a standard called JCS before signing. Reginald currently uses a home-grown scheme for the same step. The signatures are the same kind of signature, but they're computed over differently-shaped input, so a standard Verifiable Credentials library can't verify a Reginald attestation as-is. The shared-verifier promise doesn't hold until that lines up.

The fix is to adopt JCS for the signing step, version it so existing signatures keep verifying, and prove a new signature checks out under someone else's library and not only our own. It's a contained change, and it turns a deference we already claimed into one we actually keep. We have written the decision down and put the work on the list.

The Stack, Not the Pieces

Step back, and the whole thing is simple. An agent transaction on the open web wants three things proven: who's acting, that the payment is good, and that the content is real. x401 takes the first. x402 takes the second. MX and Reginald take the third, the only one about the page rather than the visitor.

You don't have to pick a side in this. The pieces were built by separate teams who mostly haven't met, and they fit anyway, because they all bet on the same open standards. The win for you is concrete: when an agent acts on your content, you can know who it was, that it was allowed, and that what it read was genuine, and you can show all three later without reconstructing them from memory.

That's what an agent at your door is really asking for. Now both halves of the answer exist.
