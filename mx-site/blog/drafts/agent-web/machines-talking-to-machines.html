Machines Talking to Machines (Neither Knew What They Were Doing)

3 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 8 min read

This morning I asked an AI assistant to help me get a remote Mac running. It connected to a service, accepted a Tailscale invite, walked me through SSH, started a service, and recommended a model based on the hardware specifications it read from the machine itself. From the outside, it looked like a competent engineer getting a job done.

It had no idea what it was doing.

When I said "help me get halfpenny mac running," the AI asked me to clarify. It did not know what halfpenny mac was. It listed my open browser tabs trying to find it. It guessed I might mean a local application, a development server, or a piece of hardware. Only when I opened the service's account page in the browser did it understand: this was a remote Mac Mini, rented by the month, accessible over a VPN mesh. The AI read the page, understood the structure, and started helping. Everything it did from that point was accurate and useful. But its competence depended entirely on finding a structured web page that explained the context. Without that page, it was guessing.

That is the first half of the problem.

The second half arrived when the machine was running and I asked the local LLM a question. I wanted to test whether it understood what I was building. I asked: "What is the MX standard and why does machine understanding matter?" The model is a 32-billion parameter Qwen 2.5, running on an Apple M4 Pro with 24 gigabytes of unified memory. It is a capable machine. It gave me a confident, well-structured answer about Mail Exchange DNS records. It thought I was asking about email routing.

It was not wrong about what MX records are. It was simply operating without any context about who I am, what I build, or what the question meant in this specific conversation. There was nothing in its training data, and nothing in the prompt, that told it otherwise. So it defaulted to the most statistically likely meaning of "MX standard" in its training corpus. That meaning is DNS. My meaning is Machine Experience.

This is not a failure of the model. It is a failure of context delivery.

What Actually Happened

Two machines talked to each other this morning. An AI assistant on my laptop connected to a remote Mac over Tailscale, started a service called Ollama, pulled a 19-gigabyte language model, and confirmed the hardware could handle it. All of this happened through a series of SSH commands, API calls, and structured service management. The machines communicated fluently at the protocol level.

At the meaning level, they were almost completely blind.

The AI assistant did not know it was setting up a local inference node for a sovereign AI project. The local model did not know it was being deployed as a potential runtime for a document-native operating system. Both machines executed their tasks with precision and no understanding of purpose. If I had not been there to supply the intent, the whole session would have been a technically successful exercise in context-free computation.

This is how most machine-to-machine interaction works right now. Machines talk at the protocol layer. They exchange credentials, negotiate connections, pass structured data, and confirm success. What they do not exchange is meaning. They do not tell each other what the work is for, who commissioned it, what constraints apply, or what the output represents. That information lives only in the human's head, and it disappears the moment the human steps away from the keyboard.

The Pattern Underneath

The pattern is not unique to AI assistants and remote Macs. It shows up everywhere machines interact with machines.

A CMS publishes content to a CDN. The CDN has no idea what the content means, who it is for, or whether it should be indexed by AI crawlers. A document lands in a shared drive. The agents trained to search that drive have no way to know whether the document is a draft, a final, a confidential brief, or a published standard. A codebase gets cloned to a remote server. The server has no information about what the code is supposed to do, who owns it, or what its dependencies mean in context.

In every case, the machines complete the mechanical task perfectly and carry no understanding of the work forward. The meaning evaporates at every handoff.

This is the machines-talking-to-machines problem. It is not a new problem. It is the oldest problem in information systems, restated for a world where the machines are increasingly capable of operating autonomously. When machines could only do exactly what they were told, missing context was a human annoyance. When machines can reason, plan, and act on behalf of humans, missing context becomes a source of confident, well-intentioned mistakes at scale.

The Fix Is Not a Better Model

The reflex response to this problem is to train better models or write better prompts. Both help at the margins. Neither addresses the structural issue.

A better model would have known that "MX standard" might mean something other than DNS records. It would have asked a clarifying question. That is an improvement, but it is an improvement in error handling, not in context availability. The model still does not know who I am, what I am building, or what the question means in the context of this project. It is guessing more carefully.

A better prompt solves it for one conversation. The next conversation starts from scratch. The context has to be reconstructed, re-explained, and re-attached to every new interaction. This is what I mean when I say AI currently operates like an oral culture: the knowledge is rich, but it does not persist. Every session is a new first day.

The fix is structured, declared, machine-readable context that travels with the work. Not a prompt. Not a system message reconstructed from memory. Context that lives in the document, the file, the repository, and the service itself. Context that any capable machine can read before it acts, regardless of which model is running, which service is involved, or how much time has passed since the last interaction.

What This Looks Like in Practice

When I set up the remote Mac this morning, the AI found the context it needed on a web page. It read the structured information about the machine, the service, the credentials, and the connection method, and it used that information to act correctly. The web page was doing the work that a COG does inside a repository: declaring what a thing is, how it works, and what context any agent needs to use it correctly.

The difference is that the web page was designed for humans. It was readable by the AI because the AI can read anything. It was not designed for machines. It had no machine-readable declarations about purpose, permissions, provenance, or intent. The AI inferred all of that from the structure of the human-readable page, which is a reasonable approximation, but not a foundation you can build a standard on.

A COG in that service's account page would declare, in machine-readable frontmatter: this is an inference node, it belongs to this project, it runs under these constraints, it should be treated as a sovereign compute resource for AI workloads that must not be exposed to external APIs. Any agent reading that COG before acting would know the purpose of the machine, not just the mechanics of connecting to it.

The same logic applies to the local model. If the repository it will eventually work with contains a SOUL.md that declares what MX is, what constraints apply, and what context any agent should carry into every interaction, the model does not need to guess. It reads the declaration first. The question "what is the MX standard" becomes unambiguous before the model generates a single token.

This is what MX OS is. It is the practice of making the context machine-readable before the machine arrives, so that every machine involved in the work, at every layer of the stack, operates with declared understanding rather than statistical inference.

The Moment That Matters

There was a moment this morning, between the model confirming it had no context about MX and the realization that it was about to be used for work that depends entirely on MX understanding, where the problem became perfectly visible.

Two capable machines were in the same network, talking to each other cleanly, executing tasks correctly, and sharing no meaningful understanding of what they were for. One had just helped set the other up. Neither knew what they were doing in any sense that would survive the end of the session.

That is the state of most enterprise AI deployment right now. Machines talking to machines, competently and blindly, with the meaning stored only in human heads and reconstructed from scratch on every call.

MX is the practice of getting the context out of the human's head and into the document before the machines start talking.
