The Agent That Hallucinated My Timezone

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

I pitched Matt at CMS Critic an article this week, and it argued one thing: content management platforms are quietly breaking an implicit contract. For thirty years the deal was that a person would read what you published. That person is now, increasingly, a machine. It arrives without the page around it, without the context you took for granted, and it acts on whatever signals it can actually read. When those signals are missing, it doesn't stop. It guesses.

Matt liked the piece. He staged it the same day and suggested we record a podcast to talk it through. We settled on a slot without any fuss: ten in the morning his time on the US East Coast, four in the afternoon mine. He said he'd send an invite. Easy.

Then the invite landed, and it was wrong by six hours.

The contract failed on the way to the call

The calendar invite came through as ten at night my time. Not four in the afternoon. I read it twice, because a six-hour error is a strange one, and then I worked out what had happened. Something had taken the meeting and resolved my end of it as though I were sitting in the United States, not in Europe. It hadn't asked where I was. It had assumed, and it had assumed wrong.

I flagged it. Matt came straight back: he'd used an AI scheduling agent to send the invite. "Ah, such is life." No drama, no blame. The agent had done what agents do. It held a meeting and two people, and it needed to place that meeting on a calendar. The one fact it most needed, where I actually am, was nowhere it could read. So it filled the gap with the most statistically likely guess, and for an agent that mostly books calls between Americans, that means assuming everyone is American. It hallucinated my timezone.

I had been making this exact argument in prose for the article, and here it was happening to me, in my own inbox, while I wrote it.

The agent did nothing wrong, and that is the point

It's tempting to call this a bug in the scheduler. It isn't. The agent behaved sensibly given what it could see. The failure sits one layer down, in the thing it was reading. My identity, as it reached that agent, didn't carry the one piece of provenance the task turned on. There was no machine-readable record travelling with me that said, plainly, this person is in Central European Time. So a system that could read everything except the thing that mattered did the only thing left to it. It guessed.

That's the content contract breaking in miniature. Swap the calendar for a product page, a policy document, a press release, an audit report. A machine meets it, away from the site that produced it, stripped of the context the author assumed would always be there. The machine needs to know what this is, who stands behind it, when it was true, and where it applies. If those facts aren't carried by the artefact itself, the machine doesn't refuse the job. It infers. And inference, dressed up confidently, is how a four-in-the-afternoon call becomes a ten-at-night one.

This is the whole reason MX exists. MX is metadata that records what a thing is, where it came from, and how it's meant to be used, and it travels with the file rather than living in a database someone has to know to query. The point isn't tidiness. It's that the next machine to pick the file up, in a context you never imagined, can still read the facts off it instead of guessing them. Had my details carried that provenance, the agent would have read my timezone the way it read my name, and the invite would have been right the first time.

Such is life

I kept the ten o'clock slot. Matt offered to move it earlier and I told him not to bother, because by then the wrong time had become the best line in the piece. The agent had no context and no provenance about where I stood on the Earth, and it hallucinated the answer with total confidence. That's the failure the piece is about, delivered to my calendar as a working demonstration by the very kind of system it warns against.

I couldn't have staged it better. The medium turned out to be the message, and it arrived six hours late.
