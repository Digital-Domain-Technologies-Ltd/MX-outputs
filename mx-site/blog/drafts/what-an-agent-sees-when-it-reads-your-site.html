What an Agent Sees When It Reads Your Site

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

Right now, while you read this, an AI agent is reading your site. Maybe a model answering a customer's question. Maybe a buying agent sizing you up against a competitor. Maybe a crawler feeding the next training run. It arrives, it reads, it reaches a verdict, and it leaves. You never see the verdict. You just live with its consequences.

The hard part is that the agent sees your site nothing like you do. It skips your JavaScript, ignores your lovely layout, and gives you no benefit of the doubt. It reads what is actually there at fetch time and works with that. If the price is hidden behind a render step, it is not there. If the meaning lives in an image, it is not there. The agent moves on, and you never find out why.

Our audit exists to show you the verdict before it costs you anything. And the way we run it is worth explaining, because how it is built is the reason you can trust what it tells you.

A team of agents, and the first ones never guess

The audit is not one clever model having an opinion about your site. It is a team of agents, each with a narrow job, and the order they work in is the whole point.

The first agents do not write a word of prose. They measure. They fetch your pages the way real machine readers do, and record what is there: what survives before any script runs, whether your structured data is present and correct, if the facts a buyer needs sit in the HTML or stay hidden, what your discovery files declare, how the page holds up under the byte caps and timeouts a live crawler imposes. These checks are deterministic. Run them on the same page twice and you get the same answer both times. There is no mood, no inference, no good day or bad day. Whatever a machine can measure precisely, it measures the same way every single run. That is where every number in your report comes from.

This matters more than it sounds. Most "AI audits" are a model glancing at your site and free-associating. Ours fixes the facts first, in a form you can reproduce, before any language model is allowed near them.

The model writes the report, it does not invent the findings

Once the facts are measured, you still need a report a human can read. That is where a language model comes in, and this is the part most people get backwards.

The model does not go and look at your site and decide what is wrong. The findings are already fixed by the measurement step. The model's only job is to turn those settled facts into clear prose, to explain a problem and why it matters, to rank what to fix first. The facts are fenced in. The language is generated around them, never the other way round. If a fact was not measured, it does not get written, because there is nothing for the model to dress up.

There is a second reason this is built the way it is, and for some of you it is the one that matters most. The model that writes your report runs locally and air-gapped, on hardware we own. Your pages, your content, your commercial facts are not shipped off to someone else's cloud to be read by a third-party service and quietly retained. For a bank, a law firm, a healthcare provider, anyone who cannot let client material wander, that is not a nice-to-have. It is the difference between being able to run the audit at all and not. The whole thing can run inside your own network.

Every action is recorded, dated, and attributable

While the audit runs, every step it takes is written to a central record. Not a marketing flourish, an actual log: what was checked, what was found, which agent did it, what the model was asked, what it answered, and when. Each artefact we hand you carries its own evidence trail alongside it.

So the report is not "trust us, we looked". It is a dated, verifiable account you can hand to a colleague, a regulator, or a buyer doing due diligence, and they can walk it themselves. In a world where more and more of what organisations do is done by machines acting on their behalf, a tamper-evident record of what was checked and what was decided is exactly the kind of documentation you are increasingly expected to be able to produce. We do not grant you compliance with anything; that stays your legal duty. What we give you is the documentation that duty asks for, structured so a machine can read it and verify it.

Accessibility is the same problem, seen from the other side

Here is the part that surprises people. The work that makes your site readable to an AI agent is almost exactly what makes it legible to a person using a screen reader.

There is no single kind of machine visitor. A small on-device model with a tight memory budget, a large one with browsing tools, a plain scraper, a converter that flattens your page to text: each reads a different, narrower projection of it, and not one of them sees the visual layout. A screen reader is in exactly the same position. It consumes the structure underneath the design, the same tree of names and roles and relationships, and it is blind to anything you expressed only with colour, position, or a picture.

So when our audit flags a button that is really a styled box with no role, a link named nothing but "read more", a form field labelled only by a fading grey placeholder, a fact locked inside an image, it is naming a failure that hits the agent and the human at once. Fix it once and you serve both. And because these failures tend to repeat across pages built from the same template, we report them as one fix to make, not a hundred copies to wade through. Accessibility and machine-readiness are not two projects. They are one, and you have been quietly paying for the gap in both.

See the verdict before it costs you

Nobody can improve what they cannot see, and right now the machine's verdict on your site is invisible to you and visible to everyone the machine talks to. The audit makes it visible. You will know what an agent actually sees when it reads your pages, which findings matter most, and what changes the verdict fastest, all of it backed by a dated record you can show anyone.

The machines are already reading your site and reaching their conclusions. The only question is whether you get to read along.
