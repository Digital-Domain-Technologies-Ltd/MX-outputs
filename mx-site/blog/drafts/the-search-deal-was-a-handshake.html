The Search Deal Was a Handshake

8 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read
  • Ai Search
  • Crawlers
  • Pay Per Crawl
  • Publishers
  • Provenance
  • Terms Of Use
  • Governance
  • Mx

A friend sent me a screenshot this week. It was one of those posts that does the rounds because it names something everyone has half-felt: AI search broke the deal the web ran on, and now the web is trying to break it back. The post called the AI answer layer a tollbooth that copied the map, blocked the road, and started charging everyone to get home.

I checked the facts in it, because confident posts about regulators and platforms are usually a little ahead of the evidence. This one was not. The numbers hold up. That is what made me want to write the part the post leaves out.

The deal nobody signed

For twenty years the web ran on a bargain that no one ever wrote down. A search engine crawled your content for free. In exchange, when someone searched, it sent them to you with a link and a snippet, and you got the visit. Crawl in, traffic out. Nobody signed it. There was no contract, no schedule of terms, no machine either side could point to and say "this is what we agreed". It was a handshake, held in place by mutual interest and the absence of a better idea.

A handshake works right up until the interests stop lining up. They have now stopped.

What actually changed, in numbers

The Pew Research Center measured the break in July 2025. Users who saw an AI summary on a Google results page clicked through to a traditional link in 8% of visits. Users who saw no summary clicked through in 15%. So the answer box did not nudge behaviour, it roughly halved it. And the link inside the summary, the citation that was meant to keep the old bargain alive in a new shape, got clicked in 1% of visits. The crawl still happens. The traffic back does not.

The other side of the road moved at the same time. In July 2025 Cloudflare became the first major infrastructure provider to block AI crawlers by default. Every new domain on the network is now asked, on sign-up, whether to let AI crawlers in at all. Alongside it Cloudflare launched pay per crawl: a crawler either presents payment intent in its request headers and gets the content, or it gets back an HTTP 402, Payment Required, with a price. Publishers pick per crawler: allow, charge, or block.

Both numbers are real, and together they describe a standoff. One side stopped sending traffic. The other side started building tollbooths. Neither move repairs the bargain. Each is a blunt instrument reaching for leverage the handshake never had to define.

A tollbooth without a schedule is a wall with a card reader

I want to be fair to pay per crawl, because it is the more interesting of the two moves. It is the first time the web has had a standard way for a page to say "access has a price" to a machine and have the machine understand the answer. That is a real primitive, and it is overdue.

But a tollbooth is only as good as its schedule. A 402 with one domain-wide price treats the front page and the archive, the press release and the proprietary dataset, the public explainer and the paywalled report, as the same thing. It answers one question, will you pay, and stays silent on every question that actually governs reuse. May this be quoted with attribution but not used for training? May an accessibility tool read it aloud for free while a model pays to ingest it? Is this fact something the publisher actually holds, or a paraphrase three hops from the source? Charge-everyone is not a contract. It is a wall with a card reader bolted on, and it turns away the crawler that would have ranked you and the screen reader that would have read you to a blind visitor, right alongside the scraper you were worried about.

Regulators have started to circle the same gap from the transparency side. On 3 June 2026 the UK's Competition and Markets Authority imposed its first conduct requirement on Google Search under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, ordering Google to give publishers per-feature, disaggregated engagement data for AI search and controls to withhold content from it. The direction is the same one the tollbooth points at: when a machine uses your content, the terms of that use should be explicit, attributable, and readable by both sides.

The missing middle

The handshake failed because it was never written in a form either party could read back. A wall fails because it answers the wrong question, in or out, to an audience that is never one thing. A flat tollbooth fails because it answers only "how much", once, for content that carries dozens of different intentions.

What sits in the middle is the part MX has been about from the start: terms that travel with the resource, written so a machine can read them without a lawyer in the loop. Not a site-wide yes or no in a robots file, and not a single price at the edge, but a declaration attached to the content itself. What this is. Who made it. What an agent may do with it: quote, summarise, train, ingest, embed, and on what terms for each. The page stops being a thing a crawler either takes or is denied, and becomes a thing that states its own terms of use, per resource, in a carrier the machine already parses.

That is the half that makes content machine-readable. There is a second half, because a term nobody can verify is just a wish. A declaration that says "do not train on this" or "this figure is attested by the publisher" only changes behaviour if the agent on the other side can check that the claim is genuine, came from who it says, and has not been altered since. That check has to run on the reader's side, against an identity the reader can resolve independently, the same way a browser verifies a certificate without phoning the company. This is the layer Reginald adds: a way for a claim, including a claim about terms, to carry proof.

Two pillars, the way we always frame it. MX makes the content machine-readable, so the terms are legible. The signing layer makes it machine-trustworthy, so the terms are enforceable by verification rather than by hope. With both, the exchange the handshake used to carry becomes explicit and accountable. A crawler reads the terms, honours them or does not, and either way there is a record. A publisher charges for the dataset and gives the explainer away, in the same breath, to the same machine. An auditor walking back from an AI answer can see which source it leaned on and whether the terms attached to that source allowed it.

The deal does not have to stay broken

The screenshot was right that the old bargain is gone, and right that the moves replacing it so far are crude. Where I would push past the post is its ending. A tollbooth is not the end state. It is the first clumsy admission that the handshake needed terms, made by the one party with a card reader and no schedule to put behind it.

The web does not need to choose between giving everything away and charging for the privilege of being read. It needs the thing the handshake skipped: terms a machine can read, attached to each resource, and proof that the terms are real. Build that, and the road reopens, except this time the deal is written down, and both sides can check it.
