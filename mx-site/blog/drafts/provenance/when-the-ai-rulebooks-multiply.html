When the AI Rulebooks Multiply, the Evidence Has to Travel

21 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

On 16 July 2026, twenty-nine countries signed a treaty in Shanghai founding a new intergovernmental body for AI, the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization (US News, Xinhua, Global Times). Its founding signatories include Russia, Pakistan, Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Brazil, and a wide span of African and Asian states. No G7 country signed. The United States wasn't there, nor the United Kingdom, nor any EU state, though the UN Secretary-General attended the ceremony.

Look past the diplomacy and you can see how the next decade lines up. AI governance isn't one conversation heading toward a single rulebook any more. It's splitting into blocs, each with its own body, its own language, and its own idea of what a trustworthy AI system has to prove.

Two rulebooks now, and more coming

The European Union has its AI Act, in force, built on documentation, traceability, and the demand to produce evidence on request.

The new Shanghai body opens a second centre of gravity, and it speaks the familiar language of governance: that AI ought to be safe and fair and work for everyone, built around people. The United States belongs to neither camp and is assembling review machinery of its own. Add the UK, Canada, Australia, Singapore, and Japan, each on its own clock, and what you've got isn't one wall every company eventually hits. It's a row of walls, at odd heights, facing away from each other.

Every one of these regimes says a version of the same sentence: show me that your AI system is what you claim it is. None of them agrees on the format, the authority, or the checkpoint. And a company doesn't get to pick one. A model trained in one country, hosted in a second, and used in a third answers to all of them at once.

The rulebook stays at the border; the file crosses it

One fact decides who gets through this cleanly. A regulation is tied to a place. A document isn't. Content moves across all of these boundaries as a matter of routine, carried by an agent, cached by a service, quoted in a report drafted on another continent. The rule that governed it back home doesn't ride along. When it lands in the next bloc, the local system reads whatever the file itself carries, and stops there.

So the question stops being "which regime do we build for" and turns into "what does this file say about itself, wherever it ends up." If the answer is a PDF and a promise, every bloc has to take that on trust, and each one trusts in its own way. If the answer is a machine-readable record any system can check with open tools, content clears each border on its own evidence, the same way every time.

That record is what MX and REGINALD carry. MX makes content machine-readable: metadata that states what a file is, who made it, and how it can be used, riding along instead of sitting in a database that stays behind. REGINALD makes it machine-trustworthy: a signed, tamper-evident attestation anyone can verify without asking the publisher for access to a thing. Readable first, then checkable. The two travel together because neither is much use alone, a formatted guess or a locked box.

Jurisdiction-neutral by construction, not by promise

This survives the split because of how it's built. MX and REGINALD don't lean on any single bloc's blessing, because they're assembled from standards that already cross every border: Dublin Core and Schema.org for description, DCAT for cataloguing, IETF RFC 9421 for the signature, JCS and Ed25519 for the cryptography, DID Core for identity. A verifier in Shanghai, Brussels, or Washington runs the same open libraries and gets the same result. That result is a property of the file, not a permission handed down by a local authority.

Neutrality here is structural, not a slogan. The metadata standard is held by The Gathering, an open community body, kept apart from the commercial engine that sells the registry. That separation is what lets a record be trusted across camps that don't trust each other. A verification layer owned by one bloc would be evidence at home and propaganda abroad. One owned by nobody is evidence everywhere.

This isn't compliance in a box

The honest limit matters, because overclaiming is how a good tool loses a serious buyer. MX and REGINALD don't grant compliance with the AI Act, with the new Shanghai framework, or with any other regime. Compliance stays a legal duty of the organisation. What the pair produces is the very thing each of these regimes is going to ask an organisation to hand over: structured, machine-readable, tamper-evident, verifiable proof of what a system is and who stands behind it. Same architecture, many rulebooks. The regimes keep multiplying; the evidence a machine can check doesn't have to.

The fragmentation is the whole argument. When there was going to be one rulebook, you could picture building to it and being finished. There won't be one rulebook. There'll be several, drifting apart, and the content your organisation publishes will have to satisfy all of them without your knowing ahead of time which one is reading. The way through isn't to bet on a bloc. It's to make each file carry its own evidence, in a form every bloc can read, before any of them asks.
