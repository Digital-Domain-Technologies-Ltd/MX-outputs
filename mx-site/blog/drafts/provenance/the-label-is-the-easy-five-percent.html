The label is the easy five percent

24 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

This week Fabio Marcello Salvadori, a sovereign-AI architect writing in his Signals from the AI Edge newsletter, published a short piece with a long shadow. His argument, stripped down: everyone is reading the EU's new Code of Practice on labelling AI-generated content as a story about an icon you drop in the corner of an image, and the icon is the easy five percent. The expensive ninety-five percent is the paper trail sitting behind it.

He is right, and it is worth saying why in plain terms, because this is the argument I have been making for two years and he has framed the hard part more sharply than I had.

The label is not the question

Read past the label and the regulation is asking a much harder set of questions. Who generated this? Who changed it? Did a person review it? Who is accountable for publishing it? And can you still prove all of that to a regulator six months from now, when nobody in the building remembers how the content was made?

That is a provenance problem, which is a plain way of saying you need a reliable record of where a piece of content came from and what happened to it on the way out. Most organisations have no such record today. They can put an icon on an image in an afternoon. They cannot answer the five questions, because keeping the answers was never a feature anyone asked for.

The mark has to survive the real world

Here is the part Salvadori names that most of the coverage misses. Marking content at the moment of creation is the easy bit. The hard bit is that the mark has to survive contact with the world. A screenshot, a crop, a re-compression, a paraphrase, a translation: any one of them can quietly strip a marker that lives in the wrong place. A label painted on the surface of a file does not travel. The moment the file is copied into a slide, summarised by an assistant, or pulled one chunk at a time into a retrieval system, the label is gone and the questions come back unanswered.

This is the whole reason I keep insisting the metadata belongs inside the file, and that it has to travel with the file. A marker that only exists on the page you published is a marker you lose the first time a machine touches your content. We wrote about the field that lets a file say it was made by a machine when the Code first landed. The field is the easy five percent. Making it survive, and making it provable, is the rest.

It arrives as a questionnaire, not a fine

The headline number is real. The Code points at obligations under Article 50 of the EU AI Act, which apply from 2 August 2026 whether you sign the Code or not, and they sit in the penalty tier that reaches fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide turnover. That is where the "€15M label" framing comes from.

But the fine is not what reaches you first, and it is not the thing most companies should be planning around. What reaches you first is a vendor questionnaire. Your European customers will start asking whether your systems can mark their outputs, whether those marks survive once the content leaves your platform, and whether you can prove any of it without them having to take your word for it. They will ask months before any national authority is involved, because the obligation runs down their supply chain to you, and the cheapest way for a large buyer to manage an obligation is to push it onto every supplier in a procurement form.

That is the timeline that matters. Not "a regulator might fine us in 2027". It is "a customer will ask us to prove this in the third quarter, and the bid is set aside if we cannot". I have written before that the fine is not the point; the evidence is. This is the same lesson with a deadline attached. The companies that filed provenance under "things we will get to eventually" just found out it is a legal exposure with a date on it.

What the record actually has to contain

Strip the regulation back and a usable record answers the five questions for any given item, and keeps answering them after the file has left your hands. Who generated it. Who changed it. Whether a human reviewed it. Who is accountable. And it has to be checkable by a third party who will not simply take your word.

That splits cleanly into two jobs. The first is making the content machine-readable, so the marker and the record travel inside the file rather than on the page around it. The second is making it machine-trustworthy, so a customer or a regulator can verify the record was not edited after the fact. The first is MX. The second is REGINALD. A label answers neither on its own; it just tells you the questions have arrived.
