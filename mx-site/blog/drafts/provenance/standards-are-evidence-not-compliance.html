Standards Are Evidence, Not Compliance

5 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 10 min read

Tom Cranstoun · The Machine Experience Authority · CogNovaMX Ltd

There is a certificate on the wall of a great many offices right now. It says the organisation has been assessed against a recognised standard, and it was not cheap or quick to earn. Near it, often on the same week's agenda, sits a letter or a board paper about a new regulation. The quiet assumption behind both is that the first document answers the second. Buy the certificate, frame it, and the legal question is closed.

It is a comfortable misreading, and it is wrong. The certificate proves you run a system. It does not prove you have met the law. Those are two claims, earned in different ways, and the distance between them is where the next two years of AI regulation will be fought.

This is not a quirk of one standard or one statute. It recurs across every AI-governance regime now arriving, on both sides of the Atlantic. Once you see the pattern, the certificate stops looking like a shield and becomes what it always was: a piece of evidence.

A Standard Is a Tool. A Regulation Is a Duty

Start with the distinction underneath everything else.

A regulation is the law. It states a duty: you must manage risk, you must keep records, you must be transparent with the people your system affects. It is enacted by a legislature or a commission, and it binds you whether or not you have ever heard of any standard.

A standard is a tool. It describes a disciplined way to meet a duty: here is how to run a management system, here is how to structure a risk assessment, here is what good record-keeping looks like. Standards are written by standards bodies, not lawmakers. Certifying against one proves you operate the system the standard describes. It says nothing, on its own, about whether a specific obligation in the law has been satisfied.

ISO/IEC 42001 is a clean example. It is the international standard for an artificial-intelligence management system: the governance scaffolding around how an organisation builds, buys, and runs AI. Earning the certificate is real work and worth doing. It is also a statement about your system, not about your conformity with any particular regulation. The wall and the board paper are answering different questions.

The European Route: Presumption of Conformity

Europe built a clever bridge between the tool and the duty, and understanding it shows precisely why a certificate is not enough.

The EU AI Act, in Article 40, offers a mechanism called presumption of conformity. A high-risk system that conforms to a relevant harmonised standard is presumed to conform to the matching legal requirements. This is the real prize. Presumption flips the burden: instead of you proving from first principles that you met the law, the regulator must show you did not. For anyone deploying high-risk AI, that is worth a great deal.

Here is the catch that the framed-certificate reading misses. Presumption attaches only to a harmonised European standard, one published and cited in the Official Journal of the European Union after a formal request from the Commission, with the annex that maps its clauses to the Act's requirements. That citation is the whole point. Without it, a standard is good practice, not a legal bridge.

ISO/IEC 42001 today is an international standard with no such citation. It carries no presumption of conformity under the Act. It is expected to feed a harmonised European standard through the CEN-CENELEC process, and the candidate to watch is prEN 18286, now in development. Once a standard like that is published and cited in the Official Journal, presumption becomes available. Until then, holding ISO/IEC 42001 is evidence of a capable, accountable organisation that an assessor will read with interest. It is not the bridge, yet.

The European Accessibility Act works the same way, which is worth saying because the accessibility world has lived this pattern for years. The Act sets duties; conformity runs through the New Legislative Framework, CE marking, and harmonised standards. Meeting a harmonised standard buys presumption; a general claim of good accessibility practice does not. The machinery the AI Act is now standing up is what European product law has used for years.

The American Route: A Different Road to the Same Place

The United States has no single AI statute and no presumption-of-conformity mechanism. It would be easy to conclude the gap does not apply there. It applies just as firmly; the road is simply different.

The state regimes reach the duty through documentation and audit rather than through harmonised standards. Colorado's AI Act asks developers and deployers of high-risk systems to keep risk-management documentation and impact assessments. New York City's Local Law 144 requires a bias audit of an automated employment decision tool and the publication of its results. Texas and several California statutes layer on transparency, disclosure, and record-keeping duties of their own.

None of these hands you a certificate that ends the inquiry. Every one of them asks you to produce something: an assessment, an audit result, a disclosure, a record that an enforcer or a plaintiff can later demand to see. The European route promises a presumption if you follow the cited standard. The American route demands evidence if anyone asks. Different mechanisms, identical destination. The organisation has to be able to show its work.

What Every Regime Actually Wants

Stand back from the detail and the regimes rhyme. Strip away the jurisdictional specifics and each one asks for the same underlying thing: documentation that survives scrutiny.

Records of how the system was built and what data trained it. Logs of what it did and when. A risk assessment that was actually performed, not back-dated. Evidence of human oversight where the law requires it. The ability, when a regulator or an auditor arrives, to trace a requirement to the specific decision or record that satisfies it, without assembling the answer by hand the night before.

A conformity assessment under the EU AI Act, in Article 43, is precisely this kind of exercise. The assessor walks the technical documentation, the logging, the risk management, the data governance, and checks that each requirement is met by something real. The certificate on the wall is one input to that walk. The evidence chain is the work. A bias-audit publication under Local Law 144 is the same instinct in a different costume: show the result and the method, and let both be checked.

This is the sentence to keep. Standards and certificates are evidence an assessor reads. They are not the compliance itself. The honest pitch, queryable and verifiable evidence, is exactly what regulators, auditors, and the engineers building AI systems need. The dishonest one, compliance in a box, collapses on the first inquiry.

Where MX and Reginald Fit

If the universal demand is for documentation that survives scrutiny, then the useful question is not "which certificate do I buy" but "how do I make my evidence readable and trustworthy to whoever asks." The machine asking is now part of the audience: the agent, the validator, the parser that reads a record to check a claim. It is the audience this work exists for.

Two jobs sit underneath that, and they are easy to blur. Making the documentation machine-readable is one job: structure, provenance, context, and intended use travelling with the file, so an assessor or an agent can read the evidence in a single fetch instead of reconstructing it. Making the documentation trustworthy is a different job: signed, tamper-evident, sequence-bound records that a reader can verify rather than take on faith.

MX does the first. It is the metadata that records a file's provenance, context, and intended use, and travels with the file wherever it goes. Reginald does the second: it produces the tamper-evident provenance records and the registry attestations a client checks for itself, against open standards, without trusting a middleman. The two are deliberately separate layers on an open standard, so that readability belongs to everyone and verification belongs to no single vendor.

Together they produce the evidence pack an assessment consumes. The provenance records pair an account of every automated step with one for every rule-driven action. The regime registry behind them now tracks, for each standard, whether it has been cited as harmonised in the Official Journal, so the moment real presumption of conformity becomes available is recorded rather than guessed. That is the difference between owning a certificate and being able to answer, on demand, the question the regulation actually asks.

The human oversight those regimes ask for is written into the same records, not attested on a separate form. An automated step that carries no human check is treated as incomplete, so the evidence shows not only what the machine did but the person who reviewed it and stands behind it. The AI attaches the trail; a human checks it; a deterministic gate refuses any trail that names a machine step and no human. Human oversight stops being a box an auditor hopes was ticked and becomes a fact in the record.

The Honest Limits

I will not oversell this, because the failure mode of governance technology is to promise more than it can deliver.

MX and Reginald do not grant compliance with any regulation. They do not stand in for a notified body's assessment. They do not turn a false claim into a true one. What they do is make the documentation an organisation already owes structured, tamper-evident, and verifiable on request. The legal duty stays exactly where the law puts it: with the organisation.

That limit is not a weakness to hide. It is the honest version of the same line the certificate-sellers should be drawing and often are not. Evidence makes your position legible and checkable. It does not make the position correct for you. Anyone who tells you a tool grants compliance is selling the framed-certificate misreading back to you at a markup.

How to Start

You do not need the full apparatus to begin, and you should not wait for every standard to be cited before you act.

Stop treating the certificate as the destination. Make the evidence behind it machine-readable now: who built the system, on what data, under what oversight, with what risk assessment, expressed so a machine can read it rather than buried in a PDF nobody can query. Sign the records that an assessment or an audit will demand, starting with the systems where a wrong answer is expensive: the ones that make decisions about people, money, health, or rights. And track which standards are actually cited in the Official Journal, so you know the day presumption of conformity stops being a promise and becomes available.

The certificate on the wall was never the answer to the letter on the desk. It was always one piece of evidence among the many the law will eventually ask you to produce. The work now is to make that evidence readable, checkable, and ready, for the auditor, the regulator, and the machine alike. Build the evidence, and the certificate becomes what it should have been all along: a part of the story, not the whole of it.
