The Agents Acting in Your Name

1 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

A post landed on my feed this week from Birgul Cotelli, an AI governance strategist, quoting a McKinsey QuantumBlack report published in June 2026, The Symbiotic Enterprise. The headline number is the one everyone will repeat: close to 60% of work hours are now theoretically automatable, and most companies still report no meaningful profit-and-loss impact from AI. McKinsey's explanation is the interesting part. Companies are embedding AI into workflows built for a world before AI, and calling that transformation. Nothing about the operating model has actually changed.

I want to sit with one part of that post longer than the headline number deserves, because it names a gap I have been trying to describe for a while, and it names it better than I have.

Two ways to fail, and a board caught between them

McKinsey frames two failure modes for leaders. Incrementalism: optimise the pre-AI model until an AI-native competitor makes its economics look slow. Overreach: deploy autonomous systems faster than the organisation can absorb or govern. Both are governance failures, and both are live at once inside most organisations I talk to. The caution that avoids one failure walks you straight into the other.

The post then lists four questions boards should be asking. Three of them are strategic - augmenting or reinventing, AI-native capability or legacy wrapper, exposure to a single AI provider's roadmap. Good questions, and outside my lane. The second one isn't strategic. It's operational, and it's the one I can actually help answer:

Do we have visibility into agentic systems operating in our name?

The question a governance framework can't answer from a policy document

Look at what that question actually demands. A board approves a governance framework: a document that states what agents may do, under what authority, with what oversight. Then agents act - drafting content, answering customers, making recommendations, executing multi-step tasks across systems the framework was written to constrain. The document describes intent. It says nothing about what actually happened. Unless something connects the policy to the action taken under it, "visibility into agentic systems operating in our name" isn't a capability the organisation has. It's a sentence in a slide deck.

This is the exact shape of problem MX and REGINALD exist to close. Not by writing a better governance policy - that's a genuine strategic and legal task, and not one metadata can do for you. By making sure that when an agent acts, the action leaves a record: what it did, under which policy version, and when. A provenance sidecar travels next to the artefact an agent produced; an attestation record in a registry lets anyone - an auditor, a regulator, an acquirer, your own board - walk from a governance clause to every decision that cited it, without asking the team that ran the system to curate the answer first.

Readable, attributable, corroborated

MX is the metadata layer: it makes a piece of content or a system output legible to a machine, not just a human, so an agent's action can be inspected the same way a person's decision can be minuted. REGINALD is the registry that makes those records trustworthy: tamper-evident, sequence-numbered, gap-detectable, signed. Between them they answer the board's question with evidence instead of assurance: here is the log of every agent action, here is the policy version each one cites, here is the signature that proves the log wasn't edited after the fact.

None of this is a compliance grant. Frameworks like the EU AI Act, the UK ICO's AI guidance, the US NIST AI RMF, and the Colorado AI Act increasingly expect organisations to produce exactly this kind of record - but producing evidence and satisfying the law remain two different things, and only qualified legal counsel can tell you which obligations bind your organisation.

The question worth asking your own board this week

If McKinsey is right that most AI governance frameworks were built for an AI that has already changed, the fix isn't to rewrite the document again and hope it stays current. It's to stop relying on the document for something only a log can answer. Before your next board update: pick one agentic system already running in your name, and ask whether anyone could produce, on request, a dated, signed record of what it did last week and which policy it was acting under. If the honest answer is no, that's the gap - and it's a smaller, more tractable problem than rewriting the governance framework from scratch.
