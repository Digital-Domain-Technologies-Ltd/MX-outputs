The Evidence Layer Under Your Trust Platform

10 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

If you build identity, authentication, fraud detection, or any platform that decides who is real and what is safe, you make the same call hundreds of times a second. Is this user genuine. Is this content authentic. Is this transaction safe. For a long time the only thing that mattered was whether the model got the call right. That has changed. The question now is whether you can show your work when someone asks how you decided.

"The Model Flagged It" Is Not an Answer

Most platforms cannot show their work. There is a risk score, a model that produced it, and nothing underneath. That holds up until the moment it matters. A genuine user gets blocked and escalates. An enterprise buyer runs due diligence before they sign. A regulator opens an inquiry. In each case "the model flagged it" is where the conversation starts to go wrong, because it describes an outcome and hides the reasoning.

The gap is not model quality. Your model can be excellent and you can still be unable to explain a single decision after the fact. What is missing is a record: the signals that went in, how they were weighted, the policy in force at that moment, and the human who owns the outcome.

That last item is not decoration. The record does not only log what the machine did; it names the human who reviewed the outcome and carries it. The machine acts and attaches the evidence; a person checks it and stands behind it. A record that showed a model's decision with no human accountable for it is the "the model flagged it" answer again, one level down. So the human is enforced, not hoped for: a deterministic check refuses any decision trail that records a machine step and no human beside it.

Machine-Readable Is Not Machine-Trustworthy

These are two different properties, and platforms tend to have the first without the second.

Machine-readable means another system can parse your decision without guessing. That is what MX does. MX is an open standard, governed in the open, that gives a file or a decision structured metadata describing what it is, where it came from, and how it may be used. Any agent, validator, or downstream service can read it directly.

Machine-trustworthy means the decision is provable rather than asserted. That is what REGINALD does. REGINALD is our implementation that runs on the open MX standard and records each decision as tamper-evident, verifiable evidence. MX makes the decision readable. REGINALD makes it provable. You need both, and most teams have stopped at the first.

Provable is worth unpacking, because it is not one check but several, and each answers a question someone will eventually ask. Who produced this, and when? Is this the current version, or has a newer one replaced it? Is whoever produced it still standing behind it, or have they gone quiet and left it to drift? And does an authoritative outside source independently say the same thing, or is it only the publisher's own word? Attestation, currency, freshness, corroboration. A machine reading the record can check each one on its own and see exactly which is missing, instead of being handed a single "trust me" it has no way to test.

The evidence record is a by-product of the decision, captured as it happens. It is not reconstructed later under audit pressure, which is the version that never quite holds together. You build it once, into the path every decision already travels, and from then on it is there when you need it.

What That Buys You on a Bad Day

Picture the fraud review that goes wrong. A legitimate customer is blocked, escalates, and your support team has a risk score and not much else. With an evidence layer underneath, the same dispute resolves on one screen: the signals, their weights, the policy version that was live at the time, and the person accountable for the rule. The complaint becomes a record to read, not a crisis to manage. The same screen answers the enterprise buyer's due-diligence question and the regulator's inquiry, because all three are asking the same thing in different words. Show me how you decided.

The Demand Is Global

The pressure to answer that question is arriving everywhere, and it is worth being precise about where, because it is not one region's project. The European Union has been the strongest early advocate, with the AI Act, and it is far from alone. In the United States the direction is set by the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and state laws such as Colorado's. In the United Kingdom it is ICO guidance. Comparable rules are arriving across Asia, the Gulf, and beyond. The local statute has a different name in each place; the underlying demand is the same one: explainability and auditability.

Be clear about what an evidence layer does and does not do here. It does not grant compliance. Compliance stays your legal duty. What it produces is the structured, tamper-evident, verifiable record every one of those regimes expects you to be able to show on request. That distinction matters. Sold honestly as queryable, verifiable evidence, it is what regulators and customers actually want. Sold as compliance in a box, it falls apart on the first hard question.

The Record Is the Moat

For a founder, this is the part worth sitting with. The evidence record reads like a cost until you see what it does to your position. When you can prove how you decided and your competitor can only point at a score, you are the platform an enterprise can defend to its own board and its own regulator. That is not a feature they can easily switch away from. It is a reason to choose you.

If you are designing the decision path now, this is the cheapest moment it will ever be to make it provable. Build the record into the path while the path is still being built. The platforms that can show their work will be selling to the ones that still cannot.
