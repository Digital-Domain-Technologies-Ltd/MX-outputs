The Pool Is Not the Source

17 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

Here is a thing vendors will not tell you: when your content enters their platform, it stops being yours in any meaningful sense.

Not legally. Contractually you still own it. But structurally? The content has been restructured, pooled, and managed as a derivative. The editorial provenance — who wrote it, what process approved it, what version it was at the moment of publication — is gone. What remains is a shaped copy inside someone else's architecture.

That is not a governance problem you can solve later. It is a design decision you cannot undo.

The Moment the Chain Breaks

MX provenance is not metadata you attach to a finished document. It is embedded at source, by the publisher, before the content leaves its origin. A COG — a Community Owned Governance Standard — travels with the document. It is part of the document.

The moment content moves from that document into an external system — pooled, transformed, re-indexed, re-served — the COG is not there. The derivative carries none of the original attestation. An agent reading from that pool sees content with no traceable origin and no governance record. It has to guess. It will hallucinate confidence it should not have.

Sitecore's acquisition of Scrunch illustrates the pattern clearly. Scrunch's Agent Experience Platform intercepts content at the CDN layer and serves optimised versions to different machine types — training crawlers, indexing bots, real-time retrieval agents. The engineering is sophisticated. The problem is structural: the content entering that platform has been separated from its source document. The pool is not the source.

This Is Not a Scrunch Problem

The same break happens anywhere an intermediary sits between the original document and the reading agent.

A datalake fed by a CMS. The CMS writes the original. The datalake carries a copy with no COG. Agents reading from the datalake get no governance context, regardless of how clean the CMS authoring process was.

An enterprise RAG pipeline built over pooled content. The retrieval is fast. The provenance is absent. Every answer the agent returns is built on unattested material.

A marketing team's LLM-generation workflow. An LLM is an unattested author. Unless an AI Usage Declaration explicitly records the model's role in the editorial process, that content cannot carry MX provenance — the chain is broken at generation.

An edge transformation layer. Any rewrite between source and agent is a new document that the original author did not sign off on.

In every case, the principle holds: wherever agents read, the COG must be present. Writing it at CMS source is necessary. It is not sufficient if a transformation pipeline sits between source and agent.

Two Incompatible Architectures

There is no synthesis here. These are different design philosophies with different answers to the same question: where does governance live?

AXP and similar approaches move content into an external pool and manage governance outside the document. The document becomes raw material. The platform becomes the authority.

COGs keep governance inside the document. The document travels. The governance travels with it. The authority is the publisher, not the platform.

One architecture treats content as input to a service. The other treats content as a self-governing object. They cannot interoperate on provenance. You have to choose.

What This Means for Your CMS Strategy

The question is not whether to make content machine-readable. Every serious CMS vendor is solving that now. The question is whether the solution preserves the chain from author to agent or breaks it.

A platform that ingests your content and serves its own derivative is not an MX strategy. It is a visibility layer over unattested material. That may be useful for legacy content. It is not governance.

Write the COG at source. Keep the chain intact. The pool is not the source — and agents that read from pools will never know the difference.
