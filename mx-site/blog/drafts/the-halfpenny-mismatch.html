The Halfpenny Mismatch

28 October 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

I rent a small Mac from a UK company called Halfpenny Mac. It's a Mac mini in a rack somewhere, reachable only over my private network, and it runs my own copy of Pal, the assistant I'm building. In late October 2026 I asked Pal a simple question about it.

"What is Halfpenny Mac?"

Pal told me it was a comedy podcast, hosted by a former New York Times journalist, and named him. None of it is true. There's no such podcast, and the journalist doesn't exist. Pal had no record of the company and no search to consult, so it answered from the model's memory, and the model filled the gap with something that sounded plausible.

The answer was confident and wrong. That pairing is the problem I want to write about, because the same week I watched the same failure happen somewhere I trusted more.

The Assistant Got It Wrong Too

I was setting up that Mac with an AI coding assistant. Three times in two days it told me something false, and each time it was reading a real signal.

The project's own record of the machine called it "a planned Mac mini". That record was months old, written before I had rented anything. The assistant read it and took "planned" as the present state.

It found an email from July saying my machine was ready and asking me to complete payment. It told me the payment was outstanding and the machine unreachable. The machine had been paid for and running since the summer.

Our fleet records listed the Mac's network name and "the Halfpenny" as two different machines. They're the same machine.

None of these were hallucinations in the usual sense. The record existed, the email existed, and so did the fleet entry. The assistant took the nearest signal as the truth and said so the moment the conclusion formed, before looking at anything that could tell the two apart.

We have a name for that in the MX principles: a proxy for a fact isn't the fact. A stale record stands in for the current state. An old email stands in for an account's status. A smarter guess wouldn't have helped. What helps is running the distinguishing check before saying anything, and here it cost almost nothing. I logged into the dashboard, and the machine was there.

What Pal Does Now

The podcast reply and the assistant's mistakes share one cause: something assembled from whatever was nearest, with no record to test it. So Pal now builds that record.

When Pal can't answer a "What is ...?" question from a record it holds, it queues a research job. A worker on the same Mac picks it up. It searches the web through a search engine running locally, reads the top pages, and looks first at what each page says about itself in its own structured data. The Halfpenny Mac site describes itself as a company in Schema.org markup, with its legal name, its address and its URL. The worker takes the kind of record from that declaration rather than guessing it.

It then fills a template in two passes, the same way our audit reports are built. A script places every fact that the page stated. A local language model writes the short prose sections, but it may use only what the script placed, and a sentence that introduces a number not among those facts is refused. The record is stored only when every slot is filled, and it names the source page and the date it was read.

I asked again. This time Pal answered that Halfpenny Mac is the trading name of Halfpenny Technologies Limited, which rents managed Apple silicon Mac minis by the month, hosted in the UK, for iOS builds, AI workloads and browser automation. It also said the record was researched by a machine and hadn't yet been reviewed by a person.

That last sentence matters as much as the facts. An answer that says where it came from, and how far to trust it, is one a person can check. Corroboration over assertion is the principle: the record names its sources, so the claim doesn't depend on the voice making it.

Keeping the Personal Out

Setting up that machine put a lot of personal detail within reach: an account username, a password on a dashboard, an invitation link to my home network, the date I placed the order. A careless researcher could have folded any of it into the record about the company.

These records are built to be shared, so the next person who asks the same question doesn't pay for the same search. Anything personal has no place in them. Before a record is stored, a script checks it. It refuses email addresses, tailnet addresses and names, invitation links, credentials, payment links, and anything whose only source is the owner's own mail or account. When it finds something, the worker withholds that value, keeps it in a private record that never leaves my machine, and stores the rest only if the remainder passes a second check.

I didn't leave that judgement to the model. A model can be persuaded, and it can't prove to anyone else what it chose to leave out. A script either matched the pattern or it didn't, and the test suite shows it refusing the exact kinds of detail I saw during setup.

The Lesson Is Small and General

Halfpenny Mac is a young company and this was one modest machine. The pattern is everywhere. Every assistant that answers from whatever is nearest will sometimes be confidently wrong, whether that's a model's memory, an old email, or a record that was true once. The answer is the same in each case. Keep a record that says where each fact came from and when, check it before you speak, and let a script, not a model, decide what's private.

The podcast host was invented. The fix wasn't.
