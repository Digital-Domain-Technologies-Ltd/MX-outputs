Telling the Machine What Pudding Is

18 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

So I've spent three posts circling one word. Pudding was a sausage before it was a dessert. The French hotel wears a little hat over a dead s. And a machine reading "pudding" doesn't know which sense I mean - it guesses, and it guesses differently in English than in Spanish, and worst of all in a small model. That's the diagnosis. This post is the cure, and it's almost embarrassingly plain.

If the machine is going to guess, and guess inconsistently, and you already know the answer - then tell it. That's the whole idea. The rest is just how.

Here is what we ship today:

The black pudding was excellent.

A human is fine with that. Every machine downstream now has to reconstruct, from statistics, the thing I already knew when I typed it: that I meant the sausage, not a scorched dessert. As the last post argued, that reconstruction is where the English model, the Spanish model, and the little on-device model quietly walk off in three directions.

Now here is the same sentence, but with the meaning declared beside it instead of thrown away. This is illustrative - the exact field names are not the point, the shape is - but it's faithful to how MX actually works: structured metadata that travels with the file.

# carried in the document's MX block - it goes wherever the file goes
mx:
  x-mx-terms:
    - term: "black pudding"
      sense: "blood sausage"          # the savoury one, not the dessert
      category: dish.savoury
      tradition: [british, irish]
      sameAs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pudding

Read what just happened. I didn't make the machine cleverer. I made the question disappear. There's nothing left to infer, because the sense is stated: blood sausage, savoury, and - the load-bearing line - sameAs, a pointer to a stable, language-independent entity that exists outside my document and outside any one language's vocabulary.

That sameAs is the part I want to dwell on, because it's where this stops being a tagging exercise and becomes the actual MX argument. Recall the problem from the last post: the trouble wasn't just ambiguity, it was that the word differs by language. English "pudding", Spanish budín, the sausage that Spanish files under the entirely separate word morcilla. As long as the machine reasons from the word, it's trapped inside one language's accidents. But a pointer to an entity - a Wikipedia article, a Wikidata node, any stable identifier the wider web agrees on - is not a word. It's a thing. The English model following that link, the Spanish model following that link, and the tiny local model following that link all arrive at the same node. The variance I spent a whole post worrying about collapses, not because the models improved, but because I gave them a destination instead of a riddle.

And it doesn't matter what carrier the content lives in. The fact that "black pudding here means the sausage" is the same fact whether the page is markdown, HTML, a PDF, a database row, or an image caption. So in an HTML page the identical declaration just wears a different coat:

<span data-mx-sense="blood-sausage"
      data-mx-sameas="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pudding">black pudding</span>

Same meaning, same pointer, different carrier. That's what "the metadata travels with the file" means in practice - not that you tag things once in a CMS and hope, but that the declaration rides inside the artefact itself, so a machine meeting the file cold, with no access to the system that made it, still reads what you meant.

There's one more turn, and it's the difference between readable and trustworthy. Declaring sense: blood sausage makes my meaning readable - a machine can parse it. Pointing sameAs at an external entity makes the claim corroborated - it doesn't just assert the meaning, it grounds it in a source the machine can independently check, so the claim carries weight beyond my say-so. (This is a principle we hold to hard: corroboration over assertion. A load-bearing claim should point at something authoritative that states it independently, not just announce itself.) And signing the declaration - this is the part our registry, REGINALD, exists for - makes it attributable: a machine can see who declared this, and verify the declaration hasn't been tampered with since. Readable, corroborated, attributable. The same three properties whether the content is a word, a paragraph, or a thousand-page report.

That's the entire stack, and a single sausage demonstrates all of it. MX makes the meaning machine-readable. The entity link makes it machine-authoritative. REGINALD makes it machine-trustworthy. None of it asks the model to be bigger or smarter. All of it asks the author to stop discarding what they already know.

And "trustworthy" is really four small questions a machine can ask of that one declaration. Who declared this sense, and when (attestation). Is this still the version that stands, or has a newer one superseded it while the old one stays queryable (currency). Is the author still behind it - a liveness signal that holds while they keep their registry account current and freezes if they go quiet, so even an abandoned declaration still resolves but reads as unmaintained (freshness). And does an outside source independently agree, which here is the sameAs pointer doing exactly that (corroboration). The same four hold whether the thing declared is a sausage or a thousand-page report.

So, back to the menu one last time. I'm in France, reading boudin noir, and the whole reason this series happened is that I felt the gap open - the gap between what I effortlessly know that word means and what a machine, especially a small one, in another language, would make of it. Four posts later the conclusion is the least romantic thing imaginable, and I love it for that. The fix for the machine's guessing is not to admire the machine. It's to do the author's last, skipped job: write down what you meant, point at what backs it up, and let both travel with the words. The waiter never had to guess. Neither should the machine.
