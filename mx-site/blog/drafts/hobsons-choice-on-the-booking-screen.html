Hobson's Choice on the Booking Screen

4 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

I was testing a restaurant's table-booking widget this week when it stopped me cold. Date picked, party size set, time slot chosen. Then a screen appeared: "An option is required for this booking." Below it sat a single card, the only item on the list, already ticked "Selected." There was nowhere else to go and nothing to pick beyond it, and the form still made me confirm the choice before it would let me move on.

Thomas Hobson ran a livery stable in Cambridge four hundred years ago and gave his name to exactly this trick. Customers thought they were choosing a horse. They weren't. Hobson rotated the stock and handed out whichever animal stood nearest the door: take it or leave the stable. A Hobson's choice looks like a decision and isn't one.

The booking widget I was testing had built the same trap out of software, presenting what looked like a decision point where none existed, then asking me to perform the choosing anyway. For a human, that's an extra click on a page that adds nothing, a small tax, mildly annoying, forgotten in five seconds. For a machine trying to complete the same booking, it's worse. A person glances at the card, sees the tick, and hits Next without thinking about it. An agent working through the same flow has to work out whether "Selected" already satisfies the requirement, whether it needs to click the card again to register a choice, or whether the step is genuinely gating something it hasn't understood yet. Nothing in the markup says there's only one option and it isn't really optional. The agent either guesses, stalls, or fails the booking outright, and the site owner never finds out why, because nobody watches one fail a reservation the way they'd notice a human muttering at a screen.

This is the exact test MX applies to every design decision: does it work for humans and machines, or does it help one at the expense of the other? The forced-choice screen fails both. It doesn't help the human, who gets an interstitial for a decision that was already made. It doesn't help the machine, which gets a "required" field with no real alternative and no signal that the choice is trivial. Nobody wins. The screen exists because the booking platform treats "select a dining option" as one fixed interaction regardless of whether the list holds one item or ten, and never asks if that design still makes sense once nothing is left to choose between.

The fix isn't a UI redesign. It's a piece of metadata the current interaction pattern never asked for: how many options are genuinely on offer, and whether selecting one is a real decision or a formality. A field that says there's one available option and it's already assigned tells the front end to skip the interstitial for the human, and tells any agent reading the page there's nothing to decide, only something to acknowledge. The same declaration serves both readers from the same source, which is the point of designing for both audiences at once rather than bolting an "agent mode" onto a page built for eyes alone.

None of this needs a new booking platform. It needs the option data to say what kind of thing it is: a real choice with genuine alternatives, or a single item dressed up as one. Restaurants, clinics, hotels, anywhere a "select an option" step sits between a visitor and a confirmed booking, carry the same risk.
