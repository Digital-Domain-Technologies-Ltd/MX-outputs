Where MX meets the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI

10 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, adopted in November 2021 and applicable across all 194 member states, sets out four core values and ten principles for AI that serves humanity. Read it alongside the MX core principles and something becomes clear: UNESCO defines the what, MX is starting to define the how.

This post walks through the alignment.

Four core values, one shared instinct

UNESCO's four core values are human rights and human dignity; living in peaceful, just, and interconnected societies; diversity and inclusiveness; and environment and ecosystem flourishing.

The fourth one is the cleanest match to MX. UNESCO calls for AI that supports environmental flourishing. MX principle 2 makes the same demand operational: choose what is better for the planet, reduce compute, reduce inference, reduce energy. Where UNESCO frames the ethical position, MX gives delivery teams a rule they can apply on a Monday morning when picking between two architectural options.

Diversity and inclusiveness lands in MX as a hard requirement: every MX-compliant site must meet WCAG accessibility standards. Accessibility is a requirement, not optional. That phrasing matters. UNESCO sets the value; MX refuses to ship without it.

Ten principles, mapped

The Recommendation lays out ten principles. Several map directly onto MX practice.

Transparency and Explainability. UNESCO asks that AI systems be transparent and explainable to a degree appropriate to context. MX answers this with cogs: portable, self-describing documents that carry their own metadata, intent, and update instructions. A cog explains itself to the next reader, human or machine. REGINALD, the registry layer, adds signed provenance on top.

Human Oversight and Determination. UNESCO is firm that AI must not displace human responsibility. MX principle 1 says it almost in the same breath: design for both humans and machines, with the human always in the loop, and outputs readable and reviewable by both. Reviewability is the mechanism. If a machine cannot show its working to a person, the principle is not being met.

Responsibility and Accountability. The Recommendation calls for auditable, traceable systems with oversight, impact assessment, and due diligence. The Gathering (tg.community) is being set up as an independent open standards body for MX precisely so that audit and governance sit outside any single vendor. Open standards make accountability something anyone can check.

Fairness and Non-Discrimination. UNESCO wants AI's benefits accessible to all. MX makes accessibility a gate on compliance, not a feature request. The two positions agree.

Awareness and Literacy. Public understanding of AI through open, accessible education is a UNESCO principle. MX principle 3, build on what humans already know and use, is the same idea seen from the delivery side. Do not invent new mental models when existing ones work; do not raise the literacy bar unnecessarily.

Sustainability. UNESCO ties this to the UN Sustainable Development Goals. MX ties it to compute, inference, and energy at the per-decision level. The two scales meet in the middle.

Proportionality and Do No Harm, Safety and Security, Right to Privacy and Data Protection, and Multi-stakeholder and Adaptive Governance are not contradicted by MX, but they sit further from where MX currently operates. They are policy and risk territory. MX can support them, a self-describing cog with provenance is easier to audit for privacy compliance than an opaque blob, but the principles themselves are owned by governance, not delivery.

The practical hook

UNESCO has produced two instruments to move the Recommendation from principle to practice: the Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM) and the Ethical Impact Assessment (EIA). Both need something to assess against.

This is where MX has a role. RAM and EIA work better when the system being assessed is already self-describing, accessible, energy-conscious, and auditable. An MX-compliant site or AI system gives an assessor real evidence to inspect: declared intent, declared metadata, declared accessibility conformance, declared energy posture. The ethical assessment becomes a check rather than an excavation.

Put plainly: MX is not an ethics framework. UNESCO has the ethics framework. MX is one way to make the framework's expectations measurable on the ground.

What this means for the Handbook and the Protocols

For MX: The Handbook (published 2 April 2026), the UNESCO Recommendation is useful context: it tells readers that the principles MX applies are not idiosyncratic. They are familiar to any policymaker who has read the Recommendation.

For MX: The Protocols (1 July 2026), the alignment goes further. Protocols are where MX commits to specific technical behaviours. Where a protocol enforces self-description, energy posture, accessibility, or human-readable outputs, it can be cited against the relevant UNESCO principle. That makes the Protocols more useful to teams who need to demonstrate ethical AI delivery to regulators, clients, or their own boards.

For The Gathering, the Recommendation is one of several external standards the body should explicitly track. An open standards body for MX gains weight when its standards map cleanly onto an instrument that 194 member states have already signed up to.

Closing

The UNESCO Recommendation answers the question: what should AI be for? MX answers a narrower question: how do we deliver digital experiences that machines can use without breaking what UNESCO asks for? The two questions are different, but the answers want to fit together.

If you are reading the Recommendation and wondering what it means for your delivery team next quarter, MX is one practical place to start.

The Recommendation itself is at unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics.
