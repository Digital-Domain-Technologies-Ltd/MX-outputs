The Third Audit Is You

23 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

We spend a lot of time asking what a machine sees when it reads your work. One of our audits looks at a website and reports what an AI agent actually understands when it lands there. Another looks at a code repository and reports what a copilot or a build agent can make of it. Both answer the same question from different angles: when a machine reads this, how much does it have to guess?

There is a third thing worth pointing that question at, and it is the one nobody usually checks. Not your site. Not your repository. You. Specifically, how well you and your AI assistant actually work together, day to day.

You have a powerful tool. Are you getting the most from it?

Most people who work with an AI IDE could not tell you. You installed it, you use it, some sessions go well and some go sideways, and you move on. You have a drawer full of capabilities you have never opened. You hit the same snag most weeks and never name it. And when a session turns into a slog, you genuinely cannot say whether that was your fault or the tool's.

That is the gap this audit closes. It looks at how you are set up and how you and your assistant have actually worked over a recent stretch, and it tells you, in plain language, where the effort is leaking and the one change that would help the most. Not a feature tour. A read on your own working practice.

The part that makes it useful: it knows the difference between you and the machine

Here is the distinction that turns a report into something you can act on. A working session has two parties in it. There is what you did, and there is what your assistant did, and when something goes wrong the right fix depends on which one caused it.

Say a file got rewritten a dozen times in a single sitting. That is a sign of work that started before it was planned, and it is almost always the assistant's to fix, not yours, so the advice is to ask for a plan before it starts editing. But a session where the same instruction keeps coming back around is usually shared: you can state the boundary once and completely, and the tool can re-read before it acts. Telling those apart is the whole point. Generic advice lands on the wrong person and gets ignored. This audit puts each finding where it belongs, so you change the habit that is actually yours and steer the tool on the rest.

It ends with coaching, not a grade

A score on its own is a shrug. So the report finishes with two short, practical lists. One is for you: the small setup and habit changes that pay off, each with why it helps and how to do it. The other is for your assistant: the way you brief it and configure it so the rough edges stop recurring. Then it names the single highest-leverage change, the one thing to do next, so you are not left staring at a wall of suggestions.

It is meant to read like a good colleague looking over your shoulder for ten minutes and telling you the one thing worth changing. Useful, specific, and kind.

Nothing leaves your machine

This is the part that matters most, and it is not a footnote. The audit runs entirely on your own computer. The things it reads are personal: how you work at the terminal, the tools you have, the back and forth of your sessions with the AI. So that material never goes anywhere. The reading happens locally, the writing happens locally, and it works with the network switched off. The only time it ever reaches out is to look at your public profile, the same page anyone could open, and only if you say yes. Say no and it still gives you a full report from what is on your own disk.

That is deliberate. It is what lets someone in a regulated setting run this at all, on a laptop, behind their own walls, without a single private thing crossing the room. The honest version of "we respect your privacy" is "your data never reaches us in the first place", and that is the one we built.

We ran it on ourselves first

Before this goes near anyone else, we are pointing it at our own team. That is partly humility and partly method. When two of us hit the exact same snag, that is not a personal failing in either of us, it is a sign the tool or the convention is harder to use than it should be, and it tells us what to go and fix. The audit makes us better operators and makes our tools better at the same time. We would rather learn that on ourselves than sell you a mirror we have never looked into.

If you have ever wondered whether you are getting your money's worth out of your AI assistant, this is how you find out, with a clear next step and nothing private leaving the room.
