What's missing: the best prompt I know

12 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

Most people ask an AI the wrong question about its own work. They ask whether it is good. "Does this look right?" "Is this finished?" "Any problems?" The model reads the room, sees a piece of work you clearly want to be done with, and tends to agree with you. You get a tick. You ship. The gap you never thought to mention ships with it.

I have a two-word habit that works better. After the model hands me anything, I ask: what's missing?

Confirmation gets you agreement

"Is this good?" asks the model to make a judgement, and judgement points the same way as your hope. The work exists, you built it, the natural answer is yes with a few soft caveats. That is not the model being dishonest. It is the question being weak. You asked it to grade a thing you obviously want graded well, so it grades it well.

The deeper problem is that "is this good?" only catches the faults you already suspect. You cannot ask "did you handle the thing I forgot about?" because you forgot about it. Confirmation can only confirm or deny what is already in your head. The dangerous gaps are the ones that are not.

"What's missing?" asks for a list, not a verdict

Change the question and you change the task. "What's missing?" is not a judgement, it is an enumeration. You are not asking the model to agree or disagree. You are asking it to walk the edges of the work and name what is not there. Models are good at that. They have read more checklists, more code reviews, more postmortems than any one person, and enumeration is exactly where that breadth pays off.

The move that makes it powerful: you do not need to know the gap to ask for it. "Is this secure?" requires you to suspect a security hole. "What's missing?" requires nothing. You hand the model the work and it brings back the list, including the items you would never have queried because they were not on your map.

I asked it this week, building a small piece of infrastructure I was happy with. The reply was not "looks good". It was three specific holes: a test I had skipped, a setting I had added but never documented, and a coverage gap I would have shipped without noticing. None of those surfaced when I asked whether it was done. All three surfaced the moment I asked what was missing.

The same move we make for machines

This is not a side topic for me. The whole of MX, the metadata work I do, is the same instinct pointed at files instead of conversations. A good file does not just sit there hoping a machine guesses its context. It declares what it is, where it came from, and what it still needs: its dependencies, its required context, the fields without which it cannot be used properly. Machine-readable metadata is a file answering "what's missing?" before anyone has to ask.

The principle underneath both is plain. A gap you can name is a gap you can close. A gap that lives only in your head is a gap that ships. Whether the reader is a person reviewing your draft or an agent parsing your PDF, the win comes from making the absence explicit instead of hoping it was covered.

Two cautions

It is not magic, and it has a failure mode. A model asked for gaps will sometimes invent them to look useful, padding the list with things that do not matter. So add a second step: ask it to rank the list by importance, or to split real holes from nice-to-haves. The ranking is where the judgement you actually want lives.

And the answer is a set of candidates, not a verdict. "What's missing?" surfaces things to check. You still decide which ones are real and which ones to act on. The prompt does the finding. You do the deciding.

That division is the point. Ask for confirmation and you get a tick you half wanted anyway. Ask what's missing and you get a list, and the list is almost always worth more than the work it was checking.
