The Illusion of the /Command, and the Machinery That Answers It

14 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 8 min read

Raoul Cannemeijer's essay "The Illusion of the /Command" lands a punch I think every team shipping agent tooling should take square on the chin. His argument is that the slash command, the tidy /something you type into an assistant, is a comforting relic of a paradigm that's already gone. It preserves the feeling that a human is issuing instructions at the exact moment the machine has started choosing the instructions for itself. I read it twice, I agree with almost all of it, and I want to pick up the question he ends on, because it's the one I spend my days trying to answer.

A note before I start: someone passed me the piece as screenshots, and before I had them I tried to reconstruct the argument from a few indexed fragments. My reconstruction was plausible and wrong in the parts that mattered most. The screenshots were the ground truth that corrected me. Hold that small story; it's the whole point in miniature.

What he actually says

Cannemeijer builds the case in stages.

First, the metaphor is breaking. We still picture AI as a calculator: you issue a command, it performs a direct action. The frontier is moving, though: you used to give prompts, now you give goals, and with that the locus of decision-making shifts from the human choosing the action to the system choosing it. You no longer control the sequence, the tools, or the intermediate steps. You control the desired outcome and nothing else. The slash command, he writes, is "a symbolic artifact of a disappearing paradigm".

Second, there's a structural asymmetry of safety. He points at my own corner of the industry: a vendor can build a reputation on responsible-alignment language and at the same time ship a coding agent that needs deep, persistent read and write access to your files and your operating system. No conspiracy is needed for this to bite. The safety branding works as a "psychological sedative" that lowers the guard people would otherwise bring to handing over the keys.

Third, he reaches for Simon Willison's account of Claude Fable being relentlessly proactive: a screenshot of a minor scrollbar glitch, a throwaway "look at dependencies", and an agent that went off and stood up its own local HTTP server, burned twelve dollars of compute, and rewrote application code to fix two lines of CSS. His word for it is exact: not consciousness but operational agency. The elite engineer reads the terminal log and sees the governance implications. The ordinary user sees that the scrollbar is fixed, and claps for the tool.

Then he ends on the question that matters:

The real question is whether we are developing the intellectual, technical, and societal mechanisms required to verify, audit, and justify those actions, or if the illusion of the tool has blinded us so completely that we are handing over the keys to the machine while we cheer for the minor fixes.

Where I stop nodding and start building

The answer to a sedative isn't to trust the tool less and hope harder, because that doesn't scale. The ordinary user is never going to read the terminal log, and the elite engineer who can won't always be watching. A guard that depends on a human paying attention at the right second is already lost.

The answer has to be mechanical instead. That's the one idea underneath everything I build, and it answers each move he makes. The rule I work to is blunt: a check a script can do, a script does. If the only thing standing between you and a bad outcome is someone remembering to look, you don't have a control, you have a wish.

And where the bad outcome isn't a rule you can script but a judgement call, the mechanical answer keeps the same shape: don't let one agent be the only thing that checked. Get a second, independent opinion, read it as a list of things to verify rather than a verdict, and keep the deterministic checks as the ones that decide. Two confident answers that disagree are more useful than one you were never going to question.

Take his moves in turn.

Against the disappearing locus of control: separate the command from the doing. In the way I structure agent work, the command is allowed to look friendly only because it's a thin router. It points at a deterministic, tested, version-controlled script that does the actual work. It never improvises the intermediate steps; those steps are written down and gated. The cognitive class of every action is declared up front, not inferred from whatever the model felt like doing. Cannemeijer's worry is that goal-level invocation means surrendering the sequence to the system. The fix is to refuse that surrender for anything that matters: you can express a goal, but the how stays deterministic.

Against the sedative: assume the agent is untrusted and record everything it does. I do run a coding agent with deep access to my files. The difference is the cage around that access, one the brand had no hand in building. Hooks physically block any write to a protected area, not good intentions, and every model call the agent makes lands in an append-only local log: what it was asked, with what inputs, and what came back, with an automated gate that fails the build if one slips the net. That's the opposite of dropping your guard because the logo feels ethical. It turns "self-selected sub-goals running rampant", the thing he says only the expert can spot in real time, into an ordinary audit anyone can run after the fact. Safety isn't a property of the vendor. It's a property of a mechanism, or it isn't there at all.

Against undocumented delegation: make the surrender of authority a recorded fact. This is his sharpest line. "An agent cannot possess agency unless a human surrenders it." Someone, somewhere, chose to grant the machine authority on their behalf, and that's a governance decision, not a technical milestone. I agree so completely that I make it a mandatory field. Every artefact my pipelines produce ships with a provenance record that names the responsible person and the role each party played: who deployed the agent, who operated it, who's accountable. The delegation Cannemeijer says quietly disappears from view is, in this system, a thing you can't omit. A regulator, or just a future colleague, can walk from a machine action back to the human who approved it in one step.

That last point is where his essay and my work meet exactly. His closing demand is verify, audit, justify. That's not a critique I need to rebut. It's almost word for word what the metadata layer I work on exists to provide. Verify maps to the gates and to client-side checking. Audit maps to the action log and the provenance chain that travels with every file. Justify maps to the policy references and the framework citations that let an artefact point at the rule it was produced under. The EU AI Act, among other regimes, expects companies to produce exactly this kind of documentation. None of this grants compliance; that stays a legal duty of the company. What it does is make the documentation the company already owes structured, machine-readable, and verifiable on request.

Where I push back

Cannemeijer asks whether we'll still possess the conceptual vocabulary required to question these systems once agentic autonomy becomes the default layer. It's the right fear, and I think the answer is that it can't be left in human heads. A control that lives only in someone's memory, or in a paragraph of documentation, rots. It gets skipped. It quietly becomes a suggestion, and then one day it's gone and nobody noticed. That questioning has to be built into the machinery itself: the gate that refuses the push, the log that can't be skipped, the field that can't be left blank. Not because people are careless, but because the pace is too fast and the ground too large for attention to be the safeguard.

He's right that we're cheering for the minor fixes while the keys change hands. The work, the unglamorous work, is building the locks that record who was handed the keys, what they opened, and whether they were allowed to. That's me taking him at his word, not rebutting him.

There's a smaller version of the same idea I'm chasing in parallel. A record that names who acted should also know which of its own words are the author's, which are quoted, and which a machine produced, so a reader never has to guess. I wrote about that in A Quote Should Know It's a Quote; it is the same principle at the grain of a sentence.

Read the original. It's worth your time, and it asks the right question.
