AI Readiness Is the Wrong Question. MX Is the Right Frame.

11 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

The content industry has spent the last two years asking how to get content ready for AI. It's a reasonable instinct and an unhelpful question.

'AI' covers too much ground. A customer-facing chatbot, an internal document agent, an agentic production pipeline, and an answer engine optimisation play all get called 'AI'. They make different demands on content. Preparing for one does not prepare you for another. Treating them as a single target doesn't produce a strategy. It produces a to-do list with no coherent end state.

MX doesn't ask whether your content is AI-ready. It asks three sharper questions.

Can a machine read this? Not parse it - read it, in the sense of extracting meaning reliably. Semantic HTML, structured metadata, and machine-readable formats are the floor. Without this, an agent guesses. Guesses compound. At scale, compounded guesses become liability.

This is also where accessibility and machine readability converge. MX requires WCAG compliance - not as a separate track, but because an accessible page is, structurally, a machine-readable page. The heading hierarchy a screen reader depends on is the same hierarchy an agent uses to parse a document. You do this work once. It serves both audiences.

Can a machine trust this? A machine that can read a file still doesn't know whether the content is current, who produced it, or whether it has been altered since publication. Provenance isn't a nice-to-have under frameworks like the EU AI Act - it's the evidence a team must be able to produce when an autonomous system acted on a fact. An 'AI-ready' checkbox doesn't generate that evidence. A signed attestation record does.

What is a machine allowed to do with this? This is the question almost no one is asking. Most AI-readiness frameworks stop at discoverability: can the agent find the content? MX adds a governance layer. Machine affordances are explicit operational limits written into the content itself - declarations of whether an asset may be used to answer a customer query, whether it may leave the organisation, whether it has expired. An agent that meets an affordance declaration is stopped by the rule, not by its own judgment.

And every agent operating in an MX environment has a named human owner. The affordance constrains what the machine does. The named owner is accountable when the affordance itself is wrong. Governance without that accountability is a rule with no author.

The monkey's paw problem

Agentic AI does what you instruct. That's the capability and the risk in the same sentence.

An agent handed a poorly governed content estate will produce output based on whatever it finds - retired policies, draft documents, unverified facts. It won't flag the problem. It will act on the best available evidence, which is a category that includes a lot of things you wouldn't want acted upon.

This is the monkey's paw. The wish is granted exactly as stated. The organisations treating AI readiness as a content formatting exercise are handing wishes to a system that takes them literally.

What MX adds to each AI use case

The content industry tends to cluster AI use cases into three types: external-facing products (chatbots, knowledge agents), internal operations (agentic automation of the content lifecycle), and answer engine visibility (GEO/AEO). Each makes different demands.

For external-facing products, readiness means the content can support a conversation reliably. That requires structure, but it also requires currency. A customer support agent citing a retired returns policy doesn't fail because the content was hard to find. It fails because nothing declared that the policy had changed. REGINALD - the registry CogNovaMX operates - turns that into a lookup: an agent queries a signed record and confirms the content is current before citing it.

For internal agentic operations, the bar is higher. An autonomous agent editing, approving, or publishing content on your behalf needs to know not just what the content says, but what it is allowed to do. Machine affordances handle that. They turn governance policy into machine-readable rules that travel with the asset.

For answer engine visibility, the question is whether an AI crawling your estate can understand what you publish and attribute it correctly. MX metadata - structured, canonical, carrying provenance - makes that attribution possible. Content without it gets absorbed into the model's general knowledge, unattributed and unverifiable.

The vocabulary problem underneath

There is a problem the AI readiness conversation hasn't confronted yet: different AI surfaces don't share a consistent vocabulary, even within the same brand family. An instruction that works in one implementation fails in another. A term learned in one context means nothing in the next.

This isn't a quirk. It's a structural gap. And it means that 'AI readiness' as a concept is even more fragile than it looks: you can't prepare content for a category of tool that doesn't behave consistently across its own instances.

MX addresses this by making the content the declaration, not the tool. A COG - a machine-readable metadata record - attached to an asset travels with the asset wherever it goes. It doesn't depend on the consuming tool having the right vocabulary. It states what the content is, what it may be used for, and how to verify it, in a format any conformant agent can read.

The COG format is published under the MIT licence, so anyone may implement it. CogNovaMX stewards MX and dogfoods it on the repository and site behind MX: The Protocols, and its future governance is proposed through The Gathering. REGINALD is CogNovaMX's implementation that runs on it, and it stays ours. The distinction matters: the format isn't tied to one vendor. An agent built against the open standard can read a COG regardless of who produced the content or which registry attested it.

The tool changes. The COG doesn't.

What 'ready' actually means

Ready, in MX terms, is a state you can verify, not a feeling you can assert.

A piece of content is machine-ready when it carries a declaration of what it is, a provenance record that can be checked, and affordances that constrain how a machine may act on it. The declaration is readable by any agent. The provenance is signed and queryable via REGINALD. The affordances are embedded in the asset and enforced at execution.

That's a different project from reformatting your CMS output or adding an llms.txt file. It's governance infrastructure. It takes longer to build and it compounds in value over time, because every piece of attested, bounded content is one fewer source of silent agent failure.

Ask the right question and the path becomes clearer. Not: is our content AI-ready? But: can a machine read this, can it trust this, and does it know what it's allowed to do?
