The Architecture of Participation | Tom Cranstoun

23 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

Most standards fail not because they are wrong, but because using them requires sacrifice. You stop what you are doing, fill in the schema, write the metadata, check the compliance box. The cost stays with you. The value flows elsewhere.

This is the compliance model. And it atrophies.

There is a different kind of system - one where using it and improving it are the same act. Tim O'Reilly called this the architecture of participation. He coined the term around 2004 as part of what became the Web 2.0 thesis. It is the structural reason the open web compounded in value while closed, proprietary platforms repeatedly hit ceilings and stalled.

What Made the Web Work

O'Reilly's insight was not philosophical. It was architectural. He looked at the systems that were actually growing - Linux, Google, Amazon - and asked what they had in common.

Linux: every patch a developer writes to solve their own problem flows back into the codebase. Nobody is contributing to the commons as an act of virtue. They are fixing their own system, and the architecture routes that fix to everyone.

Google PageRank: every link a web author creates to cite something useful becomes a vote in the ranking algorithm. Publishing - a self-interested act - is simultaneously a contribution to the index. You do not fill in a form to tell Google what matters. You just link.

Amazon reviews: the purchase itself is the trigger. The review infrastructure captures value from normal behaviour, not from altruism recruited on top of it.

The pattern in each case: the architecture routes self-interested behaviour into collective value without requiring an additional step.

This Is Not Crowdsourcing

Crowdsourcing asks people to do extra work. It adds a layer. Architecture of participation removes the boundary between doing and contributing. The act is the contribution.

Wikipedia is often cited as a participation model, but it is closer to crowdsourcing - it asks you to write for the commons as a separate act from your normal workflow. Compare that to Git: every commit you make to your own codebase is structured, versioned, attributable, and forkable. The architecture makes contribution native.

The difference matters at scale. Crowdsourced projects depend on volunteer motivation. Participation-architecture projects depend only on people using the system to solve their own problems. The first is fragile. The second compounds.

MX Is Built This Way

When an organisation writes a COG to describe their own repository - for their own agents, their own pipelines, their own machine-readable processes - they are simultaneously contributing a structured, provenanced artefact to the broader MX ecosystem.

They are not doing this to help MX. They are doing it because their agents need context. The architecture routes that self-interested act into collective value.

REGINALD makes this explicit. Every registration of a COG-enriched repository makes the registry more valuable for every agent querying it. The benefit compounds with each addition, passively - nobody had to be recruited to contribute.

The Gathering governance captures the same dynamic at the standard layer. As practitioners write COGs to solve their own real problems, the patterns that emerge feed back into the standard itself. The governance structure is designed to capture that flow without requiring anyone to act against their own interests.

This is why MX is a practice, not a product. You cannot install the architecture of participation. You design for it - and then get out of the way.

The Strategic Difference

Most machine-readability initiatives fail because they ask organisations to add a compliance layer on top of existing work. Schema.org markup that nobody checks. ARIA attributes added as an afterthought. Metadata entered into fields that have no effect on any system anyone actually uses.

These fail because the benefit is diffuse and the cost is immediate. The architecture is not designed for participation - it is designed for compliance.

MX is not asking organisations to document their content for the good of the ecosystem. It is offering a practice that makes their own content more useful to their own machines. The ecosystem benefit is a structural side effect.

That is the architecture of participation. And it is the reason MX compounds where compliance frameworks stall.
