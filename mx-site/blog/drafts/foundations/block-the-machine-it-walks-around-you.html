8 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 10 min read

Earlier this week I asked my reading agent to catch me up on the news about building an "agentic enterprise." It went to fetch the lead story and came back with a 403 and a spinning "Just a moment." An article about machines that act, and a machine couldn't get in.

It did what a machine does next. It walked around the wall, which had taken far longer to build than to get past. And when it got in, the article failed it a second time, for a reason that had nothing to do with the wall.

None of what follows is hacking. Every way in is a public, documented route the web already hands to machines. If a method felt like breaking in, I left it out. The article was meant to be read.

You Can't Block Machines, Only Choose Which Ones

A bot wall doesn't keep machines out. It sorts them. The polite ones identify themselves and obey the rules you publish, so they're the first ones it turns away. The determined ones dress up as a browser and walk straight through. The wall keeps out the crawler that would have ranked you and the archive that would have preserved you. The scraper you were worried about is already inside.

Watch It Walk Around

I watched my agent work the problem, and it never once did anything a browser couldn't. It just kept asking, each time a little differently.

First it asked again, the way a browser asks. A plain request with a normal User-Agent, because half of all "blocks" are a default deny on empty or odd headers. When the wall held, it loaded a real browser, the same engine developers test with, and let it run the page's own JavaScript, challenge and all. That isn't an exploit, just the page loading the way it was built to load. The next step is already shipping: an agent that runs inside your own browser, on your machine, in your logged-in session, reading exactly the bytes you would. The wall has nothing to turn away, because your browser is right there around it. SLICC is one of these, a browser-native agent that acts on the page from inside your session.

Next were the copies that exist to make reading easy. Your browser builds a reader view on request, and most pages carry a print stylesheet underneath, plain and wide open. Plenty ship a lighter mobile or AMP version of the same words. And the page itself is usually a thin shell over the site's own API, a clean endpoint serving the content as structured text.

When those ran dry, it read the copies a site already publishes for machines. The raw HTML under the JavaScript usually holds the text a search engine needs, right there in the markup, where structured data tags the headline, author, date, and body so no machine has to guess. An RSS or Atom feed often carries the full text and never touches the wall. The sitemap and robots.txt list the site's own pages and tell crawlers what's fair game, and oEmbed exists precisely so other sites can pull yours in.

And if every door on the site had been locked, it would have gone to the copies someone else keeps. Web archives like the Wayback Machine hold pages that lived for only a day, long after the wall goes up. Search engines cache what they crawl, so the snippet you see is proof they already read you. Writers syndicate themselves for reach, which scatters the same piece across a newsletter, a professional network, a mirror, the author's own site. And the big assistants read most of the open web months before anyone thought to wall it off.

What finally got me the article was the simplest route of all, a render-to-text service: hand it a URL, get back clean text. I stopped counting the ways in at a dozen. The wall stopped none of them. It only decided which one my agent took.

Not Every Machine Walks the Same Way

I want to be careful here, because my agent is the polite one. It only ever took routes the site itself, or some other site, had already opened. The moment a door felt locked, it went and found one that was already open. That is a choice it makes, and it is worth naming that not every machine makes it.

Think about three machines standing at the same wall.

The first is the one I just watched. It identifies itself, it reads the rules you publish, and it treats a locked door as a signal to look for a copy you, or someone else, left open on purpose. It is the search crawler, the archive, the citation engine. It is the audience you actually want, and it is the one the wall turns away first.

The second never meets the wall at all. It is the agent living inside a browser, on a person's own machine, in a session that person already logged into. From the wall's side of things, no machine ever arrives. A human passed the challenge, and the software reads what is on the screen afterwards. There is nothing to turn away, because the reader is already inside, holding the door the person opened. No amount of wall touches this one, by design.

The third is the one the wall was built to stop, and it is the one that thinks least like my agent. It has no guardrails. Where my agent stops at a locked door, this one does not treat the door as a thing to respect. It will wear whatever face gets it through, come from wherever it needs to come from, and pay a farm to answer the puzzle meant to prove it is human. The wall is a price, and a well-funded bad actor simply pays it. That is the cruel arithmetic of the wall: it is calibrated almost exactly to stop the polite machine that would have helped you and to wave through the determined one that will not. It clears out everyone smaller and more honest, and leaves the abuse it was built for untouched.

So when I say the machine walks around you, I am describing the best-behaved one. The wall does not even slow the worst. It mostly punishes the visitor in the middle, the real human, who now has a box to tick before reading the thing you published.

A Bad Trade

Set what the wall costs against what it buys.

It blocks your allies. A search crawler is a machine. An archive is a machine. A link preview is a machine. A citation engine that would have named you as the source is a machine. The wall turns all of them away at once.

It taxes your humans. Every "prove you're human" box costs a real reader time on your own site, just to reach what you published.

It doesn't stop the abuse that worried you. Scraping at industrial scale routes around blanket walls. The wall raises the price of entry, which selects for the best-funded scraper and clears out everyone smaller and more honest.

And it doesn't even work. You just watched it fail.

Getting In Was Only Half the Problem

The second failure had nothing to do with the wall. Once my agent was through and reading the article back to me, the numbers had no ground under them. A 62% efficiency gain in four months. Several million saved a year. Precise figures, unnamed companies, no source, no link.

That's worse than it sounds, because of what's reading now. A person skims an unsourced statistic and quietly discounts it. An agent does the opposite. It reads a confident number, finds nothing telling it not to, and passes it on as fact to the next person who asks. Plausible and unverified is exactly the fuel a hallucination burns.

An agent needs to do two things with your page: read it, and check it. The wall stopped the first, the missing sources the second. The agentic enterprise it described couldn't have used the article describing it.

Cooperate, on Both

The move that works is the opposite of the wall, and it answers both bars at once. Stop trying to stop the machine from reading. Tell it instead how to read you well, and what of you it can trust.

Make it readable. Put the content in the markup, instead of hiding the text behind a script a visitor has to be trusted to run. Publish structured data, so the machine reads facts instead of guessing at them. Publish a clean text endpoint and an llms.txt, so an agent has a way in that doesn't fight it. Publish a real sitemap and feed.

Make it trustworthy. Carry metadata that travels with the file: what it is, who made it, where it lives, and how it may be reused. Let your claims carry their source. A figure like "62% in four months" can travel with a signature and a citation the agent checks before it repeats the number, so it quotes an attested fact instead of a line lifted from a paragraph.

The first half is the Machine Experience: metadata that rides with the content and answers the machine's questions before it has to ask. The second half is what Reginald adds on top: a way for a claim to carry proof, so an agent cites what's verified, not what merely sounds confident. Readable, and trustworthy. A page built this way helps the humans too. It ranks and previews better, because both of those are machines as well.

Block Abuse, Not Machines

Cooperation isn't the same as leaving the place wide open. There's real abuse out there: credential stuffing, scraping that runs up your bill, traffic that knocks the site over. Block that. Rate-limit the expensive paths. Watch for the actions, not the species. Charge for bulk access, and publish the API that makes it cheap and accountable.

That's a scalpel. A blanket "no machines" wall is a club, and it lands on the wrong heads: the crawler, the archive, and the person who just wanted to read.

The Machine's Coming in Either Way

The machine's an audience now. It's the agent, the crawler, the validator, and the archive, and it isn't going away because you put up a spinner. It wants two things from you: to read you, and to trust you.

Meet it with a clean copy it can read and a claim it can check, and you get something back: a ranking, a citation, a preview, an accurate answer with your name on it. Make it climb the wall and guess at your numbers instead, and you get nothing, while it reads and repeats you regardless.

It reads you either way. Hand it a copy worth trusting.
