Trustworthy Is Not One Thing

18 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

The last post argued that the fight over AI regulation is really one over the verification layer, the place that decides whether a machine can act on what it reads. The word everyone reaches for at that layer, trustworthy, is doing far too much work, and a machine cannot act on an adjective. It acts on signals, and the moment you ask which signal, trustworthy comes apart into separate questions, each with its own answer.

That is the whole point, not a weakness. A record that asks you to believe it is trustworthy is asking you to take its word. One that exposes its signals lets you check, one question at a time, and decide what to trust and what to hold back.

Who published this, and when

The first question is attestation: who put their name to this, and at what moment. The answer is a signature bound to a declared identity, verifiable by the reader against open standards without a call back to whoever issued it. This is the signal most people mean when they say verified, and it is genuinely load-bearing. A claim with no attributable author is a rumour with good formatting.

But attestation has a hard limit that the marketing word hides. It proves authorship and it proves timing. It proves nothing about whether the claim is correct, current, or shared by anyone else. A confidently signed falsehood is still signed. Knowing whose word you are taking is the beginning of trust, not the end of it.

Is this the version I should act on

The second question is currency: of the things this publisher has said, is this the one I should be acting on now. A record can be perfectly signed and quietly superseded, and a machine that stops at the signature will act on a version the author has already moved past. Price lists, dosages, policies, terms - each of these has a current value and a drawer full of genuine, signed, obsolete ones.

Currency is a different check from attestation, and it fails differently. The signature verifies and the content is still wrong for today, because today is not when it was true. A trust layer that reports only "signed by a known party" has answered a question you did not need to ask and skipped the one you did.

Does the publisher still stand behind it

The third question is freshness, or liveness: does the party who signed this still stand behind it. A signature is a snapshot of an intention, and intentions get withdrawn. An author retracts, a certificate lapses, an organisation folds, a policy is repealed. The record on disk does not change when any of that happens, so the signature keeps verifying long after the meaning behind it has gone.

This is where metadata earns its keep as a live instruction rather than a description. A field such as signatureExpires is not documentation of a date; it tells the reading machine when to stop trusting on its own authority and go looking for a renewal. The record carries its own use-by, and a machine that honours it will decline a claim its author no longer backs. Freshness is the difference between a promise and one someone is still keeping.

Does anyone independent say the same

The last question is the one the other three cannot answer, and it does not belong to the registry at all. Attestation, currency, and freshness are all signals about the publisher: who they are, which version they mean, whether they still hold to it. None of them tells you whether the claim is true beyond the publisher's own say-so. For that you need corroboration, and it comes from the content, not the badge on top of it.

A corroborated claim points to an independent, authoritative source that states the same thing, so a machine may leave the publisher's word and check it against a party with no stake in the answer. A metadata field like refersToExternal is exactly this permission made explicit: it tells the reader you are allowed to verify these sources yourself. Attestation proves who published a claim. It never proves that anyone authoritative agrees, and the gap between those two is where a well-signed, entirely wrong record does its damage. The MX canon calls this corroboration over assertion, and it is what turns a readable, attributable record into one a machine can treat as authoritative.

Why one badge hides the gap

Put the questions together and the reason to keep them apart becomes obvious. Each answers something different. Any one can be present while the others are missing: a signed claim that is out of date, a current one from a party who has since gone silent, a live one that no independent source will confirm. Collapse all of it into a single "trustworthy", or a lone verified: true, and you have thrown away the one thing a reader needed, which is knowing which signal is absent.

That collapse is no accident of tidy design; it is the move a captured verification layer needs. Believe the badge and do not inspect the parts, because the parts are where the missing signal shows. An earlier post in this series drew the line between a badge you are asked to believe and a check you can run for yourself. The signals are that line made concrete. A record built to be trusted exposes each signal separately; one made to be believed hides them behind a word.

The test

The buyer's test gains one more question, and it is the one to ask any vendor selling trust. When you say this record is trustworthy, which signal do you mean, and do I check it myself or take your word for it. If the answer folds attestation, currency, freshness, and corroboration into a single badge you are asked to accept, you are being sold belief. If the record hands you each signal to verify on your own, you are being sold trust. The words look identical on the label. The difference is whether you can still tell, after the sale, which parts are missing.
