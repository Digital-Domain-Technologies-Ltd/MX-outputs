Who Decided Your Name Was Too Long?

17 November 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

In November 2026 a post in my LinkedIn feed stopped me scrolling. A trainer in technical communication had just cancelled a new payment card. The bank had printed it with a shortened form of their name that none of their accounts used, and refused to correct it: the software, staff said, couldn't handle anything that long. The customer explained that a card in someone else's name was useless to them. The suggestion that came back was to change their name, because as far as the computer was concerned, the fix wasn't going to happen.

The post ended with a question: "Have I been erased by a machine?"

My first reaction was the obvious one. Surely a bot decided this.

Nobody in the Conversation Made the Decision

Look at who took part. The customer, who knows their own name. A member of staff, who can see the problem and can't change the outcome. And a rule, written years ago by someone who never met either of them, that says a name field holds so many characters and no more.

The rule is the only party with any power, and the only one absent from the call. The member of staff is reading out a verdict rather than deciding anything. Asking a customer to change their legal name is what that sounds like when nobody has the authority to say "that's wrong, we'll fix it".

That's the dilemma. We talk about machines making decisions as though it's something coming soon, with agents and models and autonomy. It isn't. Machines have been deciding things about people for decades, through field lengths, character sets and validation rules. The decision gets made at design time, out of sight, and a human gets sent to deliver it.

The Limit Is Real, the Silence Is the Fault

I don't want to pretend the constraint is invented. Payment cards have physical and technical limits. The international standard for the magnetic stripe gives the cardholder name between 2 and 26 characters (ISO/IEC 7813, as described on Wikipedia). A card can only print so much. Somebody has to decide what happens when a name doesn't fit.

The trouble is how that decision gets made: silently. The software took the name it was given, applied a rule, and stored something else. No record says "the customer entered X, we stored Y, because of rule Z". Nothing warned the customer before the card was made. And once the shortened form was stored, it became the bank's truth about who they are. Every system downstream reads "C-Jean" and believes it, because nothing in the record says it was a guess.

Programmers have been warned about this for a long time. Patrick McKenzie's list of falsehoods programmers believe about names includes the line "People's names fit within a certain defined amount of space" (McKenzie, 2010). The W3C's guidance on personal names says to make input fields big enough for long names, and to avoid limiting the field size for names in the database (Ishida, n.d.). Names in some cultures run much longer than the designer's own. A limit that seems generous in one country is a wall in another.

What Declaring It Would Change

This is where I come at it from Machine Experience. MX is about a file or a record carrying what the next reader needs to check it: where it came from, what was done to it, and its intended use. A name that has been shortened by a rule is a record that has been changed, and it should say so.

Three things would have turned this from an erasure into a conversation:

  • Declare the limit where people meet it. If a field holds 26 characters, the form says so, and the person chooses how their name is shortened. They do the choosing, not the code.
  • Keep what was entered beside what was stored. The legal name stays the record. The card form is a derived value, marked as derived, so nobody downstream mistakes it for the original.
  • Record the rule that made the change. "Shortened to fit the card, by this rule, on this date" gives a member of staff something to act on, and a reviewer something to fix.

None of that needs new technology. It needs the system to admit that it made a decision.

Machines Downstream Will Believe It Too

There's a reason this matters more now than it did when the rule was written. More of what reads our records is a machine. An agent checking that a payee name matches an account, a model drafting a letter, a matching service comparing one database with another: all of them trust the stored value as written. A silent rewrite made for a card printer turns into a mismatch flag, a failed check or a letter addressed to someone who doesn't exist.

The person at the bank couldn't fix the software. They could have said "our system can't do this, and that's our failure, not your name". That's the human part. The machine part is a system honest enough that the person at the counter can see what happened and has the authority to put it right.

So, was the customer erased by a machine? Not quite. They were erased by a decision that nobody owns, carried out by a machine that never had to explain itself. The fix is to make it explain itself.
