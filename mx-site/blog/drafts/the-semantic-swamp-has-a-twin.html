The Semantic Swamp Has a Twin

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

A post by Juan Sequeda went round my feed this week, and it names a failure mode so cleanly that I want to borrow the phrase before I add the half it leaves out. He opens with a warning most teams should read twice:

Everyone is building semantics for AI agents. I wonder if most are going to regret it.

His worry is not that semantics are the wrong idea. It is that almost everyone is building them the quick way, one agent at a time, for the use case in front of them:

most organizations building semantics today are doing it only for the AI use case. They want it fast. Quick and dirty. Let the model figure it out. Ship it.

And he is exactly right about where that lands you.

The swamp he describes

Build meaning per agent and per project, and the meanings drift apart. Two agents answer the same question two slightly different ways, because each carries its own private notion of what a customer is, what active means, what counts as a complaint. Sequeda's forecast:

You're going to end up with multiple agents, each carrying their own version of semantics, each giving you slightly different answers to the same question, brittle systems.

He points out that we have lived this before. It is the data-dashboard sprawl and the data swamp, returned in new clothes:

Just with a new layer on top. The Semantic Swamp.

His cure is the one any serious data person would give. Stop building the meaning per consumer. Build it once, properly, and map it across the systems you already have:

Do the semantic work once. Define it properly. Map it across your systems. Then every new consumer, agents included, just plugs into that foundation instead of building its own version of reality.

I agree with all of it, and I want to be plain about that before I add anything, because what I am going to add does not contradict a single word. The discipline he is describing is real, it is hard, and the organisations that skip it will pay for it. His closing line is the part I would pin to a wall:

This is not an AI problem. It's a leadership problem. And it has a solution if you're willing to do the work.

The twin problem his post does not reach

Here is the half the post does not get to. A central semantic foundation, an ontology or a shared model mapped across your systems, governs meaning while the content is sitting inside those systems. It is the warehouse, the catalogue, the graph that every internal consumer queries. That is the right place for the data layer to agree with itself.

But content does not stay inside the systems that produced it. A report is exported to a PDF and emailed to a partner. A page is fetched once by an agent and cached somewhere you will never see. A spreadsheet lands on a contractor's laptop. A document is pooled into a third-party platform and re-served as a derivative. The moment any of that happens, the foundation stays behind. The ontology that told your systems what active customer means is still in the warehouse; the artefact now in the agent's hands carries none of it. The machine reading the file is back to guessing, which is the exact condition the central foundation was built to end.

So you can do the semantic work once, define it perfectly, map it across every system you own, and a machine that meets your content one hop outside your estate still gets nothing. Define once solves the swamp inside the building. It does nothing for the meaning that walks out the door, and in an agentic world a great deal of your content walks out the door by design.

Define once is necessary. Make it travel is the other half

This is the whole of what MX is for. MX is metadata that records a file's provenance, the context that produced it, and how it is meant to be used, and it travels with the file. Where the central foundation keeps the meaning in one place and asks every consumer to come to it, MX attaches the meaning to the artefact so it survives the trip. The named principle is Metadata Everywhere: the file says what it is wherever it ends up, with no system standing behind it to be consulted.

The two are not rivals. They compose. You do the semantic work once, exactly as Sequeda says, and then you write the result into the things you publish, so the agreed meaning rides along when the artefact leaves. The foundation governs the data at rest inside your systems; MX governs the meaning in flight, once the content is somewhere your query layer cannot reach. One without the other leaves a gap: a foundation with nothing travelling is meaning that stops at your firewall, and metadata travelling with no foundation behind it is a private notion attached to a file. Both, and the meaning is consistent at the source and still legible at the destination.

There is a second property the file has to carry once it travels, and it is the one a central ontology never had to think about. Inside your systems, trust is assumed; the data is yours. Outside them, a machine reading your file has no reason to believe it is genuine, current, or published by who it claims. So MX makes the content machine-readable, and REGINALD, the registry that runs on the same open standard, makes it machine-trustworthy: a dated, signed attestation a reading agent can verify for itself. Readable, then trustworthy. The first lets the agent understand the file it found; the second lets it know whether to believe it. A central foundation gives neither once the artefact is gone, because it was never designed to leave.

Not a strategy that fights the ontology

I want to head off the obvious misread. None of this argues against the semantic foundation. MX is not a replacement for doing the modelling work, and a cog full of travelling metadata is worth less if the meaning it carries was invented per file rather than agreed once. MX defers to the foundation for what the foundation does well, the same way MX defers to Schema.org and Dublin Core rather than reinventing them. Do the semantic work once, then let MX carry the result to wherever the content goes. The leadership decision Sequeda names has a clause his post does not write down: it is not only do the semantic work once, it is also keep the meaning attached when the work leaves the building.

Get both right and the swamp drains in both directions. Inside your systems, every consumer plugs into one foundation instead of inventing its own reality, which is his point and a good one. Outside your systems, every file an agent meets still carries the meaning and the proof, which is the half that decides whether any of the upstream work survives contact with the agentic web. The semantic swamp has a twin, and you cannot drain one and leave the other full.
