Disposable Agents Need Durable Documents

30 September 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

On 29 September 2026, at its DevDay in San Francisco, OpenAI launched dots. A dot is an always-on agent that runs on GPT-6 Astra, has "their own cloud computer" in OpenAI's words, and keeps working between conversations (Schoon, 2026). You reach it through ChatGPT, Slack or Teams. It does background research through read-only connections to your tools, and you set rules for when it may act alone and when it has to ask.

The reaction on my LinkedIn feed split in two. Benedict Evans was unimpressed: "This is a rather confused product launch", with consumer branding, startup engineering tasks and wedding planning mixed together. Jamie Smith looked past the launch to where it leads: "an agent for your boiler, another for your car, another for the weekend trip". He sketched a line of computing eras that ends at the agent, and closed with a prediction: "And Agents will be disposable shortly."

Both can be right.

The first product is muddled, and the direction is clear. Agents are getting cheap enough to make one for a single job and throw it away when the job is done.

What a Disposable Agent Doesn't Know

Think about the boiler agent. It didn't book the last service. It doesn't know the engineer's report was superseded, that the warranty terms changed in the spring, or that the manufacturer's advice for your model was withdrawn. It has no history with your boiler at all. It was made this morning, and it'll be gone by Friday.

So the agent starts cold every time it opens a document. The agent fetches the service record, the warranty document and the manufacturer's page, and the only memory it has is what those documents say about themselves. If they don't say who owns them, whether they're current and when they stop being valid, the agent has to fill the gap. A machine filling a gap is a guess.

Scale that up. An always-on dot and a hundred single-task agents behind it all read the same documents repeatedly, from a standing start each time. The cheaper agents get, the more cold reads each document receives, and the more guesses are made about it.

The Guess Is Measurable

I've tested this directly with a small decider, the kind of narrow model that makes one cheap decision and hands hard cases up the chain (the measured runs are written up here). Handed a bare document, it guessed, and it invented an owner nine times out of ten. Handed the same document with its owner and currency declared as labelled fields, it stopped guessing and read the answer.

The model didn't get smarter between the two runs. The document told it more. That's the point for a world of disposable agents: you can't train judgement into an agent that exists for an afternoon, but you can put the facts it needs into the thing it reads.

Agents Come and Go, the Document Stays

This changes where the lasting value sits. If agents are disposable, the market for any one of them is fragile; the next launch replaces it. What lasts is the material every agent reads: the service record, the warranty terms, the price list, the hotel's cancellation policy. Those documents outlive every agent that reads them.

MX is to machines what UX is to users. UX asks whether a person can understand a page and act on it. MX asks the same of a machine, and a cold, disposable agent is the hardest of those readers: no history, no context and no time to work it out. A document that works for that agent works for everyone else too.

What to Do Now

Start with the documents that carry money or liability, such as policies, prices and terms. In each one, declare three facts as labelled fields rather than prose:

  • Who owns it, a named person or team who stands behind it.
  • Whether it's current, a status an agent can read without inferring it from the page design.
  • When it stops being valid, a date after which no agent should trust it.

Your own agents stop guessing about your documents. The agents your customers send, whichever company made them, read the same declared facts. And you hold a dated record of what you published.

I've argued before that GEO is the safe purchase of 2026, and that humans browse while agents fetch once. Disposable agents sharpen both points. Nobody will remember which agent booked the boiler service. Somebody will remember whether it booked the right one.
