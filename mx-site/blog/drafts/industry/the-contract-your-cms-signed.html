The Contract Your CMS Signed

18 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

There's a contract you never read. Your content management system signed it on your behalf the day you bought in, and it's been quietly in force ever since.

Here's the whole of it, in one line: we'll make your content visible to your audience in a way that benefits you.

That's the promise every CMS has ever sold. Be found. Reach your people. Publish at scale without calling a developer for every change. You paid for that, and you're still paying for it.

The promise was always about the audience

Look hard at the wording. The term is "your audience". For thirty years that meant humans with eyes, so the loophole sat there unnoticed. Your readers had eyes, your CMS rendered pages for them, and the promise was kept.

Then your audience changed.

It now includes machines. The shopping agent comparing your prices. The assistant answering a customer's question about your opening hours. The crawler deciding whether you're worth citing. A growing share of the people who "see" your content never lay eyes on it. A machine reads it for them, and tells them what it found.

The contract was never reopened to add them. It didn't have to be. The wording always said "your audience", and your market decides who belongs there, whatever the brochure assumed at signup. So the promise stayed exactly as sold. It simply stopped being kept.

You won't see it happen

Here's the cruel part. The breach is invisible from where you stand.

You open your own site in a browser and it looks fine. The images load, the menu works, the checkout button sits right where it should. So you keep trusting the platform that serves it, because every signal you can see says the thing is working.

The machine sees something else. It reads the page your server actually sent, before any of the clever JavaScript runs, and finds a price locked inside a script it can't execute, a stock status that only exists after a click, a "Confirm" button that's a styled div with no meaning attached. It can't finish what it came to do, so it does the rational thing and moves on, quietly, to a competitor whose page it could read.

No error. No bounce you can explain. No line in your analytics that reads "an agent gave up here". Just revenue that never arrives, from customers you didn't know were sent.

How you find out

You find out late, and it lands hard. It usually shows up as one of these:

  • A customer tells you their assistant quoted the wrong price, or said you were closed, or claimed you don't ship to their country. Every ambiguity in your markup became a confident wrong answer, multiplied across everyone who trusted it.
  • A competitor keeps getting named by the AI tools your buyers use, and you don't, and nobody can tell you why.
  • Your AI-referred revenue trails the businesses you out-rank on Google, and the gap widens every quarter.
  • Someone finally runs an audit, puts the whole gap on a single page, and you can't un-see it.

Whichever way it arrives, the feeling's the same. The platform you trusted hadn't been keeping the promise it sold you, and that had been true for a while.

That's the moment the trust goes. Not slowly. All at once.

Then comes the part that should make you angry

Your first instinct is the right one: this is the vendor's job to fix. They control the output layer; you don't. You handed them the templates and the markup the day you signed up, which was the entire point of buying a CMS. You can't hand-edit what the platform emits. The duty to make your content legible to the new audience sits with whoever holds the controls, and that isn't you.

So you go to your vendor and ask them to fix it.

Here's the likely response, and you should brace for it.

They'll sell it back to you.

The fix won't arrive as an apology and a patch. It'll arrive as a new tier. "AI-Ready." "Agent Optimisation." "Machine Discovery, now available on the Enterprise plan." A premium add-on, a fresh line item, to deliver the very thing the original contract already promised. They'll charge you twice to keep one promise.

Watch the language when it lands. "Listening to customer feedback." "Expanding your reach to new channels." "Unlocking the agent economy." Translated: we let the contract lapse, we noticed that you noticed, and we've priced the repair.

Don't pay twice.

What readable actually costs

The awkward truth, for the vendor, is that keeping the contract isn't a moonshot. Machine-readable output is a handful of unglamorous, well-understood moves. Put the price and the stock status in the HTML the server sends, not in a script that has to run first. Use elements that mean what they say. Carry the structured data that names what the page is. Let the meaning survive the fetch.

That's plumbing, not magic. It belongs in the product you bought, at the layer the vendor already runs. A platform that treats it as a premium upsell is telling you, plainly, how it reads the contract: as something it owes you only if you pay again.

What to do about it

You've got more leverage than the upsell email assumes.

First, look. You can see the gap yourself before anyone sells you a report on it. Ask for your page the way a machine gets it: fetch the raw HTML, before JavaScript, and check whether your price, your stock, and your headline facts are really in there. If they vanish, so does your machine audience. A proper machine-readiness audit goes further and names the platform and CMS behind the gap, then benchmarks you against sites on the same stack, so you can tell a setting you could change from a ceiling your vendor built.

Second, reframe the ask. Readable output isn't a feature. It's the contract. Treat it as table stakes you're already owed, not a future enhancement you're lucky to be offered. Make your vendor put in writing what an agent sees when it reads the pages they emit for you.

Third, remember whose duty this is. If a platform can't make your content legible to the audience that now mediates your market, and will only do it for a surcharge, you've learned something no feature list will tell you. A CMS that's quietly lost your trust is already a transitional technology, whatever its release notes say.

And you don't have to win the argument with your platform to get there. The work is well understood, and a capable agency or build partner can deliver readable output on top of whatever you run today, or move you to something that ships it by default. That, in practice, is what tends to happen. The client doesn't out-argue the vendor. They find someone who'll keep the contract, and they take their content with them when they go.

The contract was always there. You've just read it for the first time. So has your vendor, watching to see whether you'll act on it before someone else offers to.
