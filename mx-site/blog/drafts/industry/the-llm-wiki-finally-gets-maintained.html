The LLM Wiki Finally Gets Maintained

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Every team I have worked with has a dead wiki. Somewhere there's a Confluence space, a Notion workspace, or a folder of Obsidian notes that was accurate the week it was set up and has been quietly lying ever since. The onboarding page still names a manager who left two reorganisations ago. The architecture diagram describes a system that was rewritten last spring. Nobody trusts it, so nobody reads it, so nobody fixes it, and the rot compounds.

I used to think this was a tooling problem. Pick a better wiki, get better adoption, write a better template. It isn't a tooling problem. It's a maintenance problem, and that is the thing humans are worst at sustaining.

Why wikis die

A wiki is true on day one because someone just wrote it. It drifts by month three because the world moved and the page didn't. By month twelve it's actively misleading, and the cruel part is that it fails silently. There's no error, no broken build, no red mark. The page just sits there being wrong, and the only signal you get is a new hire confidently acting on it because the documentation told them to.

The reason is boring and human. Keeping a knowledge base current means updating the cross-references nobody can see, the ones three documents away from the change you actually made. You fix the deployment runbook, and you don't remember that the onboarding page, the architecture note, and the incident playbook all referred to the old process. Doing that consistently, for every change, forever, is tedious. People stop. They don't decide to stop; they just have a deadline, and the wiki is never it.

So the knowledge decays, not because anyone chose the wrong format, but because the bookkeeping never got done. Every wiki that ever died, died of this.

The thing that changed

Andrej Karpathy put his finger on what is different now. The value of an "LLM wiki" is not that a model writes prettier prose. It is that the wiki finally stays current, because an agent does the upkeep a person abandons.

An agent doesn't get bored. It doesn't have a deadline that matters more than the documentation. When a fact changes, it doesn't forget the three documents downstream that referred to it; it updates the cross-reference, and it'll touch a dozen files in a single pass to keep the whole set consistent. Then it does the same thing tomorrow, without sulking, without needing to be reminded, without the slow erosion of goodwill that kills every documentation initiative run by humans alone.

That is the actual unlock. The hard part of a knowledge base was never the writing. It was the maintaining. For the first time, the maintaining has an answer that does not depend on human diligence holding out forever, because the thing doing the maintaining does not tire.

What OKF actually contributes

This is how I'd read Google's Open Knowledge Format. I wrote a few days ago that OKF confirms the argument I have made for two years, that the answer to machine-readable knowledge is a format, not a platform. That's true and it matters. But the quieter point is why this format, of all the ones that came before it, has a real chance of being kept current.

OKF is a directory of markdown files with YAML frontmatter, linked into a graph. It's thin. It's minimally opinionated; it doesn't dictate what you write, only how it's structured. And thinness is exactly the property that makes it maintainable by an agent. A format that needs an expert to edit it rots the moment that person is busy. One that's just legible text with a little structure on top can be read and rewritten by a human and an agent alike, with no translation layer between them. The structure is light enough for an agent to keep consistent deterministically, and the meaning it carries makes that consistency worth having.

That's the difference between a knowledge format that gets adopted and one that gets admired and abandoned. The Semantic Web was rich enough; what it never was, for ordinary people or their agents, was thin. OKF errs the other way, and that's the right error.

I find this persuasive because I have watched it work. Give an agent a structure thin and legible enough to maintain, with scripts checking what the prose claims against what the files actually say, and it'll keep the thing current in a way no team ever sustained by hand. The knowledge base stops being a museum of how things used to be and becomes a live description of how they are. That isn't a hypothetical; it's the difference between documentation people trust and the kind they route around.

The half that does not maintain itself

Here is the catch, and it is the same half I keep coming back to.

The more autonomously an agent maintains your knowledge, the more you need to know whether to trust what it wrote. A wiki nobody updated was at least frozen; you knew it was stale and you treated it with suspicion. A wiki an agent rewrites unattended is live, which is more useful and more dangerous at once. When something touches a dozen files in a single pass while you're asleep, the question that matters is no longer whether it's current but who changed it, and on what authority. An unattested edit at machine speed is just drift with better production values.

Reading is not the same as trusting, and an agent maintaining a knowledge base is doing a great deal of reading and writing on your behalf. The maintenance is the work an agent can finally do. The trust is the work the format leaves untouched: a signed, dated record of who published a fact and whether a newer version has replaced it, that a machine can check for itself rather than take on faith. Structure describes the change; it does not validate it. That validation is what a cog carries and what REGINALD records, and it is the layer that turns an agent-maintained knowledge base from a convenience into something you can put in front of a regulator, a partner, or an acquirer.

Where this leaves us

The maintenance problem killed every wiki you ever abandoned. It finally has an answer, and it isn't a better tool for humans to ignore. It's a format thin enough that an agent can keep it current, which is the quiet reason OKF, and the machine-readable content built on top of it, will get used where the others got shelved.

Let the agent do the bookkeeping. Then sign the books, so the next machine to read them knows it can believe what it finds.
