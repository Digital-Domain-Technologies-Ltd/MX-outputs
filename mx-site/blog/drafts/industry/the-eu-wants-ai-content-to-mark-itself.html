The EU just told AI content to mark itself. We added the field.

13 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

Today the European Commission published the final Code of Practice on marking and labelling of AI-generated content. It is voluntary, it was drafted by six independent experts with input from more than 180 stakeholders, and it sets out practical steps to help providers and deployers of generative AI meet the transparency obligations of the EU AI Act, which apply from 2 August 2026.

Note: This page describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

I want to pull out one line, because it is the whole reason I am writing this. From that date, AI-generated or AI-manipulated content has to be "marked in a machine-readable way" so it "can be detected as artificially generated or manipulated". Deepfakes get labelled. Machine-written text on matters of public interest gets labelled when no human reviewed it. And people get told when they are talking to a chatbot rather than a person.

Read that again with an engineer's eye. The regulator is not asking for a banner a human can see. It is asking for a marker a machine can read, attached to the file, before a human is in the loop. That is the argument I have been making for two years, and it just arrived in a Commission instrument with a date on it.

This is the machine-first argument, in the regulator's mouth

The case for Machine Experience has always been that a file should be able to tell a machine what it is, without the page that produced it, without a human translating, without anyone guessing. Who made this. When. Under what authority. Whether a person reviewed it. Most content on the web cannot answer any of that, which is exactly why agents hallucinate about it and auditors end up taking the operator's word.

The Code of Practice picks the sharpest version of that question and makes it a transparency duty: was this made by a machine, and can a machine tell? If your answer lives only in a human-readable disclaimer, or nowhere at all, you do not have a machine-readable marking. You have a hope.

What we changed this week

MX already had a field for how content was created. provenanceOrigin could say human-directed, ai-assisted, or automated-fact-check. What it could not say cleanly was the case the Code of Practice cares about most: a machine made this, on its own, with no real human authorship behind it.

So we extended the field with the two values the regulation itself names:

  • ai-generated for content a machine created with no substantive human authorship or editorial control.
  • ai-manipulated for human-origin content a machine materially altered, which is the deepfake and synthetic-edit case.

A file declares it in its own carrier and the declaration travels with the file. For a document that is the frontmatter. For an image it is the XMP packet. For an Office file it is the custom properties. And because a marking obligation is useless if it stops at text, we wired the same declaration into binary assets: a vendor can now mark an image, a diagram, or a deck as machine-made with one command, and the mark rides inside the file.

node scripts/decorate-asset.cjs hero.png --field mx.provenanceOrigin=ai-generated

After that, a validator that opens hero.png anywhere it travels reads provenanceOrigin: ai-generated straight out of the file. No surrounding page required. That is what "machine-readable" is supposed to mean.

The honest part, because it matters

Here is where I have to be careful, because this is the kind of moment where a vendor oversells and gets caught.

Declaring that a machine authored a file is not the same as watermarking the file so a detector can prove it is synthetic. The first is provenance. The second is content authenticity, and that is the job of a marking standard like C2PA Content Credentials or a watermark like SynthID. MX is not a watermark, and I am not going to tell you it satisfies Article 50 on its own. It does not.

What MX gives the obligation is the layer the regulation quietly assumes already exists: a machine-readable declaration of who, or what, authored the content, carried in the file, sitting alongside whatever watermark the artefact also bears. The watermark proves it is synthetic. MX records the authorship and the provenance chain around it. Carry both and you have answered the whole question. Claim one is the other and you will be corrected on the first inquiry.

That distinction is the difference between selling "queryable, verifiable evidence", which is honest, and selling "compliance in a box", which collapses the moment a regulator looks. MX and REGINALD are the first kind. A voluntary code that lets a signer demonstrate compliance is evidence you can produce on request, not a certificate you can hide behind. Compliance stays the organisation's legal duty. What we make is the documentation that duty requires, machine-readable and travelling with the file.

The web is filling up with content no one made by hand. The EU just said that content should be able to say so, in a form a machine can read. As of this week, MX has the field, and your files and your assets can carry it.
