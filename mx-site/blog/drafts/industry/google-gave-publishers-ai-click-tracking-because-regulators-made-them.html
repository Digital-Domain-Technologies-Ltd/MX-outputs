Google Must Give Publishers AI Click Tracking Because Regulators Made It

27 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

The UK's Competition and Markets Authority issued Google a notice on 3 June 2026. Its terms: give publishers control over how their content is used in AI search features, and show them what happens to it there (GOV.UK). Google has until 3 December 2026 to comply, with three more months for a page-level control (CMA, The publisher conduct requirement).

Note: This page describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, type of business, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

This is the most concrete proof yet of the argument this series has been making. Platforms don't voluntarily solve the transparency problem. They solve it when regulators make them. And even when they do, the tracking you get is the tracking they decide to give you, in the format they choose, measured by the metrics they define.

That isn't the same as owning your own provenance.

What Google Has to Provide

Until now, clicks from AI Overviews were blended into the general search data, hidden in the same number as organic web results. The conduct requirement obliges Google to provide transparency metrics for its search AI features, aggregated across features such as AI Overviews and AI Mode (CMA):

  • Impressions: How many times the AI showed your content in the answer
  • Clicks: How many times someone clicked through from the AI answer to your site
  • Click-through rate: What percentage of impressions converted to clicks

It also requires referral data, so publishers can judge the quality of that traffic. And it requires controls to withhold content from training Google's generative AI models and from grounding answers in AI Overviews, AI Mode and Gemini, without being downranked in the rest of Search. The grounding controls inside Search have to work at directory and page level.

The CMA expects Google to start sooner than the deadline, and notes that Google is already rolling out parts of the control and elements of the fuller transparency. Until the full set arrives, publishers are working with whatever Google has shipped so far, not with everything the requirement promises.

This is a real win. Publishers have had little visibility into what happens to their traffic in AI search. That traffic loss has been happening in silence. The requirement is what makes it visible.

The Regulator Made This Happen

Google didn't volunteer this. The CMA ordered it. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, the Authority designated Google as having strategic market status in general search in October 2025. In June 2026 it imposed its first conduct requirement under the regime: give publishers what Google had always kept hidden.

The timeline matters. The requirement gives Google six months, to 3 December 2026, with three more for page-level controls. Whatever Google ships before then, it ships with a deadline behind it.

This is how platform transparency actually works. Not through appeals to fairness. Not through industry best practice. Through law. A regulator names the problem, sets a deadline, and the platform calculates that compliance is cheaper than resistance.

What This Means for Your Strategy

Take the data. Use it. Understand what's happening to your traffic in AI search. If the zero-click rate is killing you, you have options: rewrite your content to be less answer-shaped, withhold it from AI, or accept the traffic loss as the cost of being cited in AI answers.

But don't mistake platform-provided metrics for owned data.

Google shows you what Google decides to show you, in the format Google chooses, on the schedule Google maintains. The tracking could change next quarter. The metrics could be redefined. The feature could be withdrawn if the regulator's attention moves elsewhere. You have no guarantee that this transparency will persist, because you don't own it. Google can revoke it. And if Google's business incentives shift - if the ad revenue from AI Overview attribution becomes more important than publisher goodwill - the metrics could quietly narrow.

This is the difference between platform transparency and owned provenance. Platform transparency is what regulators can force. Owned provenance is what you build on open standards, held by neutral bodies, verified on your own domain.

The Larger Pattern

This is the pattern that repeats across every platform. Platforms won't solve transparency problems voluntarily because doing so undermines their business. Regulators solve them by force. And even when a regulator forces a fix, the platform still owns it: measured by its rules, revocable at its discretion.

The CMA is forcing Google to show publishers AI search metrics. Portability of that data, a standard format that would let publishers aggregate it across platforms, and open access so they could build their own analysis on top all fell outside what the CMA required. The regulator solved the immediate problem - visibility - but not the structural one - ownership.

This is why you need both layers. Use Google's tracking to understand what's happening in Google Search. Then build your own layer - structured, attested, portable - that survives independent of what Google decides to track, what OpenAI decides to measure, what any platform decides to provide.

Where Machines Come In

There's one more dimension to this. Two publisher-owned feeds already exist beside any platform's dashboard: RSS for content discovery and llms.txt for agent access. Both are machine-readable, both are publisher-owned, both are hosted on your domain where you control them.

RSS never went away. It was sidelined by social platforms and search dominance, but it never stopped being the most reliable way to tell machines "this is my new content, come read it." Now it's being joined by llms.txt, a simple text file that tells AI agents what you publish and how to find it.

Neither feed depends on a platform to exist. Neither can be withdrawn by a vendor. Neither changes at the platform's whim. A machine that reads your RSS or your llms.txt is reading content you authored and published on your own domain. If that machine is an AI agent, it's seeing the same thing a human reader sees, with no rewrite for machines and no intermediary deciding what gets shown.

This is the provenance layer platforms can't provide, because providing it means admitting machines don't need the platform to find and read your work.

What To Do Now

Three actions, in order:

First: Use Google's AI search reporting in Search Console as it arrives. Understand what's happening to your AI search traffic. This is free data from a regulator-forced transparency win. Take it.

Second: Create an RSS feed for your blog or content stream if you don't already have one. It's the standard that works everywhere - every reader, every machine, every platform that respects the open web. Put it in your sitemap. Link to it from your home page. Machines that arrive at your domain should be able to find your latest content without waiting for Google to crawl it.

Third: Add llms.txt. It's a simple text file that lists what you publish and where machines can find it. Point to your RSS feed from there. Add a note on your agent access policy - what machines may do with your content. This is the layer that survives independent of which platform is dominant and which model is trendy this quarter.

Do all three and you have covered the bases: you see what platforms are doing with your content, you help machines find your work directly, and you own the metadata that describes it. Platform transparency is a win. Owned provenance is survival.

The CMA is forcing Google to give publishers visibility into AI search. That's real progress. It's also the clearest proof that platforms only solve transparency problems when regulators make them. Build the layer you own. Don't wait for the next regulator's order to give you what you should already have.
