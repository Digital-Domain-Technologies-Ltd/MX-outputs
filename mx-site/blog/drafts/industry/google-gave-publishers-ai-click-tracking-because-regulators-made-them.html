Google Gave Publishers AI Click Tracking Because Regulators Made Them

27 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

On 3 June 2026 the UK's Competition and Markets Authority issued Google a conduct requirement: show publishers exactly what their content does inside AI search features. Google resisted. Publishers complained. The regulator was unmoved. Last week, Google added the tracking to Search Console.

This is the most concrete proof yet of the argument this series has been making. Platforms do not voluntarily solve the transparency problem. They solve it when regulators make them. And even when they do, the tracking you get is the tracking they decide to give you, in the format they choose, measured by the metrics they define.

That is not the same as owning your own provenance.

What Google Just Added

Search Console now shows disaggregated AI search metrics. Before, AI Overview clicks were blended into the general search data, hidden in the same number as organic web results. Now publishers can see:

  • Impressions: How many times the AI showed your content in the answer
  • Clicks: How many times someone clicked through from the AI answer to your site
  • Click-through rate: What percentage of impressions converted to clicks

The data is per-URL and per-directory, so you can see what happened to a specific article or a whole section. You can also control it: publishers can now tell Google to withhold content from AI Overviews and AI Mode if they want.

This is a real win. Before this week, publishers had zero visibility into what was happening to their traffic in AI search. The zero-click rate for AI answers ranges from 20% to 50% depending on category - health, local news, and finance are hit hardest. That traffic loss was happening in silence. Now it is visible.

The Regulator Made This Happen

Google did not volunteer this. The CMA ordered it. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, the Authority designated Google as a strategically important digital service in October 2025. In June 2026, it issued the first conduct requirement: give publishers what Google had always kept hidden.

The timeline matters. The requirement gave Google nine months to implement. Google dragged. Pressure mounted. At some point last week, Google decided it was cheaper to add the feature than to fight the regulator further. The tracking appeared.

This is how platform transparency actually works. Not through appeals to fairness. Not through industry best practice. Through law. A regulator names the problem, sets a deadline, and the platform calculates that compliance is cheaper than resistance.

What This Means for Your Strategy

Take the data. Use it. Understand what is happening to your traffic in AI search. If the zero-click rate is killing you, you have options: rewrite your content to be less answer-shaped, withhold it from AI, or accept the traffic loss as the cost of being cited in AI answers.

But do not mistake platform-provided metrics for owned data.

Google shows you what Google decides to show you, in the format Google chooses, on the schedule Google maintains. The tracking could change next quarter. The metrics could be redefined. The feature could be withdrawn if the regulator's attention moves elsewhere. You have no guarantee that this transparency will persist, because you do not own it. Google can revoke it. And if Google's business incentives shift - if the ad revenue from AI Overview attribution becomes more important than publisher goodwill - the metrics could quietly narrow.

This is the difference between platform transparency and owned provenance. Platform transparency is what regulators can force. Owned provenance is what you build on open standards, held by neutral bodies, verified on your own domain.

The Larger Pattern

This is the pattern that repeats across every platform. Platforms will not solve transparency problems voluntarily because transparency undermines their business. Regulators solve them by force. And even when a regulator forces a solution, it is still the platform's solution, measured by the platform's rules, revocable at the platform's discretion.

The CMA forced Google to show publishers AI search metrics. It did not force Google to make that data portable. It did not force Google to standardise the format so publishers could aggregate it across platforms. It did not force Google to open the data so publishers could build their own analysis on top of it. The regulator solved the immediate problem - visibility - but not the structural one - ownership.

This is why you need both layers. Use Google's tracking to understand what is happening in Google Search. Then build your own layer - structured, attested, portable - that survives independent of what Google decides to track, what OpenAI decides to measure, what any platform decides to provide.

Where Machines Come In

There is one more dimension to this. The very same day Google added the tracking, the web indexing standards community was finishing work on two companion feeds: RSS for content discovery and llms.txt for agent access. Both are machine-readable, both are publisher-owned, both sit on your domain where you control them.

RSS never went away. It was sidelined by social platforms and search dominance, but it never stopped being the most reliable way to tell machines "this is my new content, come read it." Now it is being joined by llms.txt, a simple text file that tells AI agents what you publish and how to find it.

Neither feed depends on a platform to exist. Neither can be withdrawn by a vendor. Neither changes at the platform's whim. A machine that reads your RSS or your llms.txt is reading content you authored and published on your own domain. If that machine is an AI agent, it is seeing the same thing a human reader sees. No special format. No machine-flavoured rewrite. No intermediary deciding what gets shown.

This is the provenance layer platforms cannot provide, because providing it means admitting machines do not need the platform to find and read your work.

What To Do Now

Three actions, in order:

First: Use Google's new tracking. Set it up in Search Console. Understand what is happening to your AI search traffic. This is free data from a regulator-forced transparency win. Take it.

Second: Create an RSS feed for your blog or content stream if you do not already have one. It is the standard that works everywhere - every reader, every machine, every platform that respects the open web. Put it in your sitemap. Link to it from your home page. Machines that arrive at your domain should be able to find your latest content without waiting for Google to crawl it.

Third: Add llms.txt. It is a simple text file that lists what you publish and where machines can find it. Point to your RSS feed from there. Add a note on your agent access policy - what machines may do with your content. This is the layer that survives independent of which platform is dominant and which model is trendy this quarter.

Do all three and you have covered the bases: you see what platforms are doing with your content, you help machines find your work directly, and you own the metadata that describes it. Platform transparency is a win. Owned provenance is survival.

The CMA forced Google to give publishers visibility into AI search. That is real progress. It is also the clearest proof that platforms only solve transparency problems when regulators make them. Build the layer you own. Do not wait for the next regulator's order to give you what you should already have.
