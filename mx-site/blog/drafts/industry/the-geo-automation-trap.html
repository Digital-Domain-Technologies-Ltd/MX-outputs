The GEO Automation Trap

24 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

There is a category of tool that promises to fix your AI visibility problem automatically.

You install it. It crawls your site. It identifies your pages, your products, your services. It generates structured data — JSON-LD, Schema.org markup, entity annotations — and adds it to every page. Your site is now, the tool assures you, ready for AI agents and language model citation. Job done.

Here is what it does not tell you.

What you got

Every JSON-LD block the tool added is inline data embedded in your HTML. Your pages now carry more data than they did before — not visible to your visitors, but present in the document that every machine parser must traverse. A page that previously loaded a product name from clean semantic HTML now loads the product name after parsing a nested Product → Offer → ShippingDetails → ShippingDeliveryTime schema graph.

For well-resourced machines reading your page in a full browser context, this is invisible. For constrained machines — lightweight agents, local models, automated tools running within shared compute budgets — it is a processing cost they may not be able to pay. The machines that benefit from structured data and the machines that get overwhelmed by it are frequently the same machines.

This is the over-decoration trap: the act of speaking more clearly to machines can create conditions that make the message harder to receive.

What the tools are

Tools like Enhancely and Adobe LLM Optimizer represent two ends of a spectrum.

Enhancely generates JSON-LD automatically and continuously, running a factuality-validation pipeline before injection — checking that extracted values match actual page content. That is better than nothing. It is not the same as human review. There is no per-page approval step before schema goes live, and there is no provenance record — no trail that tells a downstream AI agent which values were authored by the publisher and which were inferred by a machine.

Adobe LLM Optimizer takes the more careful approach: a human must approve each change before it reaches production, and it deploys at the CDN edge rather than injecting script tags into the source HTML. But the approval record lives inside Adobe's platform. It does not travel with the content. If an auditor or an AI agent needs to know what was changed and why, the answer is in a vendor dashboard, not in the page.

SiteUp, AthenaHQ, and others in this category are higher-volume operations with less documented oversight.

What is missing

None of these tools — at any point on the spectrum — produce a provenance record that travels with the content. An AI agent reading a page augmented by these tools sees structured data. It cannot tell whether that data was written by the publisher with full editorial intent, inferred by a machine that validated it against the page, generated from a template and never reviewed, or added five years ago by a tool that is no longer installed.

That ambiguity is the provenance gap. It matters to regulators, to auditors, and increasingly to AI agents that need to distinguish trustworthy claims from inferred ones. MX exists to fill this gap — metadata that records who produced a claim, when, and under what authority, and that travels with the content rather than living in a vendor dashboard.

The audit question

Before you install one of these tools, or if you already have one running, the questions to ask are:

  • What is it injecting, and into which pages?
  • Has the injected content been validated against the actual page content, or generated from templates?
  • Is there a record of what was added and when — a record that does not depend on the vendor's platform remaining accessible?
  • Has the injection increased your pages' inline data weight to the point where constrained parsers struggle?

If you do not have clear answers to all four, you are running AI optimisation on your site without knowing what it is doing.
