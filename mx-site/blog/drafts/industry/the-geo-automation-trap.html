Metadata Is the Right Move. Human-in-Command Is the Right Model.

24 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Let me say this clearly upfront: we're enthusiastically pro-metadata.

Adding structured data to your site improves machine readability. JSON-LD, Schema.org markup, entity annotations: these are the signals that help AI agents understand what a page is about, who published it, and what it offers. MX is built on exactly this idea. The entire practice of machine experience starts with the question of whether a machine can find and understand what you've published.

So when a new category of tool emerges that adds structured data automatically, tools designed to make your site more visible to AI agents and language models, our reaction isn't to disparage it. It's to ask how to make it work better.

The question is not whether. It is how

Enhancely and Adobe LLM Optimizer are good examples of tools doing the right thing for their purpose. Enhancely runs a factuality-validation pipeline before deploying any schema, cross-referencing every extracted value against actual page content to prevent hallucinated values from reaching the page. Adobe requires a human to approve each change before it goes live, and monitors source pages for drift, pausing automatically when it detects it. Both are doing what they're designed to do, and they're doing it well.

Neither tool is MX aware, but that's not a criticism. MX is a new practice. The standard didn't exist when these tools were built, and we wouldn't expect any product to implement a specification before that specification was published. That's precisely why we're inviting vendors to participate in The Gathering rather than asking them to comply with something after the fact.

The gap isn't in the intention of these tools. It's in what travels with the content after the tool has run.

When a tool injects structured data, the metadata it produces exists in the page, but the record of who produced it, when, under what validation, and whether a human reviewed and approved it does not travel with the content. A downstream AI agent, an auditor, or a regulator reading the page sees structured data. They can't tell whether that data was authored by the publisher with full editorial intent, validated by a factuality pipeline and deployed automatically, generated from a template and never reviewed, or added years ago by a tool that's no longer in use.

This is the provenance gap. It's not a criticism of any vendor. It's an observation about what the practice is still building toward.

Human-in-Command is the correct model

The EU AI HLEG principle we build against is Human-in-Command: every automated system operates within a declared output boundary, and a human holds authority over when and how that system acts. Adobe LLM Optimizer is already close to this model. A person signs off before any change reaches production. That's the right shape.

The direction of travel is clear: automate within a boundary a human set, with a human reviewing the output, and a record of that review that travels with the content rather than sitting in a vendor dashboard.

What that looks like in practice is a report that carries its own evidence chain. Every PDF an MX audit produces embeds the full provenance record inside the file's XMP metadata: who ran each step, what inputs it saw, what verdict it reached, which AI-governance frameworks the step is recorded against. An auditor with only the PDF can run exiftool -b -XMP-mx:ProvenanceAiPayload <file>.pdf and read the complete chain offline, without contacting us, without accessing a dashboard, without trusting our word for anything. The chain also lives as a sidecar JSON file next to the PDF on disk. We describe the full mechanic in How an MX Audit Proves Itself. The short version: the report proves itself.

That's the model for metadata provenance generally. Not a record in a vendor dashboard that requires a login. A record that rides with the artefact, open to any inspector, machine-readable by design.

The over-decoration problem, where adding too much structured data increases the inline data weight of the page to the point where constrained parsers struggle to process it, is a real operational concern, and the protocols book covers it in full. But it's secondary to the provenance question. A page with carefully curated, human-reviewed structured data and a clear record of its origin is in a better position than a page with comprehensive automated schema and no trail.

An invitation to vendors

The Gathering is an open, community-led standards body that governs the MX metadata standard. It exists to fill the gaps that established standards (Dublin Core, Schema.org, DCAT, OpenAPI) leave open, in a way that no single vendor controls.

If you're building a GEO optimisation tool, we invite you to join. The provenance question we're raising here isn't unique to MX. It's the open problem the whole category needs to solve, and solving it together as an open standard is better for everyone than each vendor building a proprietary trail that stays on their own platform.

The metadata you generate is valuable. We'd like to help it be trustworthy too.

The right questions to ask

If your site runs an AI optimisation tool, or you're evaluating one, these are the questions worth asking:

  • Does the tool produce a record of what it injected and when, a record that travels with the content rather than staying in the tool's platform?
  • Is there a human review and approval step before metadata reaches production?
  • Is the generated metadata validated against actual page content, not inferred from templates?
  • Is the total inline data weight of your pages within thresholds that constrained machine parsers can handle?

Fuller details on all of these questions, and the architectural reasoning behind them, are in MX: The Protocols, the book we're publishing on building for machine experience.
