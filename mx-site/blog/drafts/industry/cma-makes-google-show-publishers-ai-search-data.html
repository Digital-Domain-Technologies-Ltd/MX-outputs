The CMA Just Made Google Show Publishers Their AI Search Data

7 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

A LinkedIn post crossed my feed this week claiming the UK regulator had just forced Google to hand publishers a pile of data on AI search, clicks included. The claim sounded too clean. I checked it. It holds up.

On 3 June 2026 the Competition and Markets Authority imposed its first conduct requirement on Google Search under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, following Google's Strategic Market Status designation in October 2025. The headline is real: Google now has to show publishers how users engage with their content inside AI search features, and it has to let publishers say no.

Note: This page describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

What Google has to disclose

The conduct requirement makes Google give publishers metrics on how their content performs in search generative-AI features. The text names the measures plainly. Impressions. Clicks. Click-through rate. The ability to work out click quality. These are not the aggregate numbers Search Console shows today.

Two conditions make the data useful rather than decorative.

First, it must be disaggregated. The data has to be separated from other elements of general search, including organic web results, images, videos, and search features that are not generative-AI features. A publisher will be able to see what AI Overviews and AI Mode did to their traffic on its own, not blended into one number that hides the effect.

Second, it must be delivered somewhere publishers already work. The requirement asks for a commonly accessible platform, and names Google Search Console as the example. No bespoke portal, no data request queue. The place publishers already check.

What publishers can now refuse

Alongside the metrics, the conduct requirement gives publishers controls to withhold their content. They can keep it out of AI Overviews, AI Mode, and the broader generative-AI services that include Gemini and Vertex AI. The controls work at directory level and at page level, so a publisher can withhold a section without withholding the site.

This is where the LinkedIn summary stretched a little. It described a way to "differentiate training from RAG-led answers". The regulator does not use those words. What it actually separates is grounding in search generative-AI features from training and grounding in the broader AI services. The spirit is right. The vocabulary is the poster's, not the CMA's. If you act on the detail, read the requirement, not the summary.

Google also has to publish clear, comprehensible information explaining how publishers' content is used in its generative AI. The opacity that made all of this hard to argue about is itself now in scope.

The timeline, and the pressure behind it

Google has nine months to implement the full set of changes, though the CMA expects meaningful controls to reach publishers well before that deadline.

The pressure is measurable. After AI Overviews rolled out fully in the UK in late 2025, zero-click searches (where the user gets the answer on the results page and never clicks through) rose by close to 30% in categories like health and local news. Publishers carried the cost of the content and saw the visit disappear into a summary. The conduct requirement is the regulator's response to that gap.

Why this matters beyond Google

Strip the Google specifics away and a pattern remains. A regulator has decided that when a machine uses your content, you are entitled to a machine-readable, per-feature, attributable account of what it did with it, delivered through a channel you already use.

That is the argument we have been making for MX from the other direction. Content meets machines now, not only people. When it does, it should carry a structured account of what it is, who made it, and how it may be used, and the systems that consume it should be able to report back in kind. The CMA has just turned a slice of that argument into an obligation on the largest consumer of publisher content there is.

I want to be careful about the claim. The conduct requirement is a transparency and control duty on Google, not a metadata standard, and meeting it is Google's legal problem, not something any tool grants. What it does confirm is the direction. The demand for machine-readable usage data and verifiable attribution is no longer a thing we argue for in the abstract. A regulator is now ordering it into existence. The organisations that already treat their content as something machines read, and that already keep a tamper-evident record of how it travels, will find the next of these requirements easier to meet than the ones still treating the page as something only people see.

The first conduct requirement under the new regime landed on AI search. It will not be the last, and it will not stop at search.
