What the Commission published

On 10 June 2026 the European Commission published its final Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content. It is a voluntary code. It sets out practical steps to help providers and deployers of generative AI meet the transparency obligations in Article 50 of the AI Act: label deepfakes, label AI-generated or AI-manipulated text published on matters of public interest, and tell people when they are talking to a machine rather than a person.

Note: This page describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

Read the two facts together and the shape is clear. The Code is voluntary. The obligation it helps you meet is not, and the date is fixed: Article 50 applies on 2 August 2026. The Code is the practical path to get there, not an alternative to getting there.

The part worth noticing

The headline is the deadline. The interesting part is the method. The Commission could have invented a new marking mechanism and told the industry to implement it. It did not. It chose to promote open, interoperable marking built into the tools and platforms people already use, the approach C2PA Content Credentials were designed for.

That is the right call, and it is the call I have been arguing for since before there was a deadline to argue against. A label a person sees is only as good as the signal underneath it, and the only marking that survives contact with the real content ecosystem, where files are copied, transcoded, re-hosted and stripped of context, is an open and interoperable one. A proprietary mark works until the file leaves the system that made it. An open one travels.

This is the founding bet of MX and of The Gathering, the community body that governs the standard. We do not invent mechanisms where good open ones exist. We defer to them. Where Dublin Core, Schema.org, EXIF, XMP, the IETF signature work and C2PA already cover the ground, MX uses them rather than redrawing them. A regulator reaching the same conclusion in print is not a threat to that bet. It is the bet paying off.

Marking is a floor, not the whole house

Here is where I want to be precise, because it is easy to overclaim and I would rather not.

The Code, and Article 50 behind it, is about one thing: marking AI-generated content so a reader can tell it was made or altered by a machine. C2PA owns the hard part of that for media: a cryptographic manifest bound into an image, a video or an audio file, recording how it was made. That is a real, well-built primitive, and MX does not compete with it.

MX is the layer above. C2PA is bound to media files. MX carries provenance for any addressable artefact: a web page, a PDF, a contract, a price list, a dataset, a line of code. A claim made under MX can embed a C2PA manifest as its evidence, and a C2PA manifest can be the basis for a claim issued under MX. The two are complementary by design. Where C2PA stops at the edge of media, MX keeps going, into every other kind of file an AI agent reads on its way to quoting you to a buyer you will never meet.

So the Code does not make MX redundant. It strengthens the floor MX stands on, and it leaves MX's own job exactly where it was: making every file legible and checkable, not just the pictures.

Watermarks and Content Credentials are partners, not rivals

One more distinction, because it gets muddled constantly and the muddle matters.

Watermarking and Content Credentials are not competing answers to the same question. They answer different ones. A watermark is hidden in the content itself, so it survives a screenshot, a re-encode or a crop, when the metadata has been stripped away. Signed provenance carries verifiable history: who made this, when, and whether it has been altered since. The Content Authenticity Initiative frames durable transparency as needing both, plus content fingerprinting as a third pillar, and that framing is correct. The EU's Code reflects the same thinking.

MX, and REGINALD as its signing layer, live firmly on the signed-provenance side. We make the verifiable-history half: the signed, dated, readable record that says a file is the one its author published, unaltered. We do not do watermarking, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Watermarking is a robustness primitive that belongs with the people who build it well; MX references it, it does not absorb it. If someone tells you a single product gives you durable transparency on its own, they are selling you half the answer and calling it the whole one.

Eight weeks

Eight weeks from now, if you ship or deploy generative AI whose output touches someone in the EU, you need provenance in production. Not a signed PDF in a drawer. Provenance the file carries with it, that a regulator, an auditor or a buyer can check at the moment of an AI-mediated decision, which is the only moment that counts.

Let me be honest about what MX does and does not do here, because the temptation to oversell at a deadline is exactly when honesty earns its keep. MX and REGINALD do not make you compliant with the AI Act. Compliance is a legal duty that stays with your organisation. What they do is make the documentation you have to produce anyway into something structured, machine-readable, tamper-evident, and verifiable on request. Sold as compliance in a box, that collapses on the first inquiry. Sold as queryable, verifiable evidence, it is precisely what a regulator, an auditor and a buyer actually ask for.

What to do before August

Four things, in order, none of which require waiting for anyone:

  • Find out what machines already see. Most organisations have no inventory of how they appear to an AI agent. Audit the surfaces that get read and quoted before you change anything.
  • Mark the high-stakes files first. A price, a regulatory disclosure, a public statement, a piece of brand media. Anywhere a hallucination or a misquote would cost you.
  • Sign what you mark. A mark a reader cannot check is a claim, not evidence. Use the open authentication standards; that is what the signing layer is for.
  • Make the signals findable. A signed file no machine can find is no signature at all. Link it from your sitemap, your feeds and your llms.txt.

Brussels has told you the direction of travel and given you a date. The direction is open, interoperable, checkable provenance. The date is 2 August. The pleasant surprise, for once, is that the regulator and the open-standards community want the same thing, and the work you do to meet the one is the work that serves the other.
