Is GEO just SEO?

17 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

Short answer: mostly, yes. Generative Engine Optimization is largely off-page SEO with a new playbook, and the slice that is genuinely new is smaller than the invoices suggest. The longer answer is worth having, because the research everyone cites says something more specific than the LinkedIn summaries do, and because the part that survives the question points straight at the thing GEO quietly depends on.

So is it just off-page SEO?

The popular version of this argument is right in its bones. Look at the GEO checklist: reviews, brand mentions, getting featured in listicles, building authority, consistent messaging across platforms. That is the off-page SEO repertoire. The difference is not what you do. It is where you do it: the focus shifts from chasing any backlink going to earning a place in the specific sources that AI answer engines retrieve from. As one widely shared post put it, it is off-page SEO with a different playbook. That is a fair description, not a put-down.

What the research actually validates

The study everyone points to is real. "GEO: Generative Engine Optimization" (Aggarwal, Murahari, and colleagues) was published at KDD 2024 by researchers at Princeton, Georgia Tech, the Allen Institute for AI, and IIT Delhi. It tested nine content tactics across roughly ten thousand queries. One caveat matters before anyone quotes the numbers: it ran against a simulated, Bing-style answer engine, not live ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini, so treat the figures as direction, not a guarantee.

What it found, in its own visibility metric:

Tactic Lift
Add relevant quotations about +41%
Add statistics about +32%
Cite credible sources about +30%
Write fluently about +28%
Keyword stuffing zero to slightly negative

Notice what the winning tactics are. Corroborate your claims, quote real sources, use concrete figures, write clearly. That is not a new discipline. It is good content practice, the kind search has rewarded for years, pointed at a new reader. (You will also notice this page is built the same way, with a direct answer up top, a real table, and sources you can check. That is not a coincidence.)

What is folklore

The research is solid. The retellings inflate it. The real ceiling was a lift of roughly forty percent in a metric on a simulated engine, not a doubling and not a "three to five times" multiplier. So when a post tells you that three statistics on a page doubles your citation odds, or that comparison tables get cited three to five times more often, you are reading practitioner numbers dressed up as a Princeton finding. The paper says neither. And the claim, common in the comments, that authority and entity optimization were "born because of AI" is simply wrong: entity SEO and knowledge-graph work predate large language models by years.

There is a piece of folklore to name before it becomes a habit: that more markup is always better. It is not. Hand the job to an agent and "add structured data" becomes "tag everything", because a tool has no way to judge which facts carry weight. Bury a page under indiscriminate schema and you train the reader to stop trusting any of it, the same lesson FAQ markup taught when publishers gamed it. The skill is subtraction: tag the handful of claims a machine will act on, and let a person who understands the page decide which those are.

Where the assistants actually look

The "where, not what" point gets concrete when you see which sources each assistant leans on. An analysis by Profound of citations across the major engines found a clear split: ChatGPT draws heavily on Wikipedia, Perplexity leans on Reddit, Google's AI Overviews lean toward Reddit and YouTube, and Claude leans on editorial blogs. No one can promise that a given assistant will name your brand, and anyone who does is selling reassurance. What the evidence supports is where off-site effort is most likely to be seen, which makes off-site coverage a portfolio decision rather than a single bet.

Then why does the question matter?

Here is the part that survives "it's just SEO." Every tactic above, on-page and off, assumes something it never states: that a machine can read your content in the first place, and that it can tell the content is genuine. That assumption is the gap. You can run the off-page playbook to perfection and still lose the machine that fetches your page as raw HTML, screenshots it, reads it through a screen reader, or opens it as a PDF with no idea who published it or whether it has changed since.

That foundation is what Machine Experience covers. MX makes content machine-readable: structured so any machine, on any device, in any format, gets the same answer to what this is, who published it, and whether it is current. Reginald makes it machine-trustworthy: a verifiable record that the content is genuine and unaltered. Readable, then trustworthy, and then a third property the content itself has to carry, corroborated, where load-bearing claims point to authoritative sources that independently state them. That last one, you will have spotted, is exactly what the research found moved the needle.

So GEO is a tactic, and a useful one. It is not the foundation. The longer version of that argument lives in GEO is a tactic. MX is the specification..

The better question

"Is GEO just SEO?" is the wrong question to build a budget around, because the honest answer, mostly yes with a new playbook, does not tell you what to make. The better question is the one underneath it: what does my content have to be, so that any machine that arrives can read it, trust it, and act on it, whichever pathway it came through and whichever engine is in fashion this quarter? Answer that, and the citation tactics become the easy part you layer on top.
