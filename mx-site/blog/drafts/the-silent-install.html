The Silent Install

29 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 3 min read

What may a program do to your device without asking? The question used to be academic. It stopped being academic when a browser installed on billions of machines began writing a multi-gigabyte AI model to people's disks with no prompt, hiding the file behind an opaque name, and rewriting it whenever they deleted it. This series works through what that means and what to do about it.

The argument in a line: a signature tells you who shipped a program and that it has not been altered; it tells you nothing about what the program does once it is running, or what it pulls onto your device next. Closing that gap is a job for attestation and provenance, not for anyone's good intentions.

The series

Read in order, or take whichever question you came for.

  • Respect Runs Both Ways, the principle. MX asks people to make their content legible to machines. That is one signature on a contract. The other is the machine, and the company behind it, owing the person consent and restraint in return.
  • The Signature Covers Who Shipped It, Not What It Fetches, the mechanism. Code signing attests identity, not behaviour. Model weights are inscrutable as content and unexecutable as code, which is why reading them is off the table and attestation is the only route left. Where FAIR carries trust in code, MX carries trust in content, and the two meet at the binary.
  • From "Don't Be Evil" to "Don't Ask", the moan, and the fix. A plain look at the company whose motto was once "Don't be evil" and which deleted it quietly in 2018, and why the durable answer does not depend on any company choosing to be good.

What the series argues

One idea runs through all of it. The relationship between a person and the software acting on their behalf has to run in both directions; consent and restraint are owed back. The trust model we have checks who shipped a thing, not what it does over its life. The artefact at the centre of the question, a model's weights, cannot be read at all, which leaves attestation as the only honest way to trust it. The law that would govern this already exists and sits largely unenforced. Enforce that law. Make conduct a record rather than a promise, so consent, permitted behaviour, and what a program actually did become things an outsider can check rather than things you take on faith.

That is the whole of it. Where you cannot trust the actor, you trust the evidence, and you make sure the evidence exists.

Where your own content stands

The same discipline that would make a silent install checkable is the one that decides whether the machines now reading the web on your buyers' behalf can find, trust, and act on what you publish.
