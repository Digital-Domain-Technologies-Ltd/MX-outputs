Write Tight: Prose for Humans and Machines

31 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 3 min read

Tight prose is machine-readable-web hygiene, not a matter of taste.

Cut a sentence to its core and two readers benefit at once. The human gets the point in one pass. The machine spends fewer tokens, meets less ambiguity, and is far less likely to misread you.

That is the Convergence Principle, applied to words. Write tight and you serve the reader and the agent with the same edit.

What Tight Prose Costs a Machine

An AI agent reads your page token by token. Padding is not free. Every filler clause is tokens it pays for, and tokens cost money, time, and energy.

Ambiguity costs more. A long sentence with three nested clauses hides the claim. The agent guesses at what you meant. A wrong guess becomes a wrong answer, attributed to you.

Tight prose removes the guesswork. One idea per sentence. Subject, verb, object. The claim's right there in the text, so a machine reads it straight off. No inference, no guessing.

The Same Cut Serves the Reader

None of this trades away the human reader. The opposite happens.

Short sentences are easier to follow. Cut the hedging and the position lands. Cut the adverbs and the verb does the work. A reader who skims still gets the point, because the argument sits in the first clause, not buried after a long run-up.

What helps the machine helps the person. That is the whole MX thesis, worked down to one habit.

Watch It Happen

Here is one claim, written twice.

Before, at 43 words:

When a team publishes a page, it is important to make sure the content is structured in a way that automated systems can parse without difficulty, because a failure to do this can cause the system to misread what the page actually means.

After, at 14 words:

Structure the page so machines can parse it. Then they will not misread it.

Same claim. From 43 words to 14. A reader gets it faster, and an agent reaches it for far fewer tokens. Nothing of value was lost in the cut.

How to Cut

The method is simple to state and harder to do. Write the draft naturally. Then revise by cutting. If the meaning survives the cut, the cut was right.

Four moves do most of the work:

  • One idea per sentence. Break a compound thought into separate sentences.
  • Drop the subordinate clause. Make it its own sentence, or delete it.
  • Cut hedging and adverbs. "Somewhat", "arguably", and "quite" all soften the line.
  • Name the thing. Concrete nouns beat abstract ones. Write "build records", not "record-building capabilities".

Then read it aloud. If you stumble, the sentence is still too long.

Where This Fits MX

MX makes content readable to machines. Tight prose does that work at the level of the sentence.

We write our audit reports this way. Findings in short declarative lines. Recommendations a client can act on without decoding a paragraph. The habit that helps an agent parse a page helps a busy reader act on a report.

It lowers cost too. Fewer tokens per page means cheaper inference and less energy. Less ambiguity means fewer machine guesses, and a guess that misfires becomes a confident, wrong answer about your business.
