Your ISO 42001 Certificate Is Evidence, Not a Shield

5 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

OUTLINE PLAN - not the finished post. This file is the structure, argument, sources, and build checklist for a long-form piece in the MX blog series. Promote to full prose in a later pass, then flip x-mx-contentState to published and status to published.

Tom Cranstoun · The Machine Experience Authority · CogNovaMX Ltd

Why this post, why now

The BSI "AI standards application and interactions in assessments" piece, and the wider 2026 standards literature, all circle one distinction that buyers keep collapsing: holding a certificate against a standard is not the same as being compliant with the law. Conformity assessments under the EU AI Act begin to bite from August 2026 and August 2027. Organisations are buying ISO/IEC 42001 certification and assuming it closes the regulatory question. It does not. This post draws the line cleanly, and shows where MX and Reginald sit on the right side of it.

The angle is the one CogNovaMX already holds in every other surface: an evidence vehicle, never a compliance grant. The market's own standards bodies are now drawing the same line. The post rides that, it does not fight it.

Thesis (one sentence)

A standards certificate proves you run a system; only a harmonised standard cited in the Official Journal grants presumption of conformity; and in the gap between the two sits the documentation an assessor actually has to read, which is exactly what MX makes machine-readable and Reginald makes verifiable.

Audience

  • Primary: the buyer or compliance lead who has been told "get ISO 42001 and you are covered". Mid-market, regulated-adjacent, EU-facing.
  • Secondary: the auditor, the notified body, and the machine that walks the evidence chain. Name the machine explicitly as an audience; the metadata behind the post is for it.
  • Register: tight by default, neutral English (this publishes to mx-site), first-person, no superlatives, hyphens not em-dashes.

Note: This page describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

Section structure (the argument beats)

  1. The misread. Open on the scene a compliance lead recognises: a certificate framed on the wall, a regulator's letter on the desk, and the quiet assumption that the first answers the second. State the gap plainly. (Mirror the padlock open of "Provenance You Can See" without repeating it.)

  2. Standard versus regulation. A regulation is the law, it states the duty. A standard is a tool, it describes a way to meet a duty. ISO/IEC 42001 is an AI management-system standard, the discipline of governing AI, not a statement of what any specific law requires. Certifying against it proves you operate the system; it says nothing about whether a given high-risk system meets a given legal requirement.

  3. The presumption-of-conformity mechanism. EU AI Act Article 40: a high-risk system conforming to a relevant harmonised standard is presumed to conform to the matching legal requirements. This is the real prize, it flips the burden onto the regulator. But it attaches only to harmonised European standards published and cited in the Official Journal of the EU, shaped through a Commission standardisation request and carrying an Annex ZA mapping to the Act's requirements.

  4. Where ISO 42001 actually stands. It is an international standard. No Annex ZA, not OJEU-cited, so no presumption of conformity today. It is expected to feed the European harmonised standard through CEN-CENELEC. Name prEN 18286 as the candidate AIMS standard that, once published and cited, will carry the Annex ZA. Until then, a 42001 certificate is evidence of accountability that an assessor reads, not a shield that ends the inquiry.

  5. What a conformity assessment actually inspects (Article 43). Walk the technical documentation (Article 11 / Annex IV), the logging (Article 12), risk management, data governance, human oversight, accuracy and robustness. The assessor's job is to trace a requirement to the evidence that satisfies it. This is a documentation exercise. The certificate is one input; the evidence chain is the work.

  6. The honest line, stated outright. Standards and certificates are evidence, not compliance. Sold as "queryable, verifiable evidence" they are exactly what regulators, auditors, and notified bodies need. Sold as "compliance in a box" they collapse on the first inquiry. This is the CogNovaMX position, said plainly.

  7. Where MX and Reginald fit (the two pillars). MX makes the documentation machine-readable: provenance, context, and intended use travelling with the file, so an assessor or an agent reads the evidence in one fetch. Reginald makes it machine-trustworthy: tamper-evident provenance sidecars, sequence-bound and gap-detectable, plus registry attestation a client verifies for itself. Together they produce the evidence pack a conformity assessment consumes. Point at the AI / deterministic provenance pair and the regime registry (which now tracks harmonised-standard status per entry).

  8. The honest limits. MX and Reginald do not grant conformity, do not stand in for a notified-body assessment, do not make a false claim true. They make the documentation the organisation already owes structured, tamper-evident, and verifiable on request. The legal duty stays with the organisation.

  9. How to start. Practical first move: stop treating the certificate as the destination. Make the evidence behind it machine-readable now, sign the records that an assessment will demand, and track which standards are actually OJEU-cited so you know when real presumption of conformity becomes available. Start with the systems where a wrong answer is costly.

Proof points to land (must be accurate, cite sources)

  • Article 40 grants presumption only via harmonised standards cited in the OJEU.
  • ISO/IEC 42001 carries no Annex ZA and is not OJEU-cited as of mid-2026.
  • prEN 18286 is the candidate harmonised AIMS standard under CEN-CENELEC JTC 21.
  • Conformity assessments phase in from August 2026 / August 2027.
  • ISO/IEC 42005 (AI system impact assessment) bridges the management system and per-system impact duties.
