MX: The Protocols Is Here, and the Timing Is Not an Accident

13 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

In 2026, Google published the Open Knowledge Format: markdown files with YAML frontmatter, linked into a graph, no platform required. I wrote at the time that this confirms the foundation MX is built on and leaves the hard half open. That settled the readable layer, and the validation came from about the largest source possible. What a machine can't do, once it has read your content in that format, is tell whether to trust it or who governs the rules behind it.

MX: The Protocols publishes in January 2027, and it's the specification of that missing layer. The timing isn't a coincidence: the industry has agreed on how machine-readable knowledge is structured, and this book covers the part that agreement leaves out.

What the format leaves to you

A format tells a machine how to read a file. It doesn't tell the machine whether the file is genuine, current, unaltered, or written by who it claims. Those questions need a model rather than better styling. Authorship and dates have to be stated so a machine reads them instead of guessing. Content has to be signed so tampering shows. The whole record then goes into a dated entry that something independent can verify. The Protocols specifies that model.

It's also the half that decides whether any of this is usable under the documentation regimes that buyers, auditors, and regulators increasingly ask organisations to produce. A well-formed knowledge bundle that can't prove its origin answers none of those questions. The book is about building the signed, attested kind, which answers all of them.

What is in the book

The Protocols is the strategic companion to The Handbook. Where the Handbook gives you the implementation patterns, the Protocols gives you the model that ties those patterns together.

The MX Readiness Model, levels 0 to 5, tracks content as it moves through unreadable, discoverable, citable, and attested, and says what you do at each step. The REGINALD compliance levels, 1 to 5, cover the trust side: from a declared cog through to a signed, registry-attested record any machine can verify for itself. Between them they answer the two questions a format can't: how ready is this content for machines, and how much can a machine trust it.

The book also makes the governance argument: the standard underneath all of this is held by The Gathering, a community body independent of any single vendor, rather than by a company. OKF is the clearest example of why that distinction matters. Whoever owns a format can steer it, and the meaning of the trust layer shouldn't belong to any one company.

It also explains why the model compounds rather than stalls. The system is designed around what O'Reilly called the architecture of participation: using MX and improving MX are the same act. An organisation that writes COGs to make its content machine-readable for its own agents is contributing to MX at the same time. One that registers with REGINALD to make its content discoverable adds to the registry's value for every other agent that queries it. The adoption model runs on self-interest rather than generosity, and that's a far more reliable foundation for a standard that needs to last.

Who this is for

Technical leads deciding how their content meets the machine web. Compliance and risk owners who are being asked for documentation a marketing tactic can't produce. Founders and operators who can see that "readable by machines" is about to be table stakes and want to understand what comes after it. Anyone who followed the OKF announcement, recognised the markdown-and-frontmatter pattern, and asked the obvious next question: once the machine can read it, can it trust it?
