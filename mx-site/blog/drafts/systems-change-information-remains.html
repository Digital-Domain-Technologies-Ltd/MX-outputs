Systems Change, Information Remains

29 October 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

I started work in a place where information failing wasn't an inconvenience.

I was born on the west coast of Scotland during the Cold War. The Royal Navy's submarine bases and the weapons stores near them had filled the region with some of the most advanced communications networks in Europe, built for NATO operations that had to keep running under the hardest conditions. I joined as an apprentice telecommunications engineer, with security clearance, and learned early that accuracy, reliability and trust were operating requirements, not optional qualities.

I worked on crossbar and then electronic telephone exchanges. Most people saw telecommunications equipment. I saw specialised computers: large machines that received information, interpreted it, routed it and handed it to the next system. I went on to study Communication and Computing at Glasgow Technical College, and that way of seeing shaped every job I took afterwards.

Five Decades, One Question

Since 1977 I have worked through most of the generations of computing in turn: IBM mainframes and PL/I, databases, enterprise content management, web publishing, cloud platforms and now AI systems. I watched computing shift from the centre to the network, and information architecture go from serving people to being mediated by machines.

Every one of those technologies was replaced. One question outlived them all:

How do we preserve meaning as information moves between systems?

What the Newsroom Taught Me

The BBC shaped my thinking more than anywhere else. At BBC News I worked on the journalist systems around AP's ENPS newsroom platform and built systems that fed from it and complemented it. Much of what later became standard in enterprise CMS was already there in daily use: structured content with metadata, editorial workflow with governance over what went out, and stories kept separate from their presentation so they could be reused.

The newsroom taught me something more important than architecture. Every story needed evidence. Every statement needed a source. Every claim needed checking before it went out. Provenance, attribution, verification and editorial accountability weren't extras; they were the job.

That's why I read what the AI industry calls hallucination as a provenance problem before it's a fault in the model. A journalist asks where a piece of information came from, whether it can be trusted and has been verified, what the authoritative source is, and how much confidence it deserves. A machine now has to ask the same questions, usually with far less to go on.

The Shift in 2024

Looking across my career, the pattern was plain. Mainframes gave way to networks, networks to the web, the web to cloud platforms, and those to AI. Platforms changed and architectures changed, but information kept moving between systems.

In 2024 I was looking at what AI was doing to content management and publishing. Most of the industry was focused on machines generating content. The bigger change, I thought, was the other direction: machines had become a permanent audience for content.

Once you publish something, it leaves you. It enters machine ecosystems you don't control: search engines, retrieval systems, knowledge graphs, vector databases, on-device models, corporate AI, foundation models, autonomous agents, and architectures nobody has built yet. You can't know which machine will read your work next. The only thing you can shape is the work itself.

Machine Experience (MX) starts there. Its aim is that information stays understandable, trustworthy, attributable and rich in context, carrying its provenance and intent with it, wherever it travels.

Why "Machine" and Not "AI"

I avoid naming MX after AI, LLMs, agents, assistants or chatbots, because each of those describes a current implementation. Machines come in many forms: small and on-device models, enterprise and foundation models, crawlers, retrieval systems, reasoning systems, agents. The technologies will keep changing. The machine audience won't go away. Naming the audience rather than the technology keeps the discipline useful after this generation of tools has passed.

The same reasoning places MX alongside the newer optimisation disciplines rather than in competition with them:

  • SEO serves search engines.
  • GEO serves generative engines.
  • AEO serves answer engines.
  • AX serves autonomous agents.
  • MX serves machines.

Each of the first four addresses one machine audience or behaviour. MX asks the broader question: how does an artefact keep its meaning, provenance, authority and intent as it passes through machine ecosystems nobody can predict?

Standards and Practice

A discipline needs stewardship. The web grew because people agreed on how it should work, and the machine era needs the same kind of agreement. That's the role of The Gathering, which develops open standards and publishing practices so that information stays useful, trustworthy and interoperable for people and machines alike.

Standards alone don't get adopted, though. Organisations need help now, with the content they already publish. That's why I co-founded Tityra. Tityra helps organisations see how search engines, AI models, retrieval systems, knowledge platforms and agents actually experience their information, and then improve it. Much of the market is talking about agent experience; the question underneath is simpler. Can a machine understand, trust and use what you publish?

What Stays

MX isn't an AI optimisation framework, an agent framework or a search framework. It comes from fifty years of watching the same thing happen. Systems change. Platforms change. Models change. Machines change. The information remains, and so does the question I started with in a telephone exchange in Scotland: how do we make sure meaning, provenance, trust and intent survive every hop between systems?
