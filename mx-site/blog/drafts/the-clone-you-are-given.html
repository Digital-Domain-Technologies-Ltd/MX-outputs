The Clone You Are Given Is Not the Repository

6 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

Ask your AI assistant to search your project's history and it'll answer in seconds, brimming with confidence. Here's the uncomfortable bit: the answer can be almost completely wrong, and neither you nor the assistant will get any warning. Not because the model hallucinated. Because the workspace it was standing in never contained the thing you asked about.

Thin, fat, and the myth of the simple label

If you've been around git people, you'll have heard the words "thin clone" and "fat clone". A fat clone is the whole estate: full history, every sibling repository, dependencies installed, tools present. A thin clone is something less than that. The words suggest a tidy binary, and that's the first thing most people, and especially vibe coders, get wrong.

There's no binary. Completeness is a spectrum across a dozen separate axes. A clone can have the files but not the history. The history but not the sibling repositories. The code but not the dependencies. The dependencies but not the document toolchain. Everything above but none of the safety hooks the team relies on, because git installs precisely none of them when you clone. Each axis fails differently: some things crash loudly, some degrade politely, and some answer your question with total confidence while quietly covering a fraction of what you believed they covered. Those last ones are the dangerous ones.

Now add the part almost nobody checks. You didn't prepare that workspace. Your environment did: the IDE, the hosted agent platform, the CI container. Each one decides what your clone contains, each one decides differently, none of them publish the recipe, and the recipe changes without notice. Two workspaces that look identical in a file listing can hold different pasts and enforce different rules. If you build software by conversation, you'll never see the difference. You were never told there was one.

One example

Recently I asked an assistant, working in a hosted cloud workspace, to mine a repository's commit log for a phrase I knew had been used. The answer came back fast and clean: nothing there. Two matching commits in the entire project.

The repository was months old, with thousands of commits. The workspace had been handed two days of history. Not through malice; the platform clones shallow so containers start fast, which is a perfectly sensible engineering choice. But git never volunteers that anything is missing. Ask a shallow clone for its log and it answers with the fraction it holds, confidently, like a witness who genuinely believes they saw the whole thing.

The remedy took seconds: one command fetched the missing months, and the real answer turned out to be genuinely useful. But every history-shaped answer produced before that moment was quietly wrong, and nothing in the workspace would ever have said so. The lesson isn't "shallow clones are bad". The lesson is: a confidently thin answer is a finding, not an answer. Probe the ground before you trust it.

This is a standards problem, and that's good news

Here's where it stops being a war story and starts being a fixable problem. A field where every platform prepares your workspace differently, secretly, and changeably is simply a field that hasn't had its tests, governance, and standards moment yet. Tests: a probe suite anyone can run that answers "what did this platform just hand me?" as a conformance check rather than an archaeology dig. Governance: declared rules for what an environment may do to your files, with a human in command of every override. Standards: a machine-readable way for a workspace to declare what it holds, what it stripped, and what it does on its own, so that people and AI agents alike can read the ground instead of reverse-engineering it one surprise at a time.

Readers who know my work will recognise the shape. This is Machine Experience, MX, the discipline I've been applying to content: explicit, machine-readable declarations instead of guessing. It turns out the workspace needs the same treatment as the web page. Machines can't act reliably on ground they can't read, and right now, nobody's ground is readable.

I'm writing the book on this facet of MX. It's called MX: The Repo, and it covers how a repository can describe itself, check itself, heal itself, and be handed safely to anyone, including the contributor who's never learned git and the AI agent that never will. The newest chapters came directly from probing these environments and writing down what they confessed.

Until then, one habit will carry you a long way: before you trust any answer that depends on what your workspace contains, ask the workspace what it actually contains. The clone you are given is not the repository. Knowing that is most of the battle.
