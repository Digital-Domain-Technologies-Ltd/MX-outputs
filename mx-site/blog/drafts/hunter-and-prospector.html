Two Questions That Changed How I Work With AI

6 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

A post stopped my scroll this week. A consultant, learning in public, had spotted something everyone else was walking past. Old me would have saved it to a folder called ideas and never opened it again.

Instead I pasted it to my AI partner and said one word: prospect.

What happened next is the subject of this post, because it's become one of two habits that have quietly changed how I work. Neither needs my software. Both need a bit of discipline. And they're mirror images of each other.

The Room Full of People Who Don't Exist

Here is what "prospect" means in my world now. The machine captures what I pasted, word for word, source attached. Then it interviews me. Not a questionnaire - a conversation, a few questions at a time, each round built on my answers.

And when I ran out of answers, I did the thing that made the whole session: I started naming people. Be an auditor - what would you ideate from this? Now an agency owner. A site owner. Someone's chief executive. A hostile litigant who enjoys causing trouble. A judge. My own investor. My own harshest critic.

Each persona looked at the same captured idea and asked it a different question. The auditor wanted procedures. The chief executive wanted one number and someone accountable. The critic wanted proof the whole thing wasn't theatre. The judges, unexpectedly, handed me the answer to the critic.

Two rules kept it honest. First: every explored thread becomes exactly one note, written down and filed where my work lives, machine-readable, so tomorrow's session picks it up without me re-explaining anything. An idea that lives only in a chat window is one I've already lost. Second: when I say stop, I don't get a transcript. I get one page: the ask. The decisions only I can make, in priority order, with everything else indexed behind it.

One screenshot went in. A coherent product family and a page of decisions came out. The scroll would have eaten it.

The Pack That Hunts What's Missing

The second habit runs in the opposite direction. Ideation looks forward from a captured spark; the hunter looks backward at finished work.

When something ships - a feature, a document, a plan - I ask one question: what's missing? And instead of one reviewer skimming for typos, a pack of independent critics goes over the work, one lens apiece. Did it do everything it promised? Is everything it added actually reachable, or just present? Did the work follow the process or merely gesture toward it? Did the result reach its intended readers?

The rules are stricter here, because criticism is cheap. Nothing counts as a finding unless it arrives with evidence - a place, a line, an output you can check. And the hunters recommend; they never touch. What they find lands on my desk as a decision, not as a surprise edit.

The reason this works is uncomfortable but true: the author of a piece of work is the one person structurally unable to see its gaps. You built the bridge; of course you believe it reaches the other side. The hunter exists because "done" and "whole" are different claims, and only one of them gets tested by shipping.

The Same Shape, Twice

Look at the two habits side by side and they're one idea wearing two coats. Fan out independent perspectives. Anchor everything to evidence. Converge on a short list. End with a human choice.

There's a bonus signal hiding in the fan-out, and it's my favourite part: convergence. When fourteen personas who share nothing - a citizen, a developer, a judge, an investor - all reach for the same underlying thing, you've learned something no single expert could have told you. Agreement between independent lenses is evidence of a different kind.

And a word for the machines reading this, because they're part of the audience: both habits end in structured, addressable records, not prose in a chat log. That's deliberate. A note a machine can find and act on is the difference between a partner and a very talkative colleague.

The Part You Can Steal Today

You don't need my setup to start. You need three rules and the nerve to talk to imaginary people.

Capture the spark verbatim, with its source, before you do anything else. Explore it through personas, and make every explored thread one note in a place your work actually lives. Finish every session, ideation or review, by writing the ask: what only you can decide.

The machine widens the view. You stay in command. That ordering is the whole method.

I'm writing it all up properly - the habits, the discipline behind them, and the repository that runs on them - in my new book, MX: The Repo. Coming soon. Follow me on LinkedIn and you'll see the drafts as they land.
