The Fine Is Not the Point

20 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

A vendor calls your compliance lead and opens with a number. A fine. Up to a hundred thousand euros, they say, maybe a slice of global turnover, and the clock is running. It is a good number. It is built to make a busy person sign something this quarter. And it is the wrong way to sell you anything, because the moment your lawyer asks how often that fine has actually been levied, the fear leaks out of the room and the deal goes with it.

I have watched this play out all year. The European Accessibility Act came into force in June 2025. The first real enforcement action in the EU did not land until that November, in France, and the first ruling, against a supermarket, went to the defendant. That is not a criticism of the law. It is just the truth about new regulation: the threat arrives years before the consequences do. A buyer who has read one headline about a fine has usually read the next one about the case being dismissed.

So if you sell the fear, you are selling something your buyer can check, and it does not survive the checking.

What survives the checking is evidence

Here is what does not evaporate. When a regulator, a litigant, or an acquirer asks an organisation to show its work, the organisation either has the documentation or it does not. The fine is a maybe. The request for evidence is a certainty. It is the part of every one of these regimes that does not depend on enforcement appetite at all, because producing the documentation is the duty, whether or not anyone is fined for failing to.

That is the thing worth buying, and it is the thing we sell. Not a scare about a penalty that may never come, but a dated, attested record of exactly what a machine sees when it reads your site, and a record of who stands behind it. You can hand it to a regulator. You can hand it to a buyer doing due diligence. You can hand it to your own board. It is the documentation the duty asks for, made so a machine can read it and verify it. We do not grant you compliance with anything; that stays your legal duty. We give you the evidence that duty expects you to hold.

Why the evidence matters more every month

Two things make this urgent, and neither is a fine.

The first is that machines now read your content more than people do, and they read it badly. When researchers at the Columbia Journalism Review tested eight AI search engines, the tools gave incorrect citations to more than sixty percent of news queries. The BBC found that about half of AI-assistant answers about its own reporting carried significant problems, and that some quotes were altered or invented outright. Sit with that. The machine that is increasingly the first thing to describe your business to a customer gets it wrong more often than it gets it right, and you have no say in the matter unless your content is structured so the machine cannot misread it, and signed so the machine knows it is genuinely yours.

The second is that the evidence is quietly becoming the price of entry. In public procurement, a supplier who cannot produce an accessibility conformance statement is not argued with; the bid is set aside before anyone reads the rest of it. That pattern is years old in government buying, and it is now spreading to how every serious buyer treats AI-generated and AI-read content. From August 2026, the EU expects AI-generated content to carry machine-readable marking. The organisations that already hold the evidence will keep winning the work. The ones still planning to get to it will watch a competitor's signed record clear a gate theirs cannot.

Who this is actually for

Not everyone needs this, and I would rather say so than pretend otherwise.

If you sell on price and rankings, and being misread by a machine costs you a click you will win back tomorrow, an attested evidence chain is more than you need. Buy rankings. There are cheaper people for that.

This is for the organisations where being misread by a machine, or being unable to produce a record, carries real cost. A bank, where every published claim is a compliance surface. A healthcare or pharmaceutical publisher, where an AI getting the dose wrong is not a marketing problem. A law firm, where trust is the entire product and "what does the AI say about us" is now a board-level question. A public body, where the accessibility obligation is statutory and the audit trail is half of what procurement is buying. For these, machine-trustworthy is not a nice-to-have. It is a documented obligation, and the freelancer down the road who sells rankings cannot produce the chain that satisfies it.

That is the line. We sell evidence, so we sell where evidence is required.

The two questions a machine should be able to answer

Strip all of it back and an audit answers two questions an agent will ask about your content, on your behalf, whether you are in the room or not. What does this actually say. And who stands behind it.

Get those two right and the machine cites you correctly, the regulator finds the record in one step, and the acquirer's diligence team ticks the box instead of opening a query. Get them wrong and the machine guesses, the regulator finds a gap, and the buyer assumes the worst. The fine, if it ever comes, is the least of it.

So we do not lead with the fine. We lead with the outcome: you will know exactly what a machine sees when it reads your content, and you will hold a dated, attested record you can show anyone who asks. That is worth having on a quiet day, and it is worth a great deal on a bad one.
