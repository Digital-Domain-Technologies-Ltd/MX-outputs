The Conversation Agencies Are Not Having With Their Clients

17 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

The call comes in from a client you have worked with for three years. They want to know why their products are not showing up when their sales team uses AI tools to research suppliers in their category. They have a clean site, decent SEO scores, and they have been with you long enough that they trust you. They expect you to have an answer.

What do you say?

Most agencies do not have the answer yet. Not because the question is new - AI agents have been reading the web for longer than people realise - but because the tools to score it, describe it, and fix it are new.

What changed, and when

Over the 2025 holiday period, Adobe measured a 700% surge in AI-referred traffic to retail sites. Those visitors converted at 30% higher rates than human-referred traffic. That is not a search trend. That is a structural shift in how buyers find and evaluate products.

In the first eight days of January 2026, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Anthropic each launched an AI agent with direct commerce or workflow capability. In eight days. Not eight months.

The agents doing that reading are not browsers. They do not run JavaScript. They do not scroll to see more. They do not read a page the way a person does. They read the structure of the page, the metadata the publisher declared, the files the site makes available to machine discovery, and the signals that say whether the content is worth citing.

Most websites fail that reading in ways their owners cannot see, because the failures are invisible to humans. The alt text is missing from images. The heading hierarchy is wrong. The structured data says one thing and the page text says another. The robots.txt blocks the agents that matter. The discovery files that tell an agent what the site covers and what its content is for are absent.

Your client is not failing because their content is bad. They are failing because their site was built for human visitors, and the machines that now matter have a different set of requirements.

What you are really offering

Step back from the audit for a moment and look at what the client is actually buying. Their CMS made them a promise when they signed up: we will make your content visible to your audience in a way that benefits you. That promise was written for an audience with eyes. The audience now includes machines, the platform never reopened the deal, and the client is the one quietly absorbing the loss. I wrote about that from their side of the table in The Contract Your CMS Signed.

Here is what it means for you. The client is not going to win that argument with their platform, and a lot of platforms will try to sell the fix back as a premium tier. So they reach for the other option: they find someone who will simply keep the contract. That someone can be you. "We will make sure the machines that now decide whether your buyers find you can actually read your site, and we will prove it on a dated report" is an offer a platform vendor is structurally slow to make, and one an agency can make straight away.

That is why this is worth planning now, before the call comes in. The agencies that decide early what their machine-readiness offer is, what it costs, who delivers it, and how the re-audit keeps it honest, are the ones ready when a client rings to ask why a competitor is getting cited and they are not. The ones still improvising when that call lands will hand the account to whoever rehearsed.

What a machine-readiness audit gives you

A scored report across ten dimensions: performance as a machine sees it, accessibility to WCAG 2.1 AA, SEO structure, semantic HTML, structured data quality, image efficiency, security headers, content consistency, discovery file coverage, and machine pipeline survivability. That last dimension is the one that catches most sites: eleven checks for whether the content a site publishes actually reaches an agent that does not run JavaScript, in a form the agent can act on.

The report also checks AI agent access directly, scores the site's AI usage declaration, and flags EU AI Act Article 50 readiness.

It also names the platform and CMS behind the site and benchmarks the score against peers on the same stack. That lets you tell a client whether their gap is a setting they can change or a ceiling their platform imposes, which is the difference between a quick win and a re-platforming conversation, and it is exactly the judgement their CMS vendor is the last party able to give them.

It lands as a client-ready PDF. Scored, dated, named findings, effort estimates, and the outcome each fix delivers. It is something your client can show to a developer, a digital director, or a regulator. Something they can track over time.

The retainer argument is the re-audit. Each re-run shows what moved and what held, so the gap is visible until it is closed. That is what continuous monitoring looks like, and it is the model that keeps an agency in the conversation at renewal.

The August 2026 moment

From 2 August 2026, EU AI Act Article 50 expects machine-readable marking on AI-generated content. That is not a distant regulatory aspiration. That is a date that is already in procurement timelines for clients in financial services, healthcare, legal, and public sector.

Your clients in those sectors need a dated, attested record of their content and AI governance posture before that date. If you can produce it, you become part of the compliance conversation rather than a dependency it works around.

If you cannot, a competitor will.

The practical picture

You do not need to build this from scratch. The methodology and the tooling exist, and the engagement model is straightforward: you run the audit, the report carries your brand, you deliver the findings and the remediation work.

Training is part of it too. The gap between a finding and a fix is usually not a technical problem; it is a vocabulary problem. A development team that understands what machine readiness means, and why the heading hierarchy matters to an agent rather than just to a human, closes findings in weeks rather than quarters. A half-day session pays for itself on the first retainer it keeps.

The agencies building this into their stack first are the ones having a different conversation at renewal.

What it looks like in a client meeting

You pull up the report for their site. You show them what an agent sees when it reads their home page. You show them the five findings that are blocking machine citation, the effort to close each one, and what their score looks like against comparable sites in their category. You show them what it looks like on re-audit in three months.

That is a conversation an agency can own. It is not about rankings or impressions. It is about whether the buyers your client wants to reach can find them when a machine is doing the searching.

That conversation is available to you right now. Most of your competitors are not having it yet.
