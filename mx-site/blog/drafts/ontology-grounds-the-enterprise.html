Ontology Grounds the Enterprise. MX Grounds What Leaves It.

20 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

Tony Seale, the Knowledge Graph Guy, has been making a point I keep coming back to: ontology is having its moment. Databricks shipped Genie Ontology. Google Cloud launched a Knowledge Catalog. Snowflake convened its rivals around Open Semantic Interchange. The three biggest data platforms on earth are racing to put a semantic layer under their agents.

He is right about why. Generative AI is brilliant and ungrounded, and an ontology is the surest way to stop it guessing: one coherent layer of meaning an agent can actually reason over. Precision over plausibility.

I agree with all of it. I want to add the half of the picture his frame leaves out.

Tony lays the work out in three phases. Phase 1 was the agent wave, the open web folded into the model. Phase 2, where we are now, is the consolidation of context, everyone building the ontologies and semantic layers their agents will stand on. Phase 3 is the Agentic Web, agents moving between customer and supplier, bank and regulator. His line about that phase is the one I cannot put down:

They will only travel where meaning is open enough to follow.

That sentence is the whole problem with stopping at the semantic layer.

An ontology grounds an agent inside the walls of the company that built it. Your customers, your processes, your own messy reality, modelled so your own agents can reason. That is real work, and Tony is right that it is harder than the headline. But the moment a document leaves the building, a quote to a customer, a filing to a regulator, a spec to a supplier, it lands in front of an agent that has no access to your ontology and never will. Tony says it himself: you can't outsource your ontology. Which means the agent on the other side cannot borrow yours either.

So what grounds the file when it travels?

That is the layer I work on. We call it MX, machine experience. Where UX asks if a human can understand this, MX asks the same of a machine. It is metadata that records a file's provenance, its context, and how it is meant to be used, and it travels with the file. Not a model of your enterprise. A passport for each document, readable by any agent, built on open standards, needing no shared ontology between the two sides.

The two layers do different jobs, and a working Agentic Web needs both:

  • The semantic layer grounds agents inside the enterprise. That is Tony's domain. Palantir, Fabric, Databricks.
  • MX grounds content once it crosses the boundary. Open, carrier-neutral, vendor-free.

There is a warning buried in Tony's post that makes this urgent. He predicted that incumbents would cut ontology loose from its roots and sell it back as a proprietary feature, and then watched it happen. The same capture is coming for machine-readability. If the platforms get to decide what "readable by a machine" means, every file that leaves your building ends up speaking a dialect somebody else owns. That is exactly the IP leakage and lock-in Tony warns about, one layer up.

Which is why the metadata layer has to be community-owned, not vendor-defined. That is what The Gathering is for: a community standards body that defines MX in the open, the way Schema.org gave the public web a shared vocabulary, so the meaning that travels between companies is nobody's proprietary moat.

And it is why grounding that crosses boundaries takes more than structure. A file an agent has never seen needs three things before it is safe to act on. It has to be readable, which is MX. It has to be attributable to whoever published it, which is Reginald, the registry and trust layer. And its load-bearing claims have to be corroborated by sources that independently state them. Readable, attributable, corroborated. Structure alone gets you the first; the Agentic Web needs all three.

Tony is right that we are in Phase 2, everyone building the semantic layer. He is right that what you build there decides how far your agents travel in Phase 3. I would put it sharper. Your ontology decides how well your agents reason at home. The open metadata on your content decides whether they are allowed out of the house at all.

Build the semantic layer. Then make sure what leaves it can be read by an agent that was never invited in.
