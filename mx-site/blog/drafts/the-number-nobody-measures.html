The Number Nobody Measures

25 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

I read a post this week about Adobe, though it was not really about Adobe. The writer made a point I have not been able to put down. Finance, they said, can tell you exactly what the software costs. It is one of the biggest lines in the budget, costed to the detail. What nobody can tell you is what it is worth. And when the renewal lands, the cost shows up with nothing to weigh it against, so it looks too high, the way any figure does when nothing sits beside it.

That is the whole trap in two sentences. You measure the cost to the penny and the value not at all. Then you hold a meeting to decide the future of the thing, and the only number on the table is the invoice.

A cheaper vendor does not fix it

The reflex, when the invoice looks too high, is to go shopping. Swap the platform. Find someone undercutting the incumbent. The writer's sharpest line was that this changes nothing, and they are right. You move to the new tool, you keep the same small team doing the same generic work, and you have measured the new cost just as precisely and the new value just as not-at-all. A year later you are back in the same room with a different logo on the invoice and the same single number on the table.

The platform was never the problem. The investment stopped at the licence. Nobody funded the work that turns a licence into results, and nobody set up a way to measure the results it did produce. So the tool gets judged on a cost you can see against a value you never looked at, and it loses, every time, for a reason that has nothing to do with it.

Content has exactly this problem, and it is getting worse

I sell into this gap, so I see it in my own corner of it. An organisation can tell you what it spends on content to the pound. The agency retainer, the CMS licence, the headcount, the AI tooling that has appeared on the budget in the last two years. What it cannot tell you is whether any of it works, because the working is unmeasured. Can a machine actually read this content. Does an AI assistant describe the business correctly when a customer asks. Is there a record, anywhere, of what was published and who stands behind it. These are value questions, and almost nobody has a number for them.

So content spend sits in the same renewal trap. Measured cost, unmeasured worth. And it is getting worse fast, because the reader has changed. Machines now read your content more than people do, and they do it badly. When researchers at the Columbia Journalism Review tested eight AI search tools, they handed back incorrect citations to more than sixty percent of news queries. That is the thing increasingly describing your business to a customer, getting it wrong more often than right, and you have no number that even tells you it is happening.

The fix is to make the value a number too

You do not escape the trap by arguing that the value is real. Everyone in that meeting already suspects it is. You escape it by measuring it, so the worth stops being a feeling and becomes a number that can sit next to the cost.

That is the work, and for content it comes apart cleanly. The first part is making the content machine-readable, so the value question "can a machine use this" has an actual answer you can show. That is what MX is: metadata that travels with a file and tells any machine what it is, where it came from, and how to use it. The second part is attestation, so the question "who stands behind this, and is it current" has an answer a machine can check. That is what Reginald adds: a dated, signed record that the content is genuinely yours and is the current version.

Readable, then trustworthy. Put together, they turn the unmeasured side of the ledger into something you can hold up. An audit shows exactly what an agent sees when it reads your site, page by page. A registry record gives you a dated artefact you can put on the table beside the invoice. Now the renewal meeting has two numbers in it, not one, and the conversation is finally about value instead of just cost.

And because the record sits on an open standard, you are not buying a cage. The format is public, so you could walk and take your content with you. What you keep paying for is the part nobody can rebuild from the spec: a live picture, for your own site, of what machines attest about your content and how agents actually read it, put together from both sides at once. That picture is the number. It is your data, it can run under your own brand if you come through a partner, and it is exactly what the renewal meeting was missing.

It matters beyond the renewal room, too. The same record that proves value internally is the documentation an outside party asks for. The EU AI Act expects AI-touched content to carry machine-readable marking from August 2026. A procurement team setting your bid aside wants a conformance statement before it reads the rest. An acquirer's diligence team wants to see what you can prove. In every one of those rooms, the organisation that measured its content value walks in with the number, and the one that only ever measured the cost walks in with an apology.

What I would do with the renewal meeting

If you are heading into a "do we keep paying for this" conversation about anything in your content or AI stack, and the only number on the table is what it costs, do not start by defending the spend. You will lose, because you are arguing a feeling against a figure. Start by getting the other number. Measure what the thing is actually worth, beginning with the part an agent can check for you: what your content says, and whether a machine can read it and trust it.

That is the outcome I sell, and it is worth having on a quiet day and worth a great deal on a bad one. You will know exactly what a machine sees when it reads your content, and you will hold a dated, attested record you can show anyone who asks, the board, the regulator, the buyer, the acquirer.
