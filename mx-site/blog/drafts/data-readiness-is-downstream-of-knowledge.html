Data Readiness Is Downstream of Knowledge Readiness

17 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

A post went round LinkedIn this week that states a problem better than most white papers manage. It opens with a line that should stop any team mid-sprint:

Most of the data feeding your agentic AI isn't in your database.

And then the turn:

It's embedded in thousands of decisions people make every day without writing them down.

That is the whole problem in two sentences, and most fixes sail straight past it.

The four steps, and what they assume

The post is a response to recent McKinsey guidance on preparing your data for agentic AI. The guidance offers four steps: identify workflows, modernise architecture, improve data quality, build governance. None of that is wrong. The author's point is sharper than wrong or right. Every one of those steps quietly assumes the organisation already understands how its own work gets done.

To identify a workflow you have to know how decisions are actually made. To improve quality you have to know what "correct" looks like. To build governance you have to know which rules should be enforced. Each step takes the answer as a given and then sets about cleaning the data around it. The answer is the thing that is missing.

The contract-management story

The example in the post is the part I keep returning to. A leader went looking for those answers inside a contract-management deployment and found they were not documented anywhere. The edge cases in the data surfaced rules that existed nowhere in writing.

Experienced reviewers were applying quality standards that had never been formally defined.

The model had learned those patterns by watching the reviewers. But whose definition of good was it copying: the most experienced reviewer, or simply the most common one? No one could say. Making the logic explicit forced the team to define a standard they had only ever assumed.

This is the moment every organisation hits and most mistake for a tooling problem. The rules were real. They were doing real work. They just lived in people's heads, and an agent trained on the residue of those decisions inherits the judgement without the reasoning, and nobody can audit what they cannot read.

The line that names it

The post lands on a sentence I wish more boards had pinned to the wall:

Data readiness is downstream of knowledge readiness.

That is exactly the right ordering. You cannot clean your way to an agent that understands your business if the understanding was never written down. The author finishes by naming the real prerequisite:

The real prerequisite is making your organization's decision logic visible… who decides what, under what conditions, using what evidence.

Until that's explicit, you're cleaning and governing information without ever defining what the agent is supposed to understand.

I agree with every word. And the diagnosis stops one step short of the cure, which is what I work on.

Making it explicit is necessary, not sufficient

Suppose you do the hard thing. You sit the reviewers down, you surface the rules, you write the decision logic out: who decides, under what conditions, on what evidence. You now have the knowledge that was missing. Where does it go?

If the answer is a policy document, a wiki page, or a slide deck, you have not finished the job; you have moved the problem. Knowledge written down once and stored away from the work it governs decays the moment the two are separated. The agent acts on a contract, the rule that should have governed the action sits three systems away in a document nobody fetched, and within a quarter you are back where you started, with logic that exists somewhere in principle and nowhere at the point of decision.

The knowledge has to travel with the work. That is the part the post does not get to, and it decides whether any of the upstream effort holds.

Where the logic has to live

This is the whole of what MX is for. MX is metadata that records a file's provenance, the context that produced it, and how it is meant to be used, and it travels with the file. When you make a rule explicit, you do not file it; you attach it. The unit you attach it as is a cog: a carrier-neutral record that stays put whether the work is a markdown file, a row in a database, or a field in a PDF.

So the reviewer's standard stops being tacit and becomes a thing the agent reads on arrival. The condition under which a decision applies travels alongside it. The evidence a judgement rests on is named in the record, not reconstructed after the fact. When an agent acts, the rule it applied is legible, and the action points back to the policy that authorised it. That is the difference between an agent that has merely absorbed your habits and one you can actually govern: the first is a black box trained on residue, the second carries its reasoning in a form you and an auditor can both read.

The two properties do separate work. MX makes the decision logic machine-readable, so an agent can find and apply the right rule at the moment it acts. REGINALD, the registry that runs on the same open standard, makes it attributable and tamper-evident, so when someone later asks which rule was in force and who set it, the answer is a verifiable record rather than a reconstruction. Readable, then trustworthy. The first lets the agent understand; the second lets you prove what it understood.

The order that actually works

The post has the sequence right, and it reads cleanly once the missing step is added. Knowledge readiness comes before data readiness: you cannot govern what you have not made explicit. But explicit comes before useful: knowledge written down and stored apart from the work decays back into the drawer it came from. The order that holds is make it explicit, make it machine-readable, and make sure it stays attached to the work it governs.

The author's closing question was what the agent is supposed to understand. The answer is not a cleaner database. It is your decision logic, written down in units that carry their own meaning and their own proof, sitting right beside the work, ready to brief whatever reads it next. Get that right and data readiness takes care of itself, because the information finally has something to be ready for.
