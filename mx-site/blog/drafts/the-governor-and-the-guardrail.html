The Governor and the Guardrail

26 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 8 min read

There is a brass object that solved the first real problem of machine autonomy, and it did it more than two hundred years ago. It sat on top of a steam engine and spun. Two metal balls on hinged arms, turning with the engine, flying outward as the speed rose and dropping back as it fell. When the balls rose they pulled the throttle closed. When they dropped they opened it. The engine watched its own speed and corrected it, and a human did not have to stand there with a hand on the valve.

That object is the flyball governor, and I have started to think of it as the oldest relative in a family that now includes everything I work on. It is not a quaint relic. It is the ancestor of feedback-driven alignment, of reinforcement learning control loops, of the guardrails we wrap around language models. The lineage runs from Watt to Wiener to modern AI safety, and it is more direct than most people building agents today realise.

The same problem, at a higher dimension

The governor answered one question: how do you let a machine act on its own without letting it run away, oscillate, or tear itself apart?

That is still the question. We have just raised the dimensionality. Where the governor controlled the speed of one engine, a modern system has to hold a much larger envelope steady. Inference drift. Reward hacking. The boundary between a grounded answer and a hallucinated one. Safety constraints. Coordination across many agents at once. Provenance and the trust signals that ride with content. Different surface, same problem underneath.

And the answer is always a loop. Measure, compare, correct, stabilise. The governor measured engine speed, compared it to the speed it wanted, and moved the throttle to close the gap. Gradient descent measures the gap between output and target and moves the weights. Reinforcement learning measures a reward and adjusts a policy. An agent with a retrieval layer and a set of guardrails measures its own behaviour against a constraint and pulls itself back inside the line. Victorian engineers built the first closed-loop autonomous controller. The people building AI systems inherited the maths.

Stability is the whole game, and the Victorians learned it first

The governor taught the world a lesson that every control engineer since has had to relearn the hard way. Correct too little and the system drifts. Correct too much and it oscillates, hunting back and forth across the target and never settling. Get the timing wrong, where the correction arrives too late to match the deviation that caused it, and you do not get a wobble. You get a runaway.

Read that back with modern eyes and it is a description of failures I see every week. Under-regularise a model and it drifts, grows overconfident, starts to hallucinate. Over-regularise it and you get something brittle that underperforms on everything. Put latency in the feedback, so an agent or a robot or a tool-using model acts on stale signal, and the errors compound instead of cancelling. This is exactly why AI safety keeps reaching back into 19th-century control theory. The triad has not changed. Only the cost of getting it wrong has gone up.

You cannot govern what you cannot see

Here is a detail I love about the governor. The spinning balls were not just the mechanism. They were the display. An engineer could stand a few feet away and read the internal state of the system off the angle of two pieces of brass. The machine exposed what it was doing, in a form a human could act on, in real time.

We are still trying to recover that property. Attention maps, saliency, activation visualisation, the whole interpretability project is an attempt to get the spinning balls back, to make a model's internal state legible enough to trust. And the same instinct runs through the provenance work I spend my days on. MX metadata layers, the lifecycle and identity tracking in Reginald, the evidence chain that travels with a file. All of it is the same demand the governor satisfied without anyone naming it: a machine has to expose its internal state to stay governable. The governor was the first machine to do it, and it did it in brass.

Alignment without understanding

This is the connection I keep coming back to, and it is the one that should change how you think about building for machines.

The governor did not understand the engine. It had no model of combustion, no concept of steam, no representation of what an engine is for. It enforced a constraint through feedback and nothing more. The understanding lived in the engineers who designed it. The brass just held the line.

Modern alignment works the same way, and we should be honest that it does. A language model does not understand safety in any sense that would satisfy a philosopher. It operates inside feedback-governed boundaries that we set from outside. The governance is external, not internal. That is not a temporary limitation we will engineer away next year. It is the through-line from Watt to Wiener to now: useful autonomy has always been autonomy inside a boundary somebody else drew.

Once you accept that, a lot follows. If the governance is external, then the environment the machine operates in has to carry the rules in a form the machine can actually read. The governor could enforce a boundary because the boundary was encoded in physical form, in the geometry of the arms and the weight of the balls. The engine could not misread it. There was no ambiguity to exploit.

That is the bar. And it is the bar that most of the digital environment fails today.

From a boundary in brass to a boundary in metadata

Walk the connections out and they stop being a metaphor and start being a plan of work.

The governor enforced an envelope: operate freely, but not past this safe limit. We do the same with guardrails, constitutional constraints, policy-based action limits, sandboxed execution, tool-use permissions. Same idea, abstracted off the physical and into the logical.

The governor was also a coordination primitive, though nobody called it that. Steam engines powered factories, and a mill full of engines only works if each one behaves predictably enough to run alongside the others. Take the governors away and the mill tears itself apart. Our systems are mills now. Models calling tools, agents handing work to other agents, robots sharing space with people, inference spread across cloud and edge. Without stable feedback at each node, a multi-agent system collapses into the same chaos a Victorian mill would.

And under all of it sits the lesson the governor taught first. Autonomy requires constraints. Constraints require shared rules. Shared rules require predictable semantics. Predictable semantics require standardisation. That chain is the logic of MX, of The Gathering, of COGs, of Reginald provenance, of every machine-readable contract and agent-safe content structure I argue for. It is also, exactly, the logic that put a piece of brass on top of a steam engine and let it govern itself.

Why this matters for MX

So here is where it lands.

The entire MX thesis is that machines need shared rules, provenance, and predictable semantics to act safely. That is not a new idea I invented. It is the digital continuation of the governor's insight, carried forward two centuries and across one change of material.

The governor proved that a machine can be trusted to act on its own, but only when the environment is structured so its behaviour stays predictable. Victorian engineers structured the physical environment to make that true. They machined the arms, weighted the balls, fixed the geometry, and the boundary held because the world the machine lived in was built to hold it.

I am structuring the informational one. When an agent reads a page, parses a file, or acts on a record, it is operating in an environment too. Right now that environment is mostly unstructured, ambiguous, and easy to misread, which is the digital equivalent of asking the governor to work without the arms. MX is the work of machining the arms. Provenance that says where a thing came from. Metadata that says what it is and how it may be used. Contracts a machine cannot misinterpret because the meaning is encoded, not implied.

The governor could not run away because its world would not let it. That is the standard. Readable, attributable, corroborated content is how we hold a machine to the same standard now. The lineage is direct, and we are simply the next people in it: governor, cybernetics, control theory, alignment, machine-inclusive governance.

Autonomy is only ever safe when the environment is structured for machines to operate predictably. The Victorians worked that out in brass. The rest of the work is ours.

MX is the practice of structuring content so a machine can read it, trust it, and act on it without guessing. If you want that argument in full, the principles are the place to start, and the rest of this blog works through it one piece at a time.
