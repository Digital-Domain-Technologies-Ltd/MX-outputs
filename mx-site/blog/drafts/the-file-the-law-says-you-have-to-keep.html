The File the Law Says You Have to Keep

4 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

Everyone quotes the same EU AI Act date at me. The second of August 2026, the fifteen-million-euro fine, the content-marking rule. It's a real deadline, and we've written about it. But it isn't the one that should worry you most if you build or deploy a high-risk AI system.

There's a second obligation landing around the same time, and it's quieter, because it concerns a file rather than a fine. When a regulator, a notified body, or an acquirer asks to see that file, you either produce it or you don't. Most organisations, right now, can't.

Note: This page describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

What the Act Actually Asks You to Keep

Strip the high-risk rules down to what a system has to have on hand, and you get something familiar to anyone who's shipped a regulated product. Technical documentation, set out in Article 11 and Annex IV: what the system is, how it was built, how it was tested, and what it exists to do. Automatic logs, in Article 12: a running account of what the system did while it worked. And a conformity assessment, in Article 43, before the thing reaches the market.

None of that's exotic. It's a filing requirement. The catch is what the Act means by "automatic" and by "produce". You don't get to write the file the week before the assessment. You're meant to keep it, accurately, the whole time, and to hand it over in a form someone else can check.

That's the quiet deadline, and the question it poses is plain: when they ask, can you show them?

Why a Filing Cabinet Won't Save You

This is where the paperwork turns into a machine problem.

What the Act wants isn't a folder of PDFs a human assembled at the end. It's a trail: who decided this, on what input, under which rule, at what moment, and who answers for it afterwards. Spread that across a training pipeline, a monitoring stack, a ticketing system, and a few people's laptops, and pulling it together after the fact is slow, and worse, unprovable. A folder someone assembled last week can't prove nobody's touched it since.

The reader of that file, increasingly, isn't a person either. It's an assessor's tooling, a regulator's parser, an acquirer's due-diligence script. They need the record in a form a machine can walk, not one a human skims. If your evidence only makes sense to a human reading slowly, you've built it for the wrong reader.

Evidence, Not a Shield

I want to be careful here, because the honest version of this is the one that holds up.

Nothing we make grants you compliance. That stays your organisation's legal duty, and anyone selling you AI Act compliance in a box is selling you something that collapses the first time an assessor leans on it. A certificate is evidence an assessor reads on the way to a decision; it doesn't end the conversation.

What we make is the record the law expects you to be able to produce. Machine-readable, so the assessor's tooling can walk it. Tamper-evident, so "we didn't change it afterwards" is a property of the file rather than a promise you make with a straight face. Two layers doing two jobs: MX makes the record readable to a machine, and REGINALD makes it trustworthy to one.

That distinction, evidence rather than shield, is the whole pitch. It promises less than "we make you compliant", and it happens to be true, which is why it's worth more.

What You Actually Hand Over

Picture the moment the assessment lands. The notified body asks for your technical file and your logs.

In the version most organisations are drifting towards, that's a scramble. People stop what they're doing, dig through systems, stitch a document together, and hope the gaps don't show.

In the version we build, it's already there. Every artefact your high-risk system produces carries its own trail as it's made: what the model did, what a human checked, what rule ran, tied back to the exact source that produced it. REGINALD holds the attested bundle. You hand over one dated, verifiable file instead of assembling it under deadline pressure, and you can show it wasn't touched after the fact.

That's the outcome you're buying: when they asked, you had the answer, and it held up.

The date's coming either way. The only real question is whether the file exists before someone asks to see it.

If you build or deploy a high-risk AI system and you're not certain what you'd hand over today, that's worth finding out while the deadline is still ahead of you, not behind you.
