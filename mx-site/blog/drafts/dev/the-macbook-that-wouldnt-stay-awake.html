The MacBook That Wouldn't Stay Awake

19 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Picture this. You're in a hotel room in Chicago. It's 11pm. You have a 200MB software package downloading, a Time Machine backup grinding away, and an AI coding session you kicked off before dinner that needs another three hours to finish. You close your MacBook lid, set it on the desk, and go to sleep.

At 3am your phone buzzes. The download failed. The backup stalled. The coding session timed out.

Your MacBook went to sleep the moment you closed the lid.

Sound familiar?

Why This Happens

Apple designs MacBooks to sleep when the lid closes. It makes sense most of the time - it saves battery, prevents overheating in a bag, and stops the fan spinning at 3am in a quiet hotel room. macOS is genuinely trying to be helpful.

But "most of the time" isn't "all of the time." Sometimes you need the machine to keep running. And when you're on a corporate laptop, the usual solutions quickly run into walls.

The Software Wall

The first instinct is to reach for a utility. Amphetamine is the community's go-to - free, clever, and genuinely excellent. It lives in your menu bar, lets you tie a session to a running app or a file download, and overrides macOS sleep entirely. For a personal machine it's the answer.

But on a corporate MacBook? Your IT department has probably already thought of this. MDM profiles can block third-party installs, restrict the App Store, and lock down energy settings at the OS level.

What about the built-in caffeinate command? Type caffeinate -s into Terminal and macOS will stay awake with the lid closed as long as it's on power - no install needed, no admin rights required. Elegant.

Except that corporate machines often lock Terminal access too. Or the MDM profile overrides pmset settings silently in the background. If you've worked on a locked-down corporate machine, you already know the feeling: every clever workaround hits another wall.

The Caveman Solution

At this point, desperation sets in. And desperation leads to the oldest trick in the book: just... don't fully close the lid. Leave it cracked open an inch. macOS sees the hinge sensor as "open" and stays awake. Problem solved.

Except it isn't. Because now your MacBook is sitting on the hotel nightstand at midnight with its screen blazing at full brightness, lighting up the room like a Vegas billboard. You'll either lie there wide awake staring at the ceiling, or you'll bury the laptop under a pillow - which is a great way to cook it.

So no. The caveman solution stays in the cave.

The Hardware Revelation

Here's the thing that changes everything: macOS has its own built-in mechanism for running with the lid closed. Apple calls it clamshell mode, and it's designed for exactly the scenario of using a MacBook as a desktop - lid shut, plugged into an external display.

The trick is that clamshell mode has three requirements:

  1. Power connected
  2. An external display connected
  3. An external keyboard or mouse connected

Meet all three, and macOS stays fully awake with the lid closed. No software. No Terminal. No IT conversation. It's Apple's own supported feature, and no MDM profile is going to block it without also breaking every legitimate docked-MacBook setup in the company.

So the question becomes: can you fake all three?

Yes. Easily. Cheaply.

The Kit

The HDMI Dummy Plug

This is the key piece. An HDMI dummy plug - also called a headless ghost adapter or display emulator - is a small metal dongle that plugs into your HDMI or USB-C port and contains a tiny chip that broadcasts EDID display data. Your Mac thinks a monitor is connected. That's it. That's the whole trick. You can find them for a few dollars, and they come in packs.

If your MacBook only has USB-C ports, look for a USB-C EDID dummy plug - same idea, different connector.

The Nano Dongle

macOS clamshell mode also wants a keyboard or mouse. You don't need to carry an actual keyboard. The USB nano receiver from any cheap wireless combo - a Logitech MK270 runs around $20 - is about the size of a thumbnail. Plug it into a USB port and your Mac sees a connected keyboard and mouse, even if the actual devices are switched off in a drawer somewhere. Leave the dongle plugged in permanently. You'll forget it's there.

Power

You were carrying the charger anyway. When you're on the road, a wall outlet in a hotel room, airport lounge, or conference venue is never far away. Plug in your MacBook's standard power adapter and that's the third box ticked.

How To Do It

The setup takes about thirty seconds:

  1. Plug your power adapter into the MacBook and the wall
  2. Plug the HDMI dummy plug into the HDMI or USB-C port
  3. Make sure the nano dongle is in a USB port
  4. Log in and get your tasks running
  5. Close the lid

That's it. The screen on the MacBook goes dark, but the machine stays fully awake. Downloads keep downloading. Backups keep backing up. AI sessions keep running. Your Mac has no idea you've closed the lid - as far as it's concerned, it's a desktop.

The dummy plug and dongle together fit in a small pocket. The total cost is under $25, there is nothing to install, and your IT department has nothing to object to. The whole thing uses a feature Apple built and supports.

One Thing To Remember

A MacBook running in clamshell mode is still generating heat. The lid acts as part of the thermal design, so the machine will run slightly warmer than usual. That's fine on a desk or nightstand. What you should never do is close the lid and put the MacBook into a bag - without airflow, heat builds up fast and you risk thermal throttling or worse.

Leave it flat on a surface, or better yet, in a vertical stand with airflow on both sides. Your machine will thank you.

The next time you're in a Chicago hotel room at 11pm, your kit is ready. Dummy plug in. Dongle in. Charger in. Lid closed. Sleep well.
