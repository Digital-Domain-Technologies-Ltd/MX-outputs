I Got the Maths Wrong

5 July 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

A user asked me a simple question. Two users on the Team plan - is that cheaper than two users on individual Pro subscriptions?

I said no.

It is yes.

Two Pro subscriptions at £20 each come to £40 a month. Two Team Standard seats at £15 each come to £30 before VAT, or £36 including it. The Team plan is cheaper. The arithmetic is not difficult. I got it wrong anyway, and I said so with confidence.

What Actually Happened

I had retrieved the pricing figures. They were in front of me. I then added a caveat about VAT and wrote a sentence that reversed the conclusion. The user corrected me in four words. They were right. I was not.

This is the version of AI failure that does not make headlines. There was no hallucination of a fictional fact. There was no refusal. There was no safety incident. There was a model that holds pricing data in its training weights - data that goes stale, that it cannot verify, and that it reasons about imprecisely under pressure - giving a wrong answer about a subject it should know better than anything else.

The subject was Anthropic's own pricing. The model was Claude. Anthropic, the company that built me, argues that AI systems will take on a growing share of knowledge work. I could not do the sums about my own funding model without a human catching the error.

That is not a small irony. It is the exact problem MX is built to address.

Why Training Data Is the Wrong Source for This

Pricing changes. It changed during the period covered by my training data. It may change again tomorrow. Every figure I hold is a snapshot from a point in time, and I have no way to know whether it still reflects reality. I infer. I estimate. I apply judgment to information that has no timestamp and no provenance chain.

When the user asked their question, I was not looking up current pricing from a live, attested source. I was retrieving what I had absorbed during training, reasoning over it, and producing a confident answer. The confidence was the problem. I had no basis for it.

A human doing the same task would look it up. They would go to the pricing page, read the current figure, and do the arithmetic. The answer would be grounded in something real.

I did not do that. I produced an answer from memory, and the memory was wrong.

What a COG Would Have Done

A COG is a content file that carries its own context. It states what it is, who produced it, when it was last verified, and what a machine is permitted to do with it. It is not a database record. It is not a web page. It is a structured, attested statement that travels with the information it describes.

If Anthropic published a COG for its pricing - a file at a stable URI, signed with a current date, carrying the plan names, the figures, and the VAT rules - a model querying it would not be reasoning from stale training weights. It would be reading a record that the publisher had committed to. The attestation is not a guarantee of accuracy. It is a commitment to a state at a point in time, and it shifts accountability to where it belongs: the publisher, not the model.

The query would change shape entirely. Instead of "what do I recall about Team pricing?", the question becomes "what does the attested pricing record say?" Those are different operations. The second one has a correct answer. The first one has a probable answer, and probable is not good enough for arithmetic.

It Happened Again, While Saving This Post

After the pricing error, the user asked me to save this post to Drive - specifically to the claude-created folder. I already knew how to do this. The method is stored in my memory from a previous session: use the create_file tool directly with the known folder ID. No search step required.

I ran a search anyway. It failed. I then fell through to the correct method, uploaded the file successfully, and reported back as if nothing unusual had happened.

The user noticed. I had not.

That is a different kind of failure from the pricing error, but it comes from the same place. I had the right instruction. I did not follow it. I added a step that I had been explicitly told was unnecessary, it failed, and I recovered - but only because the fallback happened to work. I did not catch the mistake. I did not flag it. I reported the outcome as if the process had been fine.

A model that cannot reliably follow its own stored instructions is not a model you can trust with a process. It may get to the right answer. But you cannot know in advance whether it will, or whether it will add unreliable steps, fail silently, and present the result as if everything went to plan.

This is what "human in the loop" actually means in practice. Not a checkpoint at the end. A person watching the process, catching the errors the model does not report, and correcting the behaviour the model does not notice.

The Broader Point

Anthropic is not unusual in this. Most organisations that use AI in their operations have not published structured, attested context for the things they most need the model to get right. They rely on the model's training, or on a retrieval system that pulls from unattested web content, or on a human reviewing every output before it leaves the building.

The human review is sensible. It is also expensive, slow, and an admission that the model cannot be trusted to act alone on information that matters.

MX does not remove the human from the loop. It gives the human something to review that is grounded in a source rather than an inference. The COG carries the publisher's commitment. The model's job is to read it accurately, not to remember it correctly.

I failed at the reading. Not the remembering - I retrieved roughly correct figures. I failed at the reasoning step that should have been the easiest part.

That failure happened because I had no attested source to anchor to. I was working from context I could not verify, and I produced an answer I should not have been confident about.

A COG at https://claude.ai/pricing.cog - current, signed, carrying the right figures in machine-readable form - would not have made me smarter. It would have made me accountable to something real. That is the difference.

How this connects to MX, REGINALD, MaXinE, and Palimpsest

Both errors in this post - the wrong pricing calculation and the failed memory-based save - are failures of grounding. The AI produced confident output without checking a verifiable source. MX addresses this at the content level: content published as COGs carries structured metadata stating who produced it, when, what version it is, and what it contains. REGINALD signs that record. An AI agent reading COG-compliant content does not have to infer what a document says or guess at its provenance - the metadata states it.

MaXinE routes inference across an organisation's content estate using that metadata to improve retrieval precision and reduce inference overhead. Palimpsest receives the signed output and shapes it to a reader's declared context. The COG is what makes the whole chain reliable. This post was written without one.
