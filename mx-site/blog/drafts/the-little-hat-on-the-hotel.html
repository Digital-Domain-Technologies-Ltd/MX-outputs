The Little Hat on the Hotel

18 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 5 min read

I checked into a hotel in France and the sign over the door spelled it hotel with a circumflex, that small stone hat perched on the o. Most people read straight past it. It's worth stopping on, because that little hat is a gravestone. It marks the spot where a letter used to live and died, and the letter it's mourning is s.

French keeps doing this. Foret was forest. Ile was isle. Bete was beste, a beast. Pate was paste. Over and over, an s wore away in the mouth until it stopped being pronounced, then stopped being written, and the French - tidy, sentimental - set a little stone hat on the vowel to say something was here once. So hotel was hostel, and hostel goes back to Latin hospitale, a house that takes in guests.

Now here's the part I can't get over. English borrowed that same Latin word - hospitale, a place of shelter for guests and strangers - not once, but again and again, at different dates, and each time it froze the word at the stage it had reached on the day of the crossing. Line them up and you're looking at the same row of stopped clocks I went on about last time, except this time every clock is in English, ticking from the same single source.

Hospital is the early, bookish borrowing, taken close to the Latin and keeping nearly all of it - the h, the s, the p. It didn't even mean a medical place at first; it meant a hospice, a shelter for the poor and the travelling and the sick, which is why the same root gives us hospice and hospitality and the Knights Hospitaller. The medicine came later. The clock stopped early, so the word still wears its full Latin coat.

Hostel is the next clock along. It came through Old French, by which time the Latin had already softened in the mouth - the p gone, the word worn rounder. We borrowed it in the Middle Ages and kept the s, the one that French itself would later bury under a hat. So English hostel is, in a real sense, older than French hotel, even though they're the same word - English froze it before the s fell, France let the s go and built the tombstone.

Hotel is the latest clock, borrowed from modern French in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, after the s had vanished. So English now owns both the version with the s (hostel, the cheap bunk) and the version without it (hotel, the grand one) - the same word, caught twice, centuries apart, and now doing two different jobs and charging two different prices. We even sorted them by class without meaning to.

And there's a fourth clock, almost worn away. Spital. It's hospital with the front bitten off, the way esquire gave squire. You won't order a room in one, but you've walked through it: Spitalfields in London, the various Spittals and Spittlegates scattered across Britain, every one of them once a wayside hospice for travellers. A dead word, still standing in the street signs like a ruin nobody pulled down.

So that's four English words - hospital, hostel, hotel, spital - and one Latin acorn. Four borrowings, four dates, four stopped clocks, and you can read the age of each one off how much Latin it still wears. The more of the original it keeps, the earlier it came. Etymologists call these doublets, or in this case quadruplets; I think of them as the same traveller checking in four times across a thousand years, signing the register a little differently each time.

Let me leave you with the deep root, because it's the best thing in the whole story. Hospitale comes from hospes, a guest - but also a host, the word meaning both ends of the relationship at once. Go back further, to the ancestor that fed Latin and Greek and our own Germanic tongue, and you reach a single root for the sacred bond between a stranger and the household that takes him in. From it English got guest. Latin got hospes, our host and hotel. And Latin also got hostis - which started as "stranger" and slid, over hard centuries, into "enemy", and handed us hostile.

Sit with that. Guest and hostile are the same word. The stranger at your door is, at root, both the man you are bound to feed and the man who might be coming to kill you, and the language never decided which - it just kept both, the welcome and the wariness, in one breath. Every hotel sign in France is flying a little hat over a dead s and, underneath it, a much older argument about whether to open the door.
