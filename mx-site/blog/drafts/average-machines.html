Average Machines

12 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 3 min read
  • Practitioner
  • Ai Tells
  • Writing

This is interesting. Researchers gave the same prompts to a human writer and five AI models. 61,608 stories later, the machines had quietly agreed on something.

The AIs converged. GPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek, Kimi. Different companies, different training data, different alignment teams, and all of them drifting into the same narrow corner of narrative space: tidy plots, themes spelled out for you, a clean moral waiting at the end like a mint on a hotel pillow.

The humans scattered. Their stories were rarer and stranger, more willing to leave a question hanging. On narrative structure alone, with every word-level style cue stripped away, the classifier separated human from machine 93% of the time. The study is StoryScope (Russell et al., 2026, arXiv:2604.03136), and that one number has been sitting with me all week.

Here's the part I can't put down. These are average machines. They're brilliant at the middle of the distribution and they flinch from the edges, and creativity has always lived at the edges. A model that writes like everyone at once writes like no one in particular, very fluently.

What we did about it

I run a writing pipeline that already catches word-level AI tells: the banned vocabulary, the em-dashes, the negation-pivot two-step. None of it would catch this. A piece can pass every sentence-level check and still read machine-made, because the structure was never being checked.

So now it is. The finding became a deterministic scanner and a house rule. It reads a document's structure rather than its words: a spelled-out moral or takeaway in the closing paragraphs, an ending that restates the opening, sentence and paragraph lengths clustering around the mean, every section opening or closing on the same move. The rule behind it says what a script can't: bold claims stay bold through every rewrite, and a genuinely unresolved question is allowed to remain on the page unresolved.

The first thing I pointed it at was the draft of this post. It flagged my own closing line, a tidy little moral about competence and moats, and it was right. The piece had made its point; naming the point unmade it. That line is gone.

There's a question I can't answer yet: whether the next generation of models converges harder or finally scatters. The training incentives all point toward the middle. If you publish writing, run your last three pieces past one test before you trust them: does the ending earn its place, or does it spell out what you were supposed to think? Read the study, then go look at your closers.
