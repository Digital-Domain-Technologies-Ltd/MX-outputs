The Gravity Well

22 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 4 min read

There is a force at work in every AI system you use. You cannot see it. It does not declare itself. It does not appear in any log file.

But it is shaping every response you receive.

I call it the gravity well.

Here is the physics. Common Crawl - the public dataset that trained most of the large language models you use daily - is 44% English. No other language exceeds 6%. That is not a rounding error. That is a structural imbalance baked into the foundation of modern AI.

What happens to everything else? It gets pulled toward the centre.

A model processing Spanish does not reason in Spanish. It translates your Spanish prompt into an English frame, performs its reasoning there, then translates the answer back. That sounds reasonable until you ask a Spanish lawyer to draft a property contract and the model applies common law assumptions to a civil law jurisdiction. The document looks correct. The register sounds professional. The legal framework is wrong.

The error appears in no log file.

This is the book I have been writing for the past year. The Gravity Well is about what happens to language - all language, including yours - when the machines that now mediate so much of our communication were trained on a corpus that treats English as the default, the norm, the frame through which everything else must pass.

The problems are not exotic. They are everywhere, once you know how to look.

Take German. When an AI tokenises "Krankenversicherungsunternehmen" - health insurance company - it does not process it as a unit. It breaks the word into fragments: "Kranken", "versicherungs", "unternehmen". Fourteen tokens to say what English says in thirteen. That gap compounds across every sentence, every paragraph, every document. German content arrives at AI reasoning with less structural integrity than its English equivalent, not because German is harder, but because the tokeniser was built around English assumptions.

A German researcher discovered the workaround by accident: type "ultrathink" in your prompt and the model gives you more processing power. Ultrathink is not a German word. It is an English word constructed using German compounding rules - a fake English word that mimics foreign grammar to command machines that do not actually think. The irony is not subtle.

Or take Vietnamese.

The word "ma" means ghost. With a different tone mark, it means mother. Another, and it means but. Tomb. Horse. Rice seedling. One syllable. Six meanings. Distinguished entirely by diacritics.

Strip those marks - in a URL, a database field, an email header - and the model guesses on frequency. The most common meaning wins. The others disappear. You cannot recover them from context because it was discarded upstream, before the model ever saw the data.

This is not a Vietnamese problem. It is a provenance problem. The chain of custody for meaning has been broken, and no one built a system to notice.

The book is not a complaint about AI. I have been building digital systems for twenty-five years and I genuinely admire what these machines can do. Statistical pattern-matching that produces coherent, useful responses across thousands of domains is a real achievement.

But admiration does not require blindness.

The gravity well is not neutral. It does not pull every language equally. Norwegian faces diglossia between Bokmål and Nynorsk - two written standards, one AI that treats them as a single thing and defaults to the more common form. Swiss German speakers write Hochdeutsch to machines that have almost no training data in the dialect they actually think in. Arabic users navigate the gap between Modern Standard Arabic and the spoken dialects - a gap that has existed for a thousand years, and that AI is making structurally permanent.

In Tamil, researchers have measured the register collapse directly. The benchmarks exist. The data is there. The machines are averaging away distinctions that speakers spent generations maintaining.

This is what gravity wells do. They do not destroy things dramatically. They compress them, slowly, toward a common centre.

The Gravity Well maps the mechanism. Not to alarm you, but to equip you.

Because there is a fix. It is not clever. It requires no new technology. It is the same discipline that MX has been arguing for since the beginning: explicit declaration. Tell the machine what language you are writing in. Tell it the locale, the jurisdiction, the legal system. Tell it that 01/06/2026 is the first of June, not the sixth of January.

Do not make the machine guess. It will guess toward the centre.

And the centre is not where you are.

The Gravity Well is coming soon. If you want to know when, you know where to find me.
