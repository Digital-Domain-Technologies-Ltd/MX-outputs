TYPO3 and MX: editorial discipline, and a platform that supports it

18 May 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 6 min read

Earlier this year my team audited an enterprise TYPO3 site that runs to 14,264 URLs across ten locales. The site belongs to a multilingual specialist publisher with a long, authoritative article library and a careful editorial workflow. I went into the audit expecting to find a CMS that needed a great deal of work to participate in the Machine Experience layer. I came out with a different impression of what I had been auditing: not the CMS, but the editorial team, supported by a CMS that had stayed out of their way for long enough that their decisions had compounded. The remediation work needed is real, but it is small, and most of it is configuration.

This piece is the short version of what I told the client. It applies to TYPO3 deployments where the editorial team has taken publishing seriously, which is a more specific claim than "any TYPO3 site". The lesson generalises in two directions: to other content teams running TYPO3, and to other CMSes where a comparably disciplined team is at work.

A fair test for a serious CMS

A CMS earns its place in the MX layer by answering one question well: when an automated reader fetches one of your pages, can it tell what the page is, who stands behind it, when it was published, and whether it may be cited? Browser-readability and search-indexability are the easier tests. The agent test is narrower and harsher. The reader gets one HTTP fetch and the bytes that come back. Everything it needs has to be in those bytes.

The two ways a CMS can fail this test are well known. It can fail by hiding content behind client-side JavaScript that the reader will not execute. It can fail by serving content that is technically present but not machine-readable: no schema type, no author, no date, no structural landmarks. TYPO3 passes the first test cleanly. Where work is needed sits in the second.

There is a third failure mode worth naming separately, because it is harder to see and harder to legislate against. The web has grown hostile to automated readers in a way it was not five years ago. Micro-animations that gate content until they finish. Cookie banners that intercept the document on first paint. Pop-ups, toasts, and lazy-loaded sections that only resolve when a real cursor moves. Each one is a small interruption for a human and a brick wall for a machine: the content is technically present, but the furniture sitting in front of it is built only for browsers. The audited site had almost none of this. The article body sits where the markup says it sits, served directly, and a reader that arrives with a script receives the same content a human visitor does. That is a deliberate set of editorial choices the content team made; the platform's contribution was not to overrule them with a default that ships JavaScript-gated content out of the box.

What's already strong on the audited site

The audit looked at the site across a wide spread of agent-readability concerns. Several came back strong. Each one is a choice the editorial team made (the platform did not force them; TYPO3 will happily let a less-careful team ship something quite different), and each one is also a place where TYPO3's defaults and configuration model did not get in the way of that choice.

  • Server-side rendering. The served HTML and the fully-rendered HTML are practically the same. An automated reader that fetches the raw page sees what a browser sees. There is no JavaScript wall between the agent and the content.
  • JSON-LD in the document head. Structured data sits where the reader looks first. Every fetched page receives the structured data immediately, without needing to parse the body.
  • Security headers complete. HTTPS, HSTS, Content-Security-Policy, X-Frame-Options, and X-Content-Type-Options were present on every audited page. That is the table-stakes layer of trust, present here without exception (the headers are a deployment choice rather than a TYPO3 default, so the credit sits with whoever configured the site).
  • BreadcrumbList implemented and valid. Navigation context is machine-readable across the article tree, with every required property present.
  • Hreflang across ten locales. The multilingual architecture maps each language-variant page to its siblings correctly. A multilingual agent receives the right language variant on the first hop.
  • Heading quality and content ratio. A single H1 per page, no level jumps, and pages that are content-heavy rather than overhead-heavy, which keeps agents inside their fetch window.

That is a strong baseline. It comes from the editorial team making a set of disciplined choices over many years, using a platform that lets those choices reach the markup. A different team could have produced a much less complete baseline using the same TYPO3 install. What the audit found is the combination.

Where the MX gap sits, and why it is narrow

The gap sits in the markup, not in the content or the data model. The audited site stores rich editorial metadata in TYPO3's Table Configuration Array, the TCA: every long-form article carries a title, an author or editor, a creation date, and a content category as part of the normal editorial workflow. The metadata is captured. It just is not surfaced in the markup the agent reads, and the audit picked that up across every page in the sample.

Three specific shapes showed up across the five pages the audit sampled.

  • The page type tells the wrong story. Every page in the sample declares "@type": "WebPage" in its JSON-LD, which is Schema.org's most generic content type. The three long-form articles in the audit sample should carry TechArticle (or LearningResource for the more pedagogical pieces). An agent reading WebPage is told it is looking at "a web page on this domain", not at a technical article written by named subject-matter experts for a specialist audience. The ranking and citation layer treats WebPage and TechArticle very differently. The data to support the better type already exists in TCA; the markup does not yet expose it.
  • Required properties are missing. Even on the generic WebPage type, the required name property is absent from the JSON-LD block on every audited page, despite the same string sitting in the <title> element. On the long-form articles, author, datePublished, and about are the three properties an agent needs to cite the content with attribution. None of them require new content; the title, the author or editor relation, the creation date, and the content category are all already in TCA.
  • The governance layer is missing. The site publishes a well-formed robots.txt declaring nine locale-specific sitemaps, and the page-tree sitemaps themselves are complete. That is the layer the audit found in good order. The newer machine-readable governance files are absent: /llms.txt, /llms-full.txt, and /ai.txt all return HTTP 404, and robots.txt carries no entries for GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, or Google-Extended. The eight AI user-agents the audit tested all receive HTTP 200, so the site is open to them in practice but silent on publisher preference.

None of those gaps requires a content migration or a CMS upgrade. They sit in the templating and configuration layer that TYPO3 was designed to make changeable.

What to change, and in what order

The audit's remediation list is short. I have kept the audit's priority order below, with the effort each item realistically takes against the site's existing TYPO3 layout.

  1. Upgrade the JSON-LD @type on long-form article page types. In the layout template that emits the JSON-LD block, branch on the page type and output TechArticle for the long-form articles (LearningResource is defensible for the more pedagogical guides). This is a single Fluid conditional. The data already exists in TCA. Effort: low. Impact: this is the change with the most effect on what an AI assistant can do with the content, because it is what lets the assistant cite the article with attribution rather than just discovering it.
  2. Populate name, author, datePublished, and about on the long-form article JSON-LD block. These fields read from existing TCA columns: the page title, the author or editor relation, the creation or publication date, and the content category. Adding them to the JSON-LD template is a markup change, not a content one. datePublished is the property that most improves an agent's confidence in citing time-sensitive expert content. Effort: low.
  3. Add the required name property to WebPage entities site-wide. The homepage and the section landing pages share the missing-name issue. A single line in the layout template closes that gap across every page that uses the layout. Effort: low.
  4. Upgrade og:type on long-form article pages. Change the Open Graph type from the implicit website to article, and add article:author and article:published_time. Open Graph metadata is read by social platforms and by an increasing share of AI systems that ingest page metadata alongside JSON-LD. Effort: low.
  5. Generate llms.txt from the existing TYPO3 page tree. In TYPO3 v12 and later, this is a Site Set configuration comparable in scope to the existing sitemap generator: it reads the same page tree and the same TCA metadata that already produces the nine locale-specific sitemaps. List the long-form articles with their content type (LearningResource) and publisher, serve as text/html with the content in a <pre> block, and register the file in the sitemap so it enters Common Crawl's index. Effort: moderate, but the pattern is the same one the existing sitemap generator already follows.
  6. Declare AI crawler entries in robots.txt. Even when the policy is Allow: /, which is defensible for authoritative expert content, naming GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, and Google-Extended explicitly signals publisher intent. Effort: a direct file edit, no template dependency.
  7. Three template-layer accessibility fixes that are also MX fixes. The audit recorded 117 WCAG AA issues across the five-page sample. Sixty-four of those trace to fifteen recurring template-level patterns, each of which fixes once and propagates across every page that uses the template. The three with the widest reach: add a skip link to the main page template (resolves WCAG 2.4.1 across the whole site in a single partial change); add <title> and <desc> elements to the inline SVG icon sprite (the audited article pages carry sixty to seventy inline SVG icons each, none with an accessible name); wrap article body content in an <article> element (currently absent on every long-form article in the sample, which removes the landmark screen-reader users and agent parsers both rely on to jump to the editorial content). Effort: medium. Each one closes a WCAG gap and an agent-readability gap with the same change.

The full list above is, in TYPO3 terms, half a day of careful templating and a clean staging deploy. Data layer untouched. Editorial workflow untouched. What changes is what an AI assistant can do with the content: it can cite the articles with attribution, not just discover them.

Verdict

The audit's headline reading is about the combination of a careful team and a CMS that supports careful work. The site's MX-readiness is mostly the editorial team's doing: the decision to render server-side, the decision to capture rich metadata in TCA, the decision to keep the template layer reachable rather than working around it. TYPO3's contribution is real but supporting. The platform offers server-side rendering as the default rather than the exception, holds editorial fields in a structured data model designed for reuse, and exposes a configuration layer (Site Sets, Fluid templates, TypoScript) that is built to be changed without touching content. None of those defaults forced the team's hand; they just did not stand in the way once the team had decided what they wanted to publish.

The audits in which a site struggles tend to have a different combination: a team that delegated structure to the client side, that treated editorial metadata as an SEO afterthought, or that worked around their platform's template layer rather than through it. Sometimes the CMS underneath made those choices easier than the alternative; sometimes the team just did not have the time or the cover to choose differently. The audited site here had a team that chose well and a platform that did not penalise them for it. That combination is why the remediation list is so short.

The MX framing of this is straightforward. A site earns its place in the agent web by serving documents that machines can read, with the data they need to cite, under a governance policy the publisher has declared. The audited site is most of the way there because the editorial team made those choices and the platform supported them. The work that remains is configuration, applied through the TYPO3 layers the team already uses.
