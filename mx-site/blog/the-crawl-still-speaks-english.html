The Crawl Still Speaks English

3 June 2026 · Tom Cranstoun · 7 min read

Most large language models learn the web second-hand, through one archive. It's called Common Crawl: a free, public crawl of the internet that sits underneath the training data of nearly every model you have used. What's in the crawl shapes what a model knows. What's missing from it, a model mostly never learns.

That archive is heavily English. When I first wrote about this, roughly 44 percent of the Common Crawl was English, and no other single language passed 6 percent of the total. Common Crawl's AI Visibility Audit, published this month, puts the latest figure at around 41 percent English. Two years, and the share has barely moved. This isn't a passing imbalance that more data will quietly correct, but a structural one.

I work on a practice called Machine Experience, or MX. The short version: it's the work of making anything you publish - a page, a PDF, an image, a product listing - readable by the machines that now consume it, so no machine has to guess what you meant. Most of this post is about one place that guessing does real harm, which is language.

The part you cannot fix

A few years of argument lie behind the figure above. AI systems aren't simply better at English; they're English-centric by construction. Training data, the reinforcement learning that fine-tunes a model, the human feedback that shapes its manners, and even the prompts that unlock its harder thinking all run in English first. The longer version is in English Dominance in AI Systems, and the same bias one layer down, in how a model breaks a German word into pieces before it can process it at all, is in The Tokenization Trap.

What this means in practice: a model tends to translate a non-English prompt into an English frame before it reasons, then render back. It defaults to English social assumptions, the flat and slightly stilted register a native speaker spots at once. None of that's yours to fix. It lives inside the model, and it'll only change when the people building models decide to think multilingually from the start rather than bolt it on.

So the useful question isn't how to fix the model. It's what a non-English publisher can do, today, to be read and credited correctly by a model that will process in English whatever you give it.

The part you can

The answer is the layer MX works on: stop making the model guess.

An English-centric model fills gaps with English assumptions. The way to fight that is to leave fewer gaps. "Declaring" something, in this sense, means stating it plainly in a place a machine reads, rather than leaving it to infer from the prose. When your content declares its own language and locale, its own author and date, a machine has less to assume and fewer English defaults to reach for. A clean record in German, with the locale stated and the source noted, is easier for a machine to read correctly than the same facts left as prose for it to interpret through an English lens.

Here's the failure in miniature. A model knew Wiener Schnitzel, because the English-language web is full of it, but stalled on Erdäpfelsalat until a colleague explained it was potato salad by an Austrian name. The model's guessing ran out exactly where the English sources did. A declared term - the regional word, its standard-language equivalent, and where the usage comes from - doesn't ask the model to have seen it before. It tells it.

There's a sharper version of the same loss in the diacritics a language carries. English readers drop accents out of habit, and English-centric systems strip them in the plumbing, which in a language like Vietnamese doesn't blur a word but replaces it with several. Worse, there's no agreed rule to lean on: Unicode standardises how an accented character is encoded and keeps the mark, but the lossy step that strips marks was drafted as a standard and withdrawn in 2008, shelved for want of an owner and because the right fold turns out to differ by language. So every system strips its own way. I have written that up on its own in Strip the Marks, Lose the Word; the short point here is that declaring and preserving the marked form is part of not making the model guess.

Provenance survives translation

There's a second cost, and it's the one most publishers miss. When an English-first model paraphrases your German or Polish or Turkish page into an English answer, the credit and the origin go missing in the same step. Your work is reworded into a sentence with no name on it.

Provenance is the fix. By provenance I mean a checkable record of who made something, when, and whether it has changed since - carried with the content rather than asserted on a separate "about" page. When that record is attested, it holds a signature a reader's software can check, so origin is something you can confirm rather than take on trust. A model can still translate you. It can't quietly turn your work into an unsourced English line if the source travels with a provenance claim. For a non-English publisher that isn't a nicety. It's the difference between feeding the answer and vanishing into it. The same argument, in English and for any language, is in Read Is Not the Same as Trusted.

An honest limit

MX is a lever here, not a cure. Its field names are a small English-keyed vocabulary, in the way HTTP headers and HTML attributes have always been English. The keys are English; the values, the content, and the provenance are whatever language you publish in, kept as declared rather than guessed. MX doesn't make an English-first model think in Turkish. It makes your Turkish legible to it, and keeps your name on it.

The model-makers' job is the one those earlier posts named: build multilingually from the ground up. That's still owed, and still mostly unpaid. The publisher's task is smaller and available now - to stop leaving the model to guess. While the crawl still speaks English, that's the lever you hold.

There's a third job, and it's shared. The rules that decide whether a marked word survives a pipeline, and whether a declaration of language and source is honoured, have to be written down and owned by someone. The withdrawn folding standard shows how easily that work stalls. Standards are hard to make, which is the reason to attempt them in the open rather than leave each system to improvise. That's what The Gathering is for: community-led, never vendor-driven, because a rule that decides whose words survive can't belong to whoever profits most from the answer.
