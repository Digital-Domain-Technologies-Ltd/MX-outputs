Agentic Commerce Protocol (29 September 2025)

Overview

OpenAI and Stripe announced the Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP), an open standard for programmatic commerce flows between buyers, AI agents, and businesses. Unlike proprietary agent commerce systems, ACP is open source (Apache 2.0), community-designed, and works across AI agents with existing payment providers.

Key Details

Announcement Date: 29 September 2025 Organisations: OpenAI and Stripe (codevelopers) License: Apache 2.0 (open source) Implementation: Powered "Instant Checkout" in ChatGPT (AP reports OpenAI retired Instant Checkout in March 2026, citing error-prone runs and thin merchant adoption of the fee OpenAI charged; the protocol specification remains open) Availability: U.S. ChatGPT Plus, Pro, and Free users Specification: https://github.com/agentic-commerce-protocol/agentic-commerce-protocol Website: https://agenticcommerce.dev Category: Standards and Protocol Announcements

Key Capabilities

For Businesses:

  • Maintain customer relationships as merchant of record
  • Control which products can be sold and how they're presented
  • Process transactions with existing payment providers (not Stripe-exclusive)
  • Manage how orders are fulfilled
  • Portable across AI agents (not locked to ChatGPT)

For Users:

  • Make purchases directly within AI conversations
  • Natural language checkout ("buy the blue one in size medium")
  • Payment details stored securely with payment provider
  • Order tracking and history within agent interface

For AI Agents:

  • Standard protocol for commerce integration
  • No proprietary API lock-in
  • Portable delegation tokens
  • Support for physical goods, digital goods, subscriptions, and asynchronous purchases

Current Merchants:

  • Live now: Over 1 million Etsy sellers
  • Coming soon: Over 1 million Shopify merchants
  • Onboarding: URBN (Anthropologie, Free People, Urban Outfitters), Ashley Furniture, Coach, Kate Spade, Nectar, Revolve, Halara, Abt Electronics
  • Supporting: Salesforce (announced support in January 2025)

Significance for This Book

Particularly relevant: ACP is the first major open protocol for agent-mediated commerce, directly addressing the EAL delegation and platform lock-in concerns discussed in Chapter 13.

Technical Implementation Insights

Open Standard Approach:

ACP is explicitly designed to be open and interoperable. The protocol specification is published on GitHub under Apache 2.0 license, enabling any business to adopt it without Stripe dependency and any AI platform to integrate it without OpenAI permission.

Merchant-of-Record Model:

Unlike marketplace models where the platform becomes merchant-of-record, ACP preserves the direct relationship between business and customer. The merchant processes the payment, fulfils the order, and owns the customer data. The AI agent is an interface, not an intermediary.

Flexible Configuration:

ACP supports multiple commerce patterns:

  • Synchronous: Immediate payment and confirmation (e-commerce checkout)
  • Asynchronous: Book now, pay later (restaurant reservations, appointments)
  • Recurring: Subscription management (monthly boxes, SaaS billing)
  • Mixed: Physical and digital goods in same transaction

Cross-Platform Portability:

The protocol is designed so delegation tokens work across agents. A user who authorises agent A to make purchases should be able to switch to agent B without re-authorising every merchant. This portability is the key difference from proprietary systems like Microsoft Copilot Checkout.

Business Model Implications

For Payment Providers:

Stripe benefits from increased transaction volume through ACP adoption, but the protocol is deliberately provider-agnostic. Businesses processing with Adyen, PayPal, Square, or other providers can still implement ACP. This prevents payment processor lock-in.

For AI Platforms:

OpenAI gains first-mover advantage by launching Instant Checkout before competitors, but the open protocol prevents platform lock-in. ChatGPT users aren't trapped: they could switch to Claude or Copilot and retain their merchant authorisations if those platforms adopt ACP.

For Merchants:

ACP creates a strategic choice:

  1. Integrate with closed platforms (Microsoft Copilot Checkout) for immediate market access to platform-specific users
  2. Adopt open protocol (ACP) for portability across multiple AI agents
  3. Support both (Chapter 13's EAL abstraction approach) for maximum reach

The "support both" approach requires building an abstraction layer that isolates platform-specific implementations behind a standard interface.

What This Validates

From Chapter 13, "The Missing Entity Asset Layer" (lines 898-1000):

Chapter 13 identified the lack of universal EAL delegation as a critical gap: "What's missing: A universal EAL delegation layer that works across platforms and machines." ACP provides exactly this: an open protocol for delegation that isn't locked to a single platform.

The chapter argued platforms were "racing to establish first-mover advantages before standards emerge." ACP challenges this prediction by publishing an open standard immediately rather than building a proprietary system first.

From Chapter 13, "Identity Abstraction" (lines 974-991):

The chapter recommended: "Build the EAL as an abstraction. Support proprietary systems today (you need market access) but design the architecture to support open standards when they emerge."

ACP makes this recommendation immediately actionable. Agent creators can now build abstraction layers that support both Microsoft's proprietary Copilot Checkout and OpenAI's open ACP protocol, positioning for eventual standardisation without sacrificing current market access.

From Chapter 4, "E-Commerce - Where Incentives Align" (lines 117-157):

Chapter 4 argued transaction-based businesses benefit from machine traffic when implementing compatible patterns. ACP provides the infrastructure for these transactions whilst preserving merchant control and customer relationships. The protocol's merchant-of-record model prevents the identity loss problem discussed in Chapter 4.

What This Challenges

Assumption challenged (Platform Consolidation Before Standards):

Chapter 13, line 916: "The technically correct solution - build on open standards like OAuth, implement portable delegation tokens, and support cross-platform identity - doesn't exist yet because platforms have no incentive to create it."

This assumption proved incorrect. OpenAI and Stripe published an open protocol before proprietary consolidation occurred, racing to establish ACP as the standard before platform lock-in happens. The timeline Chapter 13 predicted (proprietary systems first, open standards after regulatory pressure) was compressed. Open standards emerged alongside proprietary systems.

Competitive tension created:

The book assumed a simpler competitive field: proprietary platforms versus eventual open standards. Reality is more complex:

  • Microsoft: Building closed, proprietary Copilot Checkout system
  • OpenAI + Stripe: Building open ACP protocol
  • Google, Apple, Amazon: Positions unclear but likely building proprietary systems
  • Agent creators: Must support multiple incompatible approaches simultaneously

This creates three strategic options for businesses:

  1. Platform-exclusive: Integrate only with closed platforms (immediate market, lock-in risk)
  2. Standards-first: Adopt only ACP (portability, limited agent reach at launch)
  3. Multi-platform: Support both closed and open (maximum reach, highest implementation cost)

Chapter 13's EAL abstraction recommendation becomes even more critical. Businesses need architecture that isolates platform differences behind a unified interface.

Architectural Insights

OAuth 2.0 Extension Pattern:

ACP builds on OAuth 2.0 delegation extensions, making it familiar to developers who've implemented social login or API authorisation. The delegation tokens follow established patterns:

  • User authorises agent to act on their behalf
  • Token scoped to specific merchant and permissions
  • Token revocable without agent cooperation
  • Token portable across compliant agents

Separation of Concerns:

ACP separates three distinct responsibilities:

  1. Identity Provider: Authenticates user and issues delegation tokens
  2. Agent: Executes purchase flow using delegated authority
  3. Merchant: Processes payment and fulfils order

This separation prevents any single party from controlling the entire transaction chain. Users can switch agents without losing merchant access. Merchants can switch payment providers without breaking agent integrations. Agents can support multiple merchants without building proprietary relationships with each.

Future-Proofing:

The protocol is designed for extensibility. New commerce patterns (escrow, instalment payments, group purchasing) can be added without breaking existing implementations. This flexibility makes ACP suitable for long-term adoption rather than a point solution for current agent capabilities.

Questions Raised

Will competing platforms adopt ACP?

Microsoft has invested in proprietary Copilot Checkout. Google and Apple are likely building their own systems. Will they adopt an OpenAI-initiated protocol, or will the market fragment into incompatible standards?

How will conflicts be resolved?

If a user has authorised purchases through both Microsoft Copilot Checkout (proprietary) and ChatGPT Instant Checkout (ACP), which takes precedence? How do merchants handle conflicting delegation tokens from different platforms?

What happens when regulations force interoperability?

Chapter 13 predicted regulators would eventually mandate open standards. If ACP becomes that standard through market adoption, does that give OpenAI and Stripe disproportionate influence over machine commerce infrastructure?

Can ACP prevent payment processor lock-in?

The protocol is designed to be processor-agnostic, but Stripe's co-development role creates perception of bias. Will merchants using Adyen, PayPal, or Square adopt ACP, or will they view it as a Stripe advantage?

Strategic Implications for Readers

For Web Developers (Chapter 12 audience):

If you're implementing e-commerce or checkout flows, consider ACP integration alongside traditional payment flows. The protocol provides a standard way for agents to complete purchases without requiring custom agent-specific implementations for each platform.

Implementation priority: Medium-term (6-12 months). ACP is production-ready but agent adoption is still growing. Position for future growth without disrupting current operations.

For Agent Creators (Chapter 13 audience):

ACP provides the open standard Chapter 13 advocated. If you're building agents, implement ACP support to enable commerce without platform lock-in. The abstraction layer pattern from Chapter 13 (lines 974-991) applies directly: isolate ACP behind a standard interface so you can support proprietary platforms alongside open standards.

Implementation priority: High (immediate). First-mover advantage available for agents that support ACP early whilst competing agents are still building proprietary integrations.

For Business Leaders (Chapter 4 audience):

Evaluate whether ACP adoption aligns with your machine commerce strategy. The protocol preserves customer relationships (merchant-of-record model) and enables portability (cross-platform tokens), addressing two major concerns from Chapter 4's EAL delegation discussion.

Decision framework:

  • High machine traffic potential: Adopt ACP plus platform-specific integrations (multi-platform approach)
  • Microsoft-focused customer base: Prioritise Copilot Checkout, defer ACP
  • OpenAI/ChatGPT user base: Immediate ACP integration for market access
  • Future-proofing focus: ACP for portability, platform integrations as needed

ACP/UCP Convergence Prospects

The launch of Google's Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP) in January 2026 created dual open standards for machine commerce. This section analyses convergence prospects and market implications.

Current state (January 2026):

  • ACP: First mover (launched September 2025), 1M+ merchants on Shopify/Etsy, mature tooling and documentation
  • UCP: Google-backed with 20+ major retail partners, search distribution advantage, newer infrastructure
  • Both claim compatibility with A2A, AP2, and MCP protocols, but direct ACP-UCP interoperability unverified

Best outcome: Unified standard within 6 months

Convergence would benefit all participants:

  • Merchants: One integration instead of two, reduced security surface, simplified testing
  • Agent creators: Universal agent-to-merchant transactions without platform-specific implementations
  • Platforms: Network effects strengthen unified standard more than competing standards
  • Users: Any agent works with any merchant, no vendor lock-in

What would trigger convergence:

  1. Merchant pressure: If dual-protocol integration burden becomes unsustainable, merchants will demand unified standard. Large retailers (Target, Walmart, Best Buy) have influence to force platform cooperation.

  2. Regulatory intervention: EU or US regulators might mandate interoperability as condition of market dominance. Google's search monopoly + commerce protocol creates antitrust scrutiny.

  3. Market consolidation: If one protocol clearly wins adoption race (60%+ merchant share), losing protocol may merge rather than maintain a separate merchant base.

  4. Technical compatibility demonstration: If ACP/UCP prove directly interoperable via shared A2A/MCP infrastructure, merger becomes technical formality rather than competitive decision.

What would prevent convergence:

  1. Competitive positioning: OpenAI/Stripe vs. Google competition extends beyond commerce protocols. Merger requires cooperating with direct competitor.

  2. Revenue implications: Both platforms may monetise protocol adoption (transaction fees, partnership terms). Merger requires agreeing on revenue sharing.

  3. Control concerns: Unified standard requires governance model. Who controls evolution of merged protocol? Neither platform wants to cede control to competitor.

  4. Timing coordination: Protocols are evolving independently. Synchronising release plans, feature sets, and API versions requires significant coordination effort.

Timeline assessment: 6-12 months for clarity

  • Q1-Q2 2026: Initial merchant adoption data reveals which protocol gains traction
  • Q2-Q3 2026: Merchants begin demanding convergence as dual-integration burden becomes apparent
  • Q3-Q4 2026: Either convergence announced or fragmentation acknowledged as permanent
  • 2027: If no convergence by end of 2026, expect years of dual-protocol commerce

What happens if convergence fails:

Permanent fragmentation creates:

  • Higher merchant costs (dual integration, dual maintenance, dual security auditing)
  • Smaller addressable market for each protocol (agents choose one protocol to support)
  • Slower adoption overall (businesses wait rather than commit to wrong protocol)
  • Potential for third competitor to launch "universal adapter" protocol bridging both

Connection to Chapter 9: The platform race analysis in Chapter 9 discusses fragmentation danger and convergence prospects from strategic perspective. This technical appendix provides implementation and timeline specifics for merchant decision-making.

Perplexity Comet Browser (July-October 2025)

Overview

Perplexity AI launched Comet, an AI-powered browser with integrated agent capabilities in every new tab. Initial release in July 2025 targeted paid subscribers ($200/month Max plan), followed by free global release in October 2025, democratising browser-based agent automation to millions of users worldwide.

Key Details

Initial Launch: 9 July 2025 (Max plan subscribers, $200/month) Free Global Release: 2 October 2025 Mobile Launch: November 2025 (Android), iOS coming soon Platform: Standalone browser (Chromium-based) Availability: Free for all users globally Scale: Millions joined waitlist before free release; reported millions of daily users in January 2026 Category: Browser-Based Agent Tools

Key Capabilities

Core Features:

  • Comet Assistant: AI agent integrated in every new tab for instant interaction
  • Page summarisation: Condenses articles, videos, and documents without leaving the page
  • Tab management: Organises and tracks open tabs intelligently
  • Email assistance: Drafts emails and briefs with full context from browsing
  • Shopping comparison: Compares products and prices across sites
  • Background task management: Offloads repetitive workflows to focus on higher-value work

Agent-Mode Automation:

  • Multi-step workflows: "Find flight deals under £200 and add them to a comparison spreadsheet"
  • Voice-activated control: Hands-free browser navigation and task execution
  • Natural language interface: Conversational instructions rather than precise syntax
  • Context awareness: Understands what you're viewing and pulls relevant details automatically

Technical Architecture:

  • Built on Chromium (same foundation as Chrome, Edge, Brave)
  • Perplexity AI search as default search engine
  • AI-first interface design (assistant-centric rather than traditional browser UI)
  • Operates within user's browser session (session inheritance architecture)

Significance for This Book

Particularly relevant: Comet is the earliest major production deployment of the session inheritance architecture discussed in Chapter 6. While Chapter 6:101 references "Claude for Chrome (launched December 2025)" as an example, Comet launched earlier (July 2025, free October 2025) and demonstrates the same fundamental pattern: browser agents inheriting authenticated sessions, making detection impossible.

Technical Implementation Insights

Session Inheritance Architecture:

As a standalone browser (not just an extension), Comet operates within the user's browser session, inheriting:

  • Valid cookies and authentication tokens
  • Device trust tokens built over months
  • Cloudflare clearance and CAPTCHA completion
  • Active session IDs from logged-in services
  • Two-factor authentication completion flags

This makes it impossible for websites to distinguish AI activity from human activity based on authentication state alone. That is the exact problem discussed in Chapter 6's "Session Inheritance Problem" section.

Browser-Based vs Extension-Based:

Unlike Claude for Chrome (browser extension), Comet is a standalone browser. This architecture gives Perplexity complete control over:

  • Default search engine (Perplexity AI search)
  • Navigation patterns and UI design
  • Data collection and privacy policies
  • Integration with external services

The trade-off: users must switch browsers rather than adding to existing workflow. The benefit: deeper integration and cohesive AI-first experience.

Chromium Foundation:

Building on Chromium provides:

  • Compatibility with web standards
  • Security updates from Google's Chromium team
  • Extension ecosystem (can run Chrome extensions)
  • Familiar developer tools and debugging

This reduces Perplexity's maintenance burden whilst enabling fast feature development.

Agent-Mode Marketing:

Perplexity explicitly markets "Agent-Mode automation" in consumer-facing materials (email campaigns, website copy). This signals machines becoming normalised in everyday workflows, moving from technical jargon to mainstream consumer feature.

Business Model Implications

For Perplexity:

  • Search distribution: Owns the browser, controls default search (Perplexity AI)
  • Data advantage: Observes browsing behaviour across all sites (within privacy policy)
  • Platform power: Can prioritise own services in agent recommendations
  • Competitive positioning: Competes with Chrome, Edge, and Brave whilst offering differentiated AI capabilities

Free Model Implications:

The shift from $200/month (July) to free (October) in just three months demonstrates:

  • Rapid market validation (millions joined waitlist)
  • Strategic decision to maximise adoption over immediate revenue
  • Likely monetisation through search advertising and premium features
  • Platform race dynamics: get users first, monetise later

For Competing Platforms:

Comet's free release pressures competitors:

  • ChatGPT and Claude charge for browser automation features
  • Google and Microsoft must respond with free or low-cost alternatives
  • Creates expectation that browser agents should be free, not premium features

For Website Owners:

Browser agents are no longer limited to paid subscribers. Millions of users with free access means:

  • Machine traffic becomes meaningful percentage of total traffic
  • Must optimise for machines or risk losing conversions
  • Cannot assume "machines are rare/expensive users we can ignore"

What This Validates

From Chapter 2, "The Invisible Failure":

Comet encounters the same five failure patterns documented in Chapter 2 when sites don't follow agent-friendly design:

  • Toast notifications that appear and vanish before the machine sees them
  • Pagination and hidden content it doesn't discover
  • SPA state changes without URL or semantic indicators
  • Delayed validation feedback with no upfront requirements
  • Hidden pricing revealed only at checkout

These failures affect millions of Comet users, validating the practical impact of invisible failures.

From Chapter 6, "Session Inheritance Problem":

Chapter 6:46 discusses "Browser extension assistants (ChatGPT sidebar, Claude browser extension) running inside your authenticated browser." Comet is a standalone browser but demonstrates identical session inheritance: banks cannot distinguish Comet's AI activity from human activity because Comet inherits proof-of-humanity tokens from the authenticated session.

Chapter 6:101 explicitly states: "This setup is no longer theoretical. Claude for Chrome (launched December 2025) provides exactly this capability to all paid subscribers - browser automation with full session inheritance."

Comet launched five months earlier (July 2025) and went free three months before Claude for Chrome's broad release, making session inheritance a production reality even sooner than the book's timeline suggested.

From Chapter 9, "Platform Race":

The Preface and Chapter 9 discuss the "platform race" where AI companies compete for distribution and control. Comet validates this prediction from an unexpected angle: a search engine company (Perplexity) now owns a browser, directly competing with Chrome whilst building AI-first interfaces.

Platform dynamics: Perplexity vs. Chrome vs. Microsoft Edge vs. ChatGPT browser features vs. Claude for Chrome. Multiple players racing to control the agent-mediated browsing experience.

What This Challenges

Assumption challenged (Premium Feature Positioning):

The book discusses browser automation as potentially premium-tier feature (similar to Claude's pricing model). Comet's free global release challenges this: browser agents can be free-to-use with alternative monetisation (search advertising, platform data).

Timeline acceleration:

Chapter 9 discusses rapid adoption but didn't anticipate a major platform going from "$200/month" to "free globally" in three months. This acceleration demonstrates "rocket-fuel mode" market dynamics even faster than projected.

Competitive fragmentation:

The market is more fragmented than the "proprietary platforms vs. open standards" dichotomy suggested in Chapter 13:

  • Standalone browsers with built-in agents (Comet)
  • Browser extensions (Claude for Chrome, ChatGPT sidebar)
  • Operating system integration (Windows Copilot)
  • Mobile app-based agents (Amazon Rufus)
  • Search engine integrations (Google SGE)

Each approach has different session inheritance characteristics, detection challenges, and competitive advantages.

Architectural Insights

Multi-Step Workflow Execution:

Agent-Mode automation enables complex sequences: "Find flight deals under £200, compare them, and add to spreadsheet." This requires:

  1. Understanding natural language intent
  2. Searching across multiple travel sites
  3. Extracting and normalising pricing data
  4. Creating or accessing spreadsheet
  5. Formatting and inserting data
  6. Confirming completion with user

Each step encounters the failure patterns from Chapter 2 if sites don't implement agent-friendly patterns.

Voice-Activated Browser Control:

Voice interface creates additional challenges:

  • Must parse spoken instructions accurately
  • Cannot show visual confirmation dialogs easily
  • Errors more costly (user not watching screen)
  • Privacy implications (always-listening mic)

Validates Chapter 12's emphasis on explicit state and persistent error messages. Voice users need clear feedback even more than visual users.

Background Task Management:

Offloading workflows to background execution requires:

  • Thorough error handling (user not monitoring)
  • Persistent state tracking (task may take minutes/hours)
  • Clear completion notifications
  • Rollback capabilities if errors occur

This is the validation layer architecture discussed in Chapter 12: machines need confidence scoring, error detection, and graceful failure modes.

Questions Raised

Detection and Bot Blocking:

If Comet disguises itself as Chrome (using Chromium User-Agent), how can websites distinguish legitimate human browsing from agent automation? Amazon's lawsuit against Perplexity (documented in Appendix J) centres on this question.

Privacy and Data Collection:

As a standalone browser, Comet observes all browsing activity. Privacy policy governs what Perplexity collects and how they use it, but users must trust a single vendor with complete browsing history. This differs from browser extensions (limited scope) or separate machines (no persistent access).

Search Neutrality:

With Perplexity AI as default search engine, does Comet prioritise Perplexity's results over competitors? Can users effectively switch default search? Browser ownership creates conflicts of interest in search ranking and result presentation.

Multi-Step Task Reliability:

TechCrunch testing (July 2025) found Comet's agent "struggled with complex multi-step tasks" and "hallucinated incorrect dates during airport parking reservation attempt." How reliable are multi-step workflows in production? What percentage of Agent-Mode tasks complete successfully vs. fail partially or silently?

Session Security:

If Comet inherits authenticated sessions, what happens if:

  • User shares device with family member
  • Malicious website attempts prompt injection
  • Machine makes unauthorised purchase
  • Session tokens leak to Perplexity's servers

Chapter 6 discusses these security challenges but doesn't provide definitive solutions.

Strategic Implications for Readers

For Website Owners (Chapter 11 guidance):

Millions of Comet users are attempting workflows on your site right now. Test with Comet immediately:

  1. Install Comet browser
  2. Instruct agent to complete critical workflow (book appointment, complete purchase, fill contact form)
  3. Observe where it fails
  4. Implement patterns from Chapter 11 to fix failures

Those failures cost you both human and AI-mediated conversions.

For Security Professionals (Chapter 6 guidance):

Session inheritance is production reality at millions-of-users scale. Your authentication systems cannot distinguish human from AI based on session tokens alone. Implement detection strategies from Chapter 6 that don't rely solely on authentication state:

  • Transaction velocity monitoring
  • Behavioral anomaly detection
  • Explicit agent identification requirements (though enforcement is challenging)
  • Rate limiting per session rather than per IP

For Agent Creators (Chapter 12 guidance):

Comet demonstrates gaps between marketing claims and production reliability:

  • TechCrunch found "struggles with complex multi-step tasks"
  • Date hallucination in booking workflows
  • No public metrics on success rates

Build validation layers and guardrails that prevent false positives (machine reports success when task failed). Implement patterns from Chapter 12: planning mode review, pre-approval, confidence scoring, confirmation for irreversible actions.

For Competing Platforms:

Comet's free model pressures premium pricing strategies. Consider:

  • Free tier with browser automation (match Comet)
  • Premium tier with additional safety controls, enterprise admin, priority support
  • Differentiation through reliability rather than access

WebMCP - Web Model Context Protocol (February 2026)

Overview

Google and Microsoft jointly shipped WebMCP (Web Model Context Protocol) in Chrome 146 Canary, marking the first browser-native API for AI agent interaction with web pages. WebMCP extends the Model Context Protocol into the browser, allowing websites to expose structured, callable tools to AI agents through document.modelContext. Instead of scraping DOM elements and guessing at page functionality, agents can invoke registered functions directly through a standardised interface to search product catalogues, book tables, and complete purchases.

Key Details

Announcement Date: February 2026 Organisations: Google and Microsoft (co-developers), W3C Web Machine Learning Community Group (specification host) Status: Draft Community Group Report, shipping in Chrome 146 Canary. The specification states that it is not a W3C Standard and is not on the W3C Standards Track. Specification: https://webmachinelearning.github.io/webmcp/ Implementation: Two APIs, Declarative (HTML forms) and Imperative (JavaScript registerTool) Category: Standards and Protocol Announcements

Key Capabilities

Declarative API (HTML forms):

Existing HTML forms gain machine accessibility through standard markup. No JavaScript required for basic tool exposure. Machines discover form actions, parameters, and constraints through the DOM.

Imperative API (JavaScript):

await document.modelContext.registerTool({
  name: "searchProducts",
  title: "Search products",
  description: "Search the product catalogue",
  inputSchema: {
    type: "object",
    properties: {
      query: { type: "string", description: "Search terms" },
      category: { type: "string", enum: ["electronics", "clothing", "home"] },
      maxPrice: { type: "number", description: "Maximum price in GBP" }
    },
    required: ["query"]
  },
  annotations: { readOnlyHint: true },
  execute: async ({ query, category, maxPrice }) => {
    const params = new URLSearchParams({ q: query, cat: category ?? "", max: maxPrice ?? "" });
    const results = await fetch(`/api/search?${params}`);
    return results.json();
  }
});

Websites register callable tools with a name, a natural-language description, a JSON Schema inputSchema, and an execute callback. Optional annotations describe the tool's behaviour: readOnlyHint for tools that change nothing, consequentialHint for tools with real-world or non-reversible effects (so the agent can ask the user to confirm first), and untrustedContentHint for tools whose output the site does not stand behind. Machines discover available tools through document.modelContext and invoke them with validated arguments. Access is gated by the tools permissions-policy feature, which defaults to the page's own origin.

For Businesses:

  • Expose product search, booking, and checkout as callable tools
  • Control exactly what machines can do on your site
  • No proprietary machine integrations required: One API serves all machines
  • Works alongside existing HTML, not replacing it

For AI Agents:

  • Discover available actions through standardised browser API
  • Invoke tools with typed parameters instead of simulating clicks
  • Receive structured responses instead of parsing DOM mutations
  • Works across all WebMCP-enabled sites with no site-specific code

Measured Performance: The WindTunnel Benchmark (August 2026)

WindTunnel is an open benchmark published by nekuda on 4 August 2026 that compares three ways a browser agent can operate a website: calling the WebMCP tools the site declares, driving the rendered page from screenshots, and reading the DOM and accessibility tree. The suite runs 49 tasks, three attempts each, across eight self-hosted open-source web applications and sixteen agent configurations. Tasks are outcome-scored; the code, task definitions, and full run transcripts are public under the Apache 2.0 licence.

Headline results as published:

Measure WebMCP configurations Screen-driving agents (median)
Tasks solved 48 of 49 43 of 49
Median time per task 7.8 seconds 28.1 seconds
Median cost per task 0.6 cents 5.5 cents
Final score 91.2 65.8

Every WebMCP configuration solved the same 48 tasks regardless of the underlying model, at a fraction of the tokens, time, and cost the screenshot and DOM approaches needed. The final score combines attempt success with log-normalised cost and time.

What the benchmark measures, and what it does not. WindTunnel measures the action layer only. It shows that an agent does better, faster, and cheaper when the site declares what it offers than when the agent infers it from pixels or markup. That is the declared-beats-guessed argument this book makes for the understanding layer, corroborated by an independent source on the adjacent layer. It does not measure understanding or trust. The eight sites are controlled by the benchmark authors; no task asks the agent to establish who stands behind a tool, whether a price is current, or whether the result should have been trusted at all. A near-perfect solve rate on a benchmark where trust is never in question is a measurement of capability, not of accountability, and the gap between the two is the one MX metadata and a registry exist to close.

Significance for This Book

What WebMCP provides: The action layer. Machines can invoke functions on websites through a standardised protocol.

What WebMCP does not provide: The understanding layer. WebMCP tells machines what tools are available (searchProducts, bookTable) but not what the business does, what the content means, or how it should be interpreted. A searchProducts tool with no product metadata, no content policy, no freshness indicators, and no attribution requirements is a capable tool that lacks context.

This is where MX completes the picture. MX metadata (structured content descriptions, machine instructions, content policies, freshness signals, attribution requirements) provides the understanding within which WebMCP tools operate. A machine with WebMCP can search your product catalogue. A machine with MX knows your catalogue contains organic skincare products updated daily, requires attribution when cited, and prefers API access for pricing data.

Together, they form the companion web, the machine-readable layer that runs alongside the human web. MX provides understanding. WebMCP provides action. Neither replaces the other.

Universal Cart, UCP Production Launch, AP2 (19 May 2026)

Note: This entry describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

Overview

Four months after Google announced the Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP) at NRF, Google used the I/O 2026 keynote to launch the consumer-facing instantiation: Universal Cart, a cross-merchant shopping basket that follows a single user across Search, Gemini, YouTube, and Gmail. The same announcement introduced AP2 (Agent Payments Protocol), the accountability layer that produces a tamper-evident record linking the shopper to any purchase an agent makes on their behalf within a stated set of brands, products, and a spending cap.

The launch is the moment UCP moves from protocol specification to production deployment in a consumer surface. The earlier January 2026 entry covers UCP itself; this entry covers the rollout, AP2, the partner ring, and the architectural question the launch surfaces for everyone outside that ring.

Key Details

Announcement Date: 19 May 2026 Event: Google I/O 2026 keynote Organisations: Google (lead), Shopify (UCP co-development), plus the launch partners listed below Products: Universal Cart (consumer surface), UCP (protocol), AP2 (attestation layer) Surfaces: Search, Gemini, YouTube, Gmail Category: Retail and Commerce Agents + Standards and Protocol Announcements

Launch Partners

Initial ring: Nike, Sephora, Target, Ulta Beauty, Walmart, Wayfair, and Shopify stores including Fenty (Rihanna's brand) and Steve Madden.

Composition: The ring is biased toward US-headquartered retail and toward categories where Google already has strong shopping-intent data: apparel, beauty, big-box, home. The protocol may be global in ambition; the early ring is not.

Shopify as the swing factor: Adding Shopify means any Shopify store can in principle ride into UCP without doing the engineering itself, because the platform does it for them. That gives Google a long tail almost for free, and concentrates a large fraction of independent retail behind a single integration that Google co-designed.

Outside the ring: Independent retailers on Magento, on WooCommerce, on Salesforce Commerce Cloud, on SAP Hybris, on TYPO3 commerce extensions, on bespoke stacks. Every European retailer outside the Shopify orbit. Every B2B catalogue. Every public-sector procurement portal. None of them have a UCP path on day one.

Key Capabilities

Universal Cart (Consumer Surface):

  • Single basket persisting across Search, Gemini, YouTube, and Gmail
  • Cross-merchant by default: trainers from Search, an eyeshadow palette from a Gemini conversation, and a side table from a YouTube creator's video all land in one place
  • Watches prices, flags stock returns, surfaces deals, checks compatibility on multi-item purchases
  • Native and embedded checkout integrations both supported
  • Merchant remains the merchant of record (legal relationship, payment, fulfilment)

UCP (Protocol):

  • Shared language for agent-to-merchant commerce
  • Co-developed with Shopify
  • Open specification, governed by Google
  • Native checkout integration and embedded checkout integration as two operating modes

AP2 (Accountability Layer):

  • Produces a tamper-evident record linking shopper to purchase
  • Records the brand set, product set, and spending cap the shopper authorised
  • Designed to answer "who authorised this" in a form a regulator, a fraud team, or a dispute process can rely on
  • Records each agent-mediated purchase on Google's surfaces; scope is bounded to UCP-managed flows

Significance for This Book

The protocol-of-record question, made concrete. For the launch ring, UCP is an acceptable trade: Nike has commercial weight, Walmart has commercial weight, Shopify has platform weight, and the merchant of record stays with the retailer. For a mid-size European retailer, an independent specialist, a public-sector buyer, or a B2B catalogue, the protocol of record is whichever organisation defines the structure they have to publish in. That organisation will not be them and will not be a peer of theirs. It will be a platform vendor. The MX argument that an open registry concept and an open file format are infrastructure rather than products is made empirical the moment a single-platform protocol launches in production at this scale.

AP2 has a symmetric counterpart in REGINALD. AP2 records purchases on Google's surfaces. The decision-COG records any agent action, anywhere, under an open standard published under the MIT licence, which will be donated to The Gathering. The fields are policyRef (which policy authorised this action), inputHash (what the agent saw before deciding), and model (which model made the call). Two more complete the set: humanInLoop (whether a human was involved and at which step) and sequence (where in a chain of decisions this one sits). The decision-COG is not specific to commerce: the same artefact records an agent's decision to recommend a healthcare option, approve a benefits claim, route an incident, or draft a contract amendment. AP2 covers one cell of that grid; decision-COGs cover the rest.

Technical Implementation Insights

Universal Cart is for the web. UCP is for the web. AP2 is for the web. Google's frame is the web because Google's business is the web. The launch does not extend to printed artefacts, to email attachments, to regulator archives that machines query in five years, or to any file that travels off Google's surfaces. Each of those is a category where the MX Readiness Model continues to apply and where UCP does not.

Merchant-of-record preservation, similar pattern to ACP. Both ACP (the September 2025 OpenAI/Stripe protocol) and UCP keep the merchant as the legal counterparty. They differ in governance (ACP is open and Apache-2.0 licensed; UCP is open in specification but governed by Google) and in scope of consumer surface (ACP runs in ChatGPT; UCP runs across Google's full surface area).

MX Readiness L4 and L5, packaged. Universal Cart and UCP do not invent anything that was not already in the MX Readiness Model. They package L4 (Price Understanding / Registered) and L5 (Purchase Confidence / Audited) for Google's chosen merchants on Google's chosen surfaces. The rest of the model, and the levels below L4 which UCP assumes are already in place, is what every retailer needs regardless of whether they are in the ring.

Agentic Micropayment Protocols: HTTP 402, x402, L402, Mastercard Agent Pay (2026)

Overview

Through 2026 a cluster of payment protocols rebuilt the negotiation layer agents and machines use to pay for resources on the web. The cluster shares one foundation (HTTP status code 402, Payment Required, reserved in the HTTP standard since 1997 and unused at production scale until now) and divides on settlement rail. Coinbase published the x402 specification, which settles in stablecoins on blockchain rails. Lightning Labs and the Bitcoin community formalised L402, which settles in milliseconds via the Bitcoin Lightning Network. Mastercard rolled out Mastercard Agent Pay, which settles on existing card-tokenisation infrastructure in fiat. Three rails, one negotiation layer, three commercial postures.

The development matters not because any single rail will dominate, but because the architectural separation between negotiation and settlement is now real. Resources priced in fractions of a penny per request, settled programmatically without human intervention, are operationally possible for the first time. The use cases that emerge in 2026 (registry resolution traffic, agent-to-agent data exchange, sub-cent API metering) are not feasible under traditional card-processing economics; the per-transaction overhead exceeds the transaction value.

Key Details

Announcement Dates:

  • x402: Specification published by Coinbase, 2026
  • L402: Lightning Labs has operated the protocol since 2022; 2026 saw it ratified for general agentic use beyond Lightning-native applications
  • Mastercard Agent Pay: Mastercard's agentic-commerce extension to its tokenisation rails, announced and partner-piloted through 2026

Organisations: Coinbase (x402), Lightning Labs and the wider Bitcoin community (L402), Mastercard (Agent Pay)

Category: Payment Protocols + Agent Infrastructure + Standards and Protocol Announcements

How They Differ

x402 sits closest to the HTTP standard's original intent. The server returns HTTP 402 with payment terms in response headers, the client constructs a stablecoin payment against the address and amount declared, and the client retries the request with the on-chain payment proof. No API keys, no subscriptions, no minimum transaction amounts. Stablecoin selection is open; Coinbase's reference implementation defaults to USDC.

L402 uses the same HTTP 402 negotiation shape but settles via Bitcoin Lightning. The payment proof is a macaroon, a cryptographic token that can carry capability constraints alongside the payment. A single Lightning payment can authorise a precisely-scoped follow-on action, useful for streaming-billed resources or for time-bounded access. Settlement is sub-second; transaction costs are sub-cent at typical resource sizes.

Mastercard Agent Pay uses HTTP 402 as the negotiation layer but settles on Mastercard's card-tokenisation infrastructure in fiat. Per-transaction overhead is higher than the HTTP-native crypto protocols, but the trust posture is different: organisations not yet comfortable holding crypto positions or accepting crypto-denominated invoices can transact on infrastructure their finance functions already audit and recognise.

Key Capabilities

  • HTTP 402 negotiation, programmatic, no human in the payment loop
  • Sub-cent transaction granularity on the crypto rails, and tolerable per-transaction overhead on the fiat rail
  • Settlement-protocol-agnostic resource exposure: a resource that returns HTTP 402 can be paid by any client that speaks any of the three rails, provided the server lists the rail in its payment terms
  • Capability-constrained payment proofs (L402's macaroon model), transparent on-chain settlement (x402), and an enterprise-grade audit trail (Mastercard Agent Pay)

Significance for This Book

The registry's resolution economics, made concrete. Chapter 20 names registry resolution traffic as the volume revenue line that pays for long-horizon registry operation. Sub-cent micro-fees per resolution, settled programmatically without card-processor margin, are not feasible under traditional API-billing infrastructure. The HTTP 402 cluster makes them feasible. The registry that resolves cog identifiers at scale, on millions of agent-driven requests per day, is now operationally fundable in a way it was not in 2024.

Negotiation in the protocol, settlement on the rail. The architectural separation is the development worth holding. A registry, a publisher, an API operator, a regulator-operated audit tool: each can expose resources behind HTTP 402 without committing to any specific settlement rail. Adding a new rail (the next stablecoin, the next central-bank digital currency, the next fiat-tokenisation scheme) is a server-side adapter, not an architectural change. The flexibility belongs to the protocol layer; the commercial choice belongs to each operator.

Trust friction is unevenly distributed. Large-enterprise customers with established card-tokenisation contracts will adopt Mastercard Agent Pay before they hold a stablecoin balance. Crypto-native operators will pay over x402 because the per-transaction cost is materially lower. High-frequency, capability-constrained interactions (streaming resources, time-bounded access) suit L402's macaroon model. No rail dominates the others on every dimension; resource operators that support all three reach the widest agent population.

Technical Implementation Insights

HTTP 402 as the shared layer. All three protocols use the same status code and the same general negotiation pattern: server declares terms, client pays, client retries with proof, server serves. This pattern is RFC-9110-compliant and predates any of the rails by decades. The protocols differ only in what proof of payment looks like and how it is verified.

Verification asymmetry. x402 verification is an on-chain transaction lookup; the verifier confirms the payment exists at the declared address for the declared amount. L402 verification is a macaroon check plus a Lightning settlement proof. Mastercard Agent Pay verification is a tokenised-charge confirmation against Mastercard's authorisation network. The verifier's cost differs by rail; the resource operator chooses which rails to accept partly on the basis of their own verification overhead.

Free tier first. Every working deployment of HTTP 402 micropayments observed in 2026 is built over a free tier: a small per-IP or per-anonymous-client allowance, sufficient for casual human use, with paywall-default only for high-volume programmatic use. Adoption requires that casual users encounter no payment friction; the payment layer is for sustained machine traffic, not for the consumer scanning a single QR.

Business Model Implications

For infrastructure operators: A new pricing model becomes feasible. Sub-cent per-request fees, settled programmatically, scale with consumption rather than with sales effort. The model fits resources where individual transactions are low-value but aggregate traffic is high (registry resolution, structured-data lookup, attestation verification).

For agents and AI platforms: A budgeted payment posture replaces the older subscription-or-free dichotomy. An agent operating on behalf of a user can carry a small payment balance and consume metered resources without the user negotiating per-resource contracts.

For publishers: The pre-funded resolution pattern becomes available. The publisher deposits a balance with a registry, the registry debits per resolution of the publisher's own content, and the enquirer scans for free. Publishers absorb resolution cost to remove friction; the equivalent of toll-free numbers, at agent-population scale.

For regulators and audit functions: Verification queries against registry-held attestation records become operationally cheap. A regulator-operated audit tool that resolves a thousand attestations per case incurs a sub-dollar cost rather than a per-tool licensing negotiation.

What This Validates

From Chapter 20 (Cogs and REGINALD): The registry's metadata-only posture is funded by resolution traffic, not by document hosting. The HTTP 402 cluster is the settlement layer that makes sub-cent resolution traffic operationally fundable.

From Chapter 13 (The Missing Entity Asset Layer): Agent-driven access to structured data at scale requires a payment layer the legacy card economy cannot deliver. The three-rail negotiation shape closes the gap.

From the wider architectural argument: Standards split cleanly into negotiation (HTTP 402) and settlement (multiple rails). The negotiation layer is open and protocol-resident; the settlement layer is rail-specific and commercially-resident. This is the same separation MX applies to file format (open, standard-resident) and registry implementation (open standard, multiple registry operators).

What This Challenges

The assumption that micropayments require crypto. The Mastercard Agent Pay rail demonstrates that sub-cent settlement is achievable on fiat infrastructure when the negotiation layer is HTTP-native and the per-transaction overhead is amortised across high-volume machine traffic. Crypto rails offer lower per-transaction overhead; fiat rails offer lower trust friction. The dichotomy is commercial, not technical.

The assumption that pricing must be subscription-based. The protocol stack now operationally supports a free tier plus per-request micro-fees plus an optional subscription tier: three tiers serving three populations (casual humans, machine traffic at variable volume, predictable enterprise traffic). The single-tier subscription model is no longer the only commercially-viable shape.

Architectural Insights

The protocol-layer choice is the durable one. Resource operators that expose HTTP 402 with multiple settlement-protocol options inherit any future rail that joins the cluster. Resource operators that wire their pricing into a single rail re-engineer when the rail's economics or its competitive position shifts.

Verification is a distinct concern from negotiation. Each rail has its own verification model. A registry that accepts payments over all three rails runs three verification adapters. This is a small operational cost relative to the breadth of agent populations the registry then reaches.

Questions Raised

  • How does the cluster interact with regulatory regimes on crypto holdings? EU MiCA, UK FCA stablecoin regulation, and analogous instruments in other jurisdictions place compliance obligations on operators accepting or settling in stablecoins. Fiat-rail operation under Mastercard Agent Pay sidesteps that regime; crypto-rail operation incurs it.

  • What is the dispute-and-chargeback posture across the three rails? Mastercard Agent Pay inherits the card network's chargeback rules. x402 and L402 settle finally on-chain; dispute resolution happens at the application layer rather than the rail. Resource operators choose which dispute regime fits their use case.

  • How does the cluster handle micropayments for resources whose value is non-monetary? A regulator-operated audit tool resolving thousands of attestations does not produce commercial revenue for the regulator; the cost is internal to the public function. Pre-funded enquirer balances, capability-constrained tokens, and grant-funded resolution windows all have a place; the rails do not prescribe one.

Strategic Implications for Readers

For infrastructure operators (registries, attestation services, structured-data APIs):

  • Expose HTTP 402 in the protocol layer; choose settlement rails based on the customer population the resource targets

Pope Leo XIV - Magnifica Humanitas, the First Papal Encyclical on AI (25 May 2026)

Note: This entry describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

Overview

On 25 May 2026 Pope Leo XIV published his first encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas, on artificial intelligence. He signed it on 15 May, 135 years to the day after Leo XIII signed Rerum Novarum, the 1891 letter on the rights of workers in the middle of the industrial revolution. The choice of date is the argument in miniature: this is the industrial revolution again, and the question is the same one it was in 1891. Who is this for, and who answers for it.

The encyclical's significance for the standards work tracked in this appendix is not the theology, which sits outside this book's remit, but the practical four-part ask Leo XIV makes of every government, every operator, and every author of an AI system: enforceable legal frameworks, independent oversight, informed users, and a political system that does not abdicate its responsibility. Read with an engineer's eye, every one of those four is a documentation problem before it is a moral one.

Key Details

Date signed: 15 May 2026 (the anniversary of Rerum Novarum, 15 May 1891).

Date published: 25 May 2026.

Title: Magnifica Humanitas.

Author: Pope Leo XIV, the first papal encyclical specifically on artificial intelligence.

Audience: Catholic Church and the wider international community; the document is explicitly addressed to governments, technology operators, and users.

Category: AI governance and documentation requirements, moral and policy framing.

The Four Practical Asks

The encyclical's four-part list, as it lands on the engineering and standards work:

  1. Enforceable legal frameworks. Already in motion across the EU (AI Act, EAA, DSA), the UK (ICO guidance), the US (NIST AI RMF), and several US states (Colorado AI Act). The encyclical's contribution is not new legislation but the moral language framing why the legislation is needed.
  2. Independent oversight. A regulator, an auditor, or a civil-society body must be able to inspect what an AI system did, on what basis, citing what source. Inspection requires evidence; evidence requires records the auditor can verify without the operator's cooperation.
  3. Informed users. A user, or the agent acting on the user's behalf, can only be informed if the content the user reads can tell them something about itself: who wrote it, when, under what authority, and whether a human reviewed it.
  4. Political responsibility. Governments and international bodies cannot delegate the question of who is accountable for an AI system's output to the market. The encyclical asks for a political answer to a political question.

Statistical Impact

Numbers are not the point here, but the timing is. The encyclical lands in the same fortnight as Google's Universal Cart launch, the formalisation of three agentic micropayment protocols, and the publication of the EAA's first enforcement guidance. The convergence is the signal: every layer of the agentic web (commercial, technical, regulatory, moral) is asking the same coordination question in May 2026.

What This Validates

The position that AI accountability is a documentation problem, and that the documentation must be machine-readable, machine-trustworthy, and verifiable on the reader's side, is now backed by a moral argument with a global audience. The two-pillar framing (MX makes content machine-readable; REGINALD makes it machine-trustworthy) maps cleanly onto the encyclical's asks 2 and 3. Oversight needs evidence the auditor can verify; informed users need content that can tell them about itself.

What This Challenges

The encyclical challenges two postures that had been politically convenient through 2025. The first is "the market will self-correct on AI accountability". The encyclical reads as a categorical rejection of that position, framed in moral rather than economic terms. The second is "compliance is the operator's problem, evidence is too expensive to produce". The encyclical's framing makes evidence non-optional and reframes the cost as the cost of doing business at all.

Architectural Insights

The architectural insight the encyclical surfaces, without using the language, is the inversion that makes verification trustworthy. Oversight that runs on operator cooperation is not oversight; it is the receipt of a story the operator chose to tell. Oversight runs on evidence the operator cannot edit after the fact, signed claims a third party can verify against an open registry, and a verification step that happens on the reader's or auditor's side. The technical name for this property is reader-side verification. The encyclical's name for it is independent oversight.

Honest Limits

The encyclical does not grant compliance with any regulation, and the standards work this book tracks does not either. Magnifica Humanitas is a moral argument; MX and REGINALD are plumbing. The plumbing matters because a moral argument about accountability without a mechanism to record what happened is a sermon that everyone nods at on Sunday and nothing is inspectable on Monday. The plumbing is dull on purpose: the right metadata, claims that are signed, a registry anyone can verify against, verification that runs on the reader's side and not on trust. Sold as that, it is honest. Sold as "ethics in a box", it collapses on the first inquiry, and deserves to.

WordPress / WP Engine - Governance Capture in Plain Sight (September - December 2024)

Overview

At WordCamp US in September 2024, WordPress founder Matt Mullenweg used his keynote to publicly attack the hosting company WP Engine. Within days, the wordpress.org infrastructure (under Mullenweg's personal control) blocked WP Engine from accessing the plugin and theme directory. In October, the slug of the Advanced Custom Fields (ACF) plugin was unilaterally taken over from its maintainer, Delicious Brains / WP Engine, and replaced with a fork operated by the wordpress.org team. Through November, accounts of contributors who criticised these actions were deactivated. In December 2024, a US federal court issued a preliminary injunction ordering restoration of WP Engine's access pending litigation.

The relevance to MX is structural, not technical. The text of the WordPress specification did not change during any of these events. The governance through which the specification was stewarded changed, in public, in real time, in ways against which the specification itself provided no protection.

Key Details

  • WordCamp US 2024, Portland, Oregon, 17 September 2024: keynote attack.
  • wordpress.org access block on WP Engine: 25 September 2024.
  • ACF plugin slug takeover: 12 October 2024 (renamed to "Secure Custom Fields" under Automattic stewardship).
  • Contributor account deactivations: October-November 2024.
  • US District Court for the Northern District of California preliminary injunction: 10 December 2024 (case 5:24-cv-06917-AMO).

Statistical Impact

  • WordPress powers approximately 43% of the public web by content management system share.
  • WP Engine hosts an estimated 1.5 million sites that depend on plugin and theme updates served from wordpress.org.
  • ACF (and the resulting Secure Custom Fields fork) is installed on more than 2 million sites; the takeover created a real risk of supply-chain confusion across that estate.

Technical Implementation

The infrastructure mechanism is the point. The wordpress.org plugin directory is the single discovery and update channel for WordPress plugins. Hosts and self-hosters resolve plugin updates against it by default. There is no built-in mechanism for a plugin to be served from elsewhere with the same trust signals (verified author, automatic updates, reputation). Control over the directory is therefore control over the WordPress community's record of which plugin is which, where it comes from, and whether to update.

Mullenweg's personal control of the wordpress.org infrastructure (despite the WordPress trademark being held by the WordPress Foundation, and the codebase being developed by a community) created a permission structure with no governance layer between his personal authority and the production directory. There was no committee with override authority. There was no charter constraining the operator. There was no separation between the trademark holder, the infrastructure operator, the commercial sponsor (Automattic), and the founder. All four were the same person in the operating moment.

Business Model Implications

The case demonstrates that the operator of records-provenance infrastructure has commercial leverage over every party whose records that infrastructure serves. The leverage holds whether or not the operator chooses to use it. The mere capacity, once visible, changes how every commercial dependent must price the dependency.

For organisations evaluating any standards body that operates a registry, directory, or other discovery infrastructure, the WordPress case is the worked example of what the implicit risk looks like when it becomes explicit. The conventional wisdom that "the open-source code protects you" is incorrect: the code did not change. What changed was access to the infrastructure that operationalises the code.

What This Validates

  • The separation of the standard's text from the governance through which the standard is operated is a real and load-bearing distinction. Both must be defended; defending only the text is insufficient.
  • Founder-dependency, when it exceeds the body's institutional capacity, is a vulnerability that materialises in predictable ways.
  • DID-based identity (publishers anchoring their identity in their own DNS authorities, rather than in a shared directory) addresses one of the structural failure modes the case exhibits.

What This Challenges

  • The assumption that an open-source licence on the code is sufficient governance protection. It is not. Governance is a separate layer.
  • The assumption that a foundation holding a trademark provides commercial protection. The trademark and the infrastructure are different assets, controlled by different parties in this case, with different incentives.

Architectural Insights

Three structural failure modes are visible in the case and apply to any specification worth controlling:

  1. Capture by infrastructure ownership: The entity that hosts the directory is also a commercial competitor of the parties that depend on it.
  2. Capture by sponsor concentration: A single commercial sponsor accumulates enough share of the operating budget to make the body institutionally unable to refuse the sponsor's preferences.
  3. Capture by founder dependency: A person whose unpaid labour, personal infrastructure, or commercial backstop the body cannot do without retains a veto that becomes load-bearing as the body grows.

The MX response starts with an open specification and registry protocol that any operator can implement, separated from any one operator's commercial product (The Gathering Administration Ltd is a separate UK legal entity with no commercial product of its own). A founding charter caps any single sponsor at a fixed share of annual income (25%, including the founding sponsor). DID-based identity (did:web by default) distributes trust across publishers rather than concentrating it in a shared directory.

Questions Raised

  • What due-diligence test should an organisation apply to a standards body before adopting its specifications? The buyer's three-question test (who owns the infrastructure; who funds the body; what happens to the standard if the founders leave) is one answer.
  • How should governance disputes between a standards body and a commercial dependent be resolved? The current answer (US federal court) is expensive, slow, and depends on a jurisdiction the global community does not share.

Strategic Implications

For organisations adopting standards: Apply the three-question test before adoption. Verify the answers via independently verifiable sources (annual transparency reports, public charter, court filings) rather than the standards body's own marketing.

For organisations operating standards: Publish the answers to the three questions in a place a sceptic can find without your cooperation. The annual transparency report is the obvious place. Cap any single sponsor's share of income, including your own founding sponsor. Anchor identity in publisher DNS authorities rather than in your own directory.

For sponsors: A capped sponsorship signal trustworthiness in a way that an uncapped one cannot. The cap is the contribution. The right to influence accumulated through scale of contribution is exactly the right a sound standard must refuse.

Overview

The European Accessibility Act, Directive (EU) 2019/882, began enforcement on 28 June 2025 after a six-year implementation period. Within weeks, two French consumer-rights organisations, ApiDV (Association pour la promotion de l'inclusion par le digital et le vivre-ensemble) and Droit Pluriel, filed emergency injunctions under the référé procedure at the Tribunal judiciaire de Paris against four major French retailers: Auchan, Carrefour, E.Leclerc, and Picard Surgelés. The case relevant to this book's thesis is E.Leclerc, which had already improved its measured compliance from 32% to 50% by the date of filing. The action was filed against it anyway. The signal to regulated organisations is that under EAA enforcement, "we tried" is not a defence; "we published this on this date and here is the proof" is.

Key Details

  • EAA enforcement commencement: 28 June 2025.
  • Lead applicants: ApiDV and Droit Pluriel.
  • Court: Tribunal judiciaire de Paris (Paris civil court of first instance).
  • Procedure: référé (the French emergency-injunction procedure, designed for urgent civil matters where the underlying right is not seriously contested).
  • Respondents: Auchan, Carrefour, E.Leclerc, Picard Surgelés.
  • E.Leclerc's measured compliance trajectory: 32% improving to 50% in the months leading to filing.

Statistical Impact

  • EAA scope covers websites, mobile applications, e-commerce, banking, transport ticketing, e-books, and audiovisual services across the EU's twenty-seven member states.
  • The four respondents collectively operate thousands of physical stores and online estates serving tens of millions of French consumers.
  • The référé procedure is the fastest civil procedure available in French courts: hearings within weeks, orders enforceable immediately upon issue.

Technical Implementation

The directive incorporates WCAG 2.1 Level AA by reference. The French transposition (Ordonnance n° 2023-859) applies to private-sector services above defined thresholds and creates civil liability enforceable by consumer-rights organisations.

The compliance question the court will adjudicate is essentially evidentiary: what did the publisher publish, on what date, in what state of conformance? The publisher's existing materials are not the record the court wants. A live site reflects the state at the date of inspection, not the state at the date the complaint was filed. A Git repository reflects code, not what the server rendered to a visitor. Screenshots are evidence the publisher produced about itself, with no independent way for the receiver to confirm the bytes were not edited.

What survives the procedure is an attested record: the bytes-exact copy of what was served, a publisher signature confirming this is what was published, a registry entry that lets the court (or its expert) query the state at a given time without the publisher's cooperation.

Business Model Implications

EAA enforcement creates the first operational regulatory regime in which machine-readable provenance materially reduces legal exposure. The same semantic-HTML, structured-metadata, accessibility-tagged content that makes a page machine-readable for AI agents also makes it self-attesting for a court. The discovery work the rest of this book argues for and the compliance defence the new regime requires are the same work. There is no separate compliance project; there is one piece of infrastructure with two payoffs.

For organisations operating in the EAA enforcement zone, the question is no longer whether to publish accessible content. The question is whether the publication is in a form that survives the référé procedure when a consumer-rights organisation tests it.

What This Validates

  • The MX Readiness ladder lines up with the litigation case: Level 3 (Attested) is the lowest rung where compliance-defence becomes operational. Below that, the publisher's account of what they published is a story; above it, the account is checkable.
  • The reading layer (RAG, search, agent retrieval) and the publishing layer (MX, attestation, registry) occupy different sides of the publisher / consumer boundary, and the boundary itself is where the value lands. RAG reads. MX proves.

What This Challenges

  • The assumption that pre-emptive partial compliance ("we improved from 32% to 50%, so we are on a trajectory") is a defence. E.Leclerc demonstrates that the trajectory is not the case. The state at the date of filing is the case.
  • The assumption that accessibility regulation is reactive (complaint-driven, individual-grievance-based). The référé procedure with consumer-rights-organisation applicants is structural and proactive.

Architectural Insights

The three properties an MX attestation provides, which the publisher's existing materials do not, are: bytes-exact integrity (this is the file as published, not a reconstruction); publisher-attributed signing (a cryptographic statement that this is what we said under our authority); currency in the registry (queryable for state-at-a-given-time without the publisher's cooperation).

The narrowness of what the publisher attests matters here. The publisher attests this is what I said. The publisher does not attest what I said was accessibility-compliant. The court's compliance verdict is the court's verdict; the publisher's contribution is to make the record of what was published clean enough that the verdict has something firm on which to operate.

Questions Raised

  • Which other directives and regulations will adopt the référé-and-referee pattern? The EU AI Act has parallel structural features. The UK ICO has indicated interest in equivalent enforcement mechanisms. The pattern, once it works once, tends to be copied.
  • What is the operational cost of MX Readiness Level 3 versus the litigation cost of operating without it? The enforcement is now recent enough for the comparison to be empirically grounded rather than theoretical.

Strategic Implications

For retailers in the EAA enforcement zone: Audit current pages against MX Readiness levels. Below Level 3 is operating uninsured. Plan the path to Level 3 against the date of the next référé filing in the sector, not against any internal accessibility plan.

For organisations in adjacent regimes (UK Equality Act, US ADA Title III, similar): The French enforcement is the leading indicator. The same pattern will arrive on the same schedule once the référé mechanism is observed to work.

For standards bodies and registries: This is the regulatory regime under which the value of the attestation infrastructure becomes legible to general counsel. Use the enforcement as the worked example when explaining the architecture to non-technical buyers.

Overview

CCBot collects for Common Crawl, the non-profit public archive that sits underneath the training data of much of the field. The same one-line rule reaches anywhere from a single vendor to most of the industry, and the file itself gives no hint of which.

Key Details

  • Robots.txt surveys of large publisher samples place GPTBot and CCBot among the most-disallowed AI user-agents, at roughly 29 and 27 per cent of sites in one Cloudflare-network analysis. Over the year the top crawlers have increasingly been set to full disallow rather than partial.
  • CCBot is the crawler for Common Crawl, an open repository that many AI companies, research labs, and startups train on rather than crawling the web themselves.
  • A large share of the blocking is a copied security configuration or a plugin default that treats "block AI" as a single setting rather than a list of bots with very different reach.
  • Training reads crawl snapshots taken at a point in time. A site missing from one cycle's archive stays out of whatever that snapshot feeds until a later crawl picks it up.

What This Validates

  • Blocking is a per-crawler decision, not a category switch. One company's bot and the shared-dataset crawler carry costs that differ by orders of magnitude, and an owner blind to that gap cannot price the choice. This is the bot-wall anti-pattern read at the level of the individual user-agent.
  • Reachability is upstream of provenance, and a default-configured block severs it silently. A site can publish clean, well-marked, machine-readable content and still be absent from the archive most models are built on, because a template made the robots.txt call before anyone read it.
  • MX cannot restore a site to a crawl it excluded itself from, but it makes the exclusion legible. A reader who knows which crawler feeds which consumer can write a rule that blocks abuse without erasing the site from the shared dataset on which the industry trains.

What This Validates

Claude Commerce Agents Blueprint (2 September 2026)

Overview

On 2 September 2026 Anthropic published an open reference blueprint for building commerce agents on Claude (Anthropic, 2026a). It contains two agents. A shopping agent a retailer embeds in its own app or website: it searches the catalogue, compares products, plans multi-item purchases, fills the cart, and answers order, returns, and policy questions in the same conversation. A merchant agent the retailer's staff use: it tracks inventory and flags problems, explains sales performance, recommends prices and promotions from the store's own history, and drafts campaigns. Four runnable verticals ship with the repository (retail, travel, telecom, ticketing), each running both agents over the same libraries, together with a Claude Code plugin that scaffolds an agent against a deployment's own systems (Anthropic, 2026b).

The design is merchant-side. The agent runs on the retailer's property, not inside a chatbot. It reaches the retailer's systems only through a backend interface the deployment implements. The cart is rendered and handed to the retailer's own checkout or its platform's hosted checkout; and every merchant write is staged until a person approves it. Anthropic states that it is not moving into commerce: "Claude is your intelligence layer, not a storefront or checkout", with no advertising or paid placement of Claude inside any agent a retailer builds (Anthropic, 2026c).

Key Details

  • Published 2 September 2026. Apache 2.0. Repository anthropics/commerce-agents.
  • A reference implementation, not a product: Anthropic does not maintain the code, does not accept contributions, and offers no service level agreement on it. A deployment forks the repository and owns the result.
  • Each agent is defined once (prompt, skills, tool contracts, gates) and runs on the Messages API, the Claude Agent SDK, and Managed Agents; the code also deploys on Amazon Bedrock, Microsoft Foundry, and Google Cloud Vertex AI.
  • No connectors ship. Both agents reach a retailer's catalogue, cart, order, policy, analytics, inventory, and pricing systems through backend interfaces the deployment implements server-side; the model reads only the result.
  • Safety runs inside the tool call on every path: fencing, provenance gates, caps, memory validation, and the merchant approval gate. The shopping agent is "structurally prevented by the harness from inventing a price or a product"; cart and refund authority have hard limits and anything past them escalates to a person; manipulative upsell patterns are prohibited.
  • Named enterprise customers: Shopify and Priceline. Named solutions partners: Accenture, Mastercard, Visa. Intuit, Klaviyo, and Square are quoted in the announcement. Shopify has published its own implementations of the blueprint over a real store (Shopify, 2026).
  • Anthropic's reported outcome: retailers running shopping agents on Claude see carts up to 35% larger and shoppers 60% more likely to complete a purchase. These are the vendor's own figures, not an independent benchmark.
  • Category: Retail and Commerce Agents.

What This Validates

  • The reasoning layer separated from the rails. The blueprint owns no checkout protocol, no marketplace, and no ad layer; it leaves payment to the retailer's existing checkout or an agentic payments provider. This is the split the Machine Payments Protocol entry above describes from the settlement side: negotiation and settlement are somebody else's protocol, and the agent's job is comprehension and decision. It also inverts the Visa Payment Network in ChatGPT entry: there the agent lived in the chatbot and arrived at the site with a wallet; here the agent lives on the site and hands the money back to the till. The conversation, and the responsibility for what the agent says, return to the retailer's own property.
  • Human-in-Command written into code. Every merchant write is a staged change a person applies; nothing reaches the storefront on the agent's authority. The Protocols' argument that governance is a mechanical gate rather than a policy sentence arrives here as a shipped default. The resulting approval record is the kind of contemporaneous evidence Chapter 7 says a regulator will request when a price changed.
  • A provenance gate moves the trust question; it does not answer it. The gate checks the agent's claim against the backend's answer, so the model cannot invent a price. It cannot make the backend right. The agent will faithfully quote a stale price the catalogue still carries and will have nothing to cite when the returns policy is a PDF the backend cannot turn into text. Because no connectors ship, the record behind the gate is entirely the retailer's. That record is the MX layer: a price that carries its source, its date, and its author; a policy declared as structured, dated, attributed text. REGINALD supplies the other half, an attestation that the catalogue entry is current and its publisher still stands behind it. The blueprint assumes that record exists; most catalogues do not yet hold it. Corroboration over Assertion applies with full force: the blueprint's fence is only as good as the records it encloses.

What This Challenges

  • The assumption that the assistant platform owns the shopper. The book's commerce chapters and the January 2026 platform-race entries model the assistant (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Alexa+) as the place where the purchase decision forms. A credible merchant-side alternative, one that a retailer can fork and own, means some of that decision stays on the retailer's own site. Which model wins is a distribution question the entries above leave open; the technical shape now exists for both.

TypeSafe Releases Jev, a System One Decision Model (15 September 2026)

Overview

On 15 September 2026 TypeSafe AI released Jev. Its founder, Diogo Almeida, describes it as the company's first System One model, "a new class of frontier models built to make fast, structured decisions that software can use directly" (TypeSafe AI, 2026a). Jev never generates text. It takes a state (any text or JSON) and a set of typed questions, and answers each with a distribution over options the caller declared: a Choice from a list, a Score on a defined scale, or a Noul, the probability that a statement is true. It cannot return a value outside the schema (TypeSafe AI, 2026b). The name follows William Stanley Jevons, and the company's own framing expects each drop in the price of a decision to unlock far more uses for it.

Key Details

  • Released 15 September 2026, in early access.
  • The vendor's own figures: end-to-end responses of 70 to 500 milliseconds; input priced at $0.042 per million tokens with output free; forty to two hundred times faster than current large models on this class of task. These are the vendor's measurements, not an independent benchmark.
  • The vendor's evaluations score agreement with the averaged answers of large reference models, so "as good as a frontier model" means matching those models, not being right about the world.
  • The documentation sets a higher confidence bar for a destructive action than for a read-only one, and routes a call below the bar to a person, to a clarifying question, or to another system (TypeSafe AI, 2026c).
  • Within days, public projects built on it: a link-mapper that judged 8,460 link decisions across 566 pages for $0.27, and a router that sets which model and how much reasoning each turn of a coding agent gets.
  • Category: Decision Models and Agent Infrastructure.

What This Validates

  • Thresholds by reversibility. The vendor's guidance is Protocols Chapter 13's rule stated as an inequality: the bar rises with the cost of being wrong, and below it the machine hands the call to a person.
  • The question is the product. The first public tools do the gathering in ordinary code and let the model judge one narrow question. A decision inherits the quality of its question, which in turn depends on the state it reads: the argument for declared metadata, reached from the other direction.

What This Challenges

  • The assumption that cheaper models mean lower spend. The name is the Jevons paradox adopted as a business plan. Per-decision cost falls, decisions multiply, and so do the automated choices nobody recorded. Calibration is honest about the state the model was handed and says nothing about whether it was true, current, or authorised.

Epoch AI Measures the Plunging Price of Thought (22 September 2026)

Overview

On 22 September 2026 Epoch AI published The Plunging Price of Thought, by Luke Emberson and David Roodman. For five benchmarks covering mathematics, hard sciences, and games of skill, the report finds the cheapest way to reach a given score on each date and measures how fast that cost falls. The answer is about 47% per quarter since 2023, or 13 times per year, which the authors describe as "faster than for any other transformative technology in history" (Emberson & Roodman, 2026).

Key Details

  • Published 22 September 2026, with its data and code public.
  • Headline rate: about 47% per quarter (13 times per year), averaged across the five primary benchmarks; slower on game puzzles, at 39-43%, and faster on mathematics, at 50-52%.
  • The sharpest case: OpenAI's o3 reached 75% on GPQA Diamond, a multiple-choice exam in PhD-standard physics, chemistry, and biology, for about 30 cents a question in January 2025. Just under eighteen months later GPT-5.6 Luna matched the score for $0.0004 a question, a 725-fold fall.
  • The fall is steepest when a level of performance first becomes state of the art: 66% per quarter, or 75 times a year. Two years later it is half that, 32% per quarter. The authors suggest that the first provider to reach a level charges a premium until competitors catch up.
  • Epoch's own comparisons put the pace at four times that of DNA sequencing, six times that of compute, 18 times that of lithium batteries, and 54 times that of electricity in the century to 1973.
  • The stated limits: the window covers about three years, benchmark scores stand in for useful work and can be trained towards, and the rate assumes a buyer who always switches to the cheapest capable model.
  • The report measures price at a fixed level of performance. It does not measure how much inference anyone uses.
  • Category: AI Economics and Inference Cost.

What This Validates

  • The Jevons argument has a measured driver. TypeSafe named its decision model Jev after William Stanley Jevons, who argued in The Coal Question that a more efficient use of coal raises total consumption rather than lowering it: "It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth." (Jevons, 1865/1866). The paradox needs a falling price to act on, and this is the steepest fall Epoch has measured for any technology.
  • Declared facts sit outside the price curve. What a file declares is read, not inferred, so its cost does not depend on what inference charges. As inference becomes cheaper and more frequent, the declaration is the part of each read that returns the publisher's answer rather than a fresh estimate.

What This Challenges

  • An economic case framed only as cost per call. If a given capability gets thirteen times cheaper every year, an argument for structure built on the expense of each inference weakens every quarter. The case that survives is accuracy at volume. A local decider measured on fixed questions lost accuracy as its price fell, from 97% on a hosted model to 72% on a three-billion-parameter one. Embedded MX metadata took that small model from 20% to 100% on whether a PDF was current.
  • Reading cheaper inference as lower spend or energy. Epoch measures price, not volume. Enterprise spending on generative AI rose 3.2 times in 2025, to $37 billion, on a count that excludes chips, inference and model serving, and AI features built into existing software (Menlo Ventures, 2025). That is consistent with the paradox, and it is not a measurement of inference volume.

FTC Model-Deletion Orders: Everalbum, WW International and Kurbo, Rite Aid (2021-2024)

Overview

The US Federal Trade Commission has required companies to destroy models built from data they should not have used, a remedy commentators call algorithmic disgorgement. Everalbum, whose photo app applied face recognition without the consent it promised, settled in 2021. WW International and its Kurbo weight-management app, which collected children's personal information without parental consent, settled in 2022 in federal court. Rite Aid, whose in-store facial recognition flagged innocent shoppers as known shoplifters, settled in 2024 and was barred from the technology for five years. None of the three tested the remedy in a contested case; each order was agreed.

Note: This entry describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

Key Details

  • Everalbum and Kurbo define "Affected Work Product" as "any models or algorithms developed in whole or in part using" the data at issue, and require its deletion with a sworn statement confirming it.
  • Everalbum's definition reaches models built from any Ever user's biometric data, consenting or not; only the face records themselves are split by consent status.
  • The Rite Aid complaint alleged the company failed to "implement or enforce any procedure for tracking the rate of false positive matches or actions that were taken based on those false positive matches".
  • The Rite Aid order requires any automated biometric surveillance system to document, for each output, whether it was inaccurate and what action followed, and to tell a person acted against the date, time, and location of the match.
  • None of the orders states a rule that the remedy narrows where lawful and unlawful data can be separated, a reading that circulates widely in commentary.

What This Validates

  • "In whole or in part" puts the burden of proof on the company. Anything it cannot show to be untouched is inside the order. Chapter 7 of the Protocols ("When the Remedy Is Deletion") argues that such proof needs contemporaneous evidence. That means metadata on each data file declaring its origin and training policy, and a provenance trail of every step that turned inputs into weights or outputs into decisions.
  • The record can become the licence to operate. Rite Aid's order makes per-output documentation the condition for running the technology again, which is the provenance discipline MX specifies arrived at by enforcement rather than by design.
  • The exposure travels down the supply chain. An organisation that uses a vendor's model loses it when the vendor complies with an order, and is still asked what it decided. The same holds, in its sharpest form, for anyone who downloads released open weights.

The Erasure Engine: John Kundert on Personal Agents That Exercise Data Rights at Scale (1 September 2026)

Overview

On 1 September 2026 John Kundert published "The Erasure Engine: How Personal AI Will Break the Toxic Ad-Funded Web" in his newsletter The Customer's Sovereign Agent. He argues that an AI agent native to the phone will exercise its owner's data rights continuously. Where a relationship with a site is dormant, the agent asks for erasure. Where it is active, the agent uses the site's own privacy settings to clear tracking logs, search history and off-site activity on a schedule. Kundert's precedent is Apple's App Tracking Transparency prompt, introduced in iOS in April 2021, which made cross-app tracking opt-in. Most people declined, and Meta's finance chief told investors the change would cost the company around $10 billion in a year. Kundert's point is that an agent asking on every platform, permanently, is the same mechanism without the single gatekeeper. He calls the profile a platform builds from years of observation "behavioural DNA".

Note: This entry describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

Key Details

  • The rights the agent would use already exist in many jurisdictions: access to personal data and its erasure under the EU and UK GDPR, with comparable laws in Brazil and California. Kundert's argument is that people rarely use them because the friction is high, not because the rights are weak.
  • He names the gap himself: an organisation may interpret, challenge or refuse a request that comes from an automated system, and the law may need refining to close it.
  • He sees three steps that would take adoption beyond activists and the well informed: agents that remove the friction, phones that do the housekeeping natively, and a vendor that markets privacy as a feature.
  • The commercial consequence he predicts is that advertising money moves rather than vanishes: to contextual placement, to trusted publishers, and to retail media built on first-party purchase history.
  • He cites the European Commission's €200 million fine on Meta's "pay or consent" model, issued under the Digital Markets Act in April 2025, as a sign that platforms will try to sell privacy back as a premium product.
  • An earlier essay, "Agentic AI: It's going to screw who?" (7 July 2026), sets out the commercial half of the argument. An agent that compares offers continuously ends the loyalty penalty, the price rise that follows an introductory discount. When millions of agents read the same market-leading offer, they can move together, which he compares to a murmuration, with systemic risk for any provider they leave at once. In that essay the same agent already files deletion requests whenever it judges the data to work against its owner.
  • His advice to businesses is to prepare for subject access requests, automated clean-up signals and deletions at scale, and to know what share of revenue depends on data held without a durable, consented right.
  • Category: Consumer Agents and Data Rights.

What This Validates

  • The person's agent is a machine reader with a principal. Joymaker is built on the same premise: personal data stays with the owner, who decides through software what each site receives. Kundert extends the premise from what is shared next to what was shared before.
  • Friction is the barrier, and structure removes it. An agent can only clear a relationship it can find and reach. A site that declares, in machine-readable form, where rights requests go and what its privacy settings do can be used without guessing. A site that hides them behind a dark pattern cannot. The W3C Data Privacy Vocabulary can already describe where and how each right is exercised; what is missing is an open way for a page to point to that description.

What This Challenges

  • A request without a record. At the volume Kundert describes, both parties need evidence. The organisation must show what it did with each request, against its deadline. The agent must show what it asked for, on whose authority, and what came back. A business can dismiss as "automated" any demand that arrives with no proof that a person stands behind it. MX gives each party its own evidence: an obligation record for the organisation, and for the agent a provenance trail whose human-check step is the owner's approval.
  • A machine-readable offer is read by every agent at once. Structured terms remove the misreading. They also let millions of agents act in the same minute. A publisher that declares an offer to machines should also declare its limits (eligibility, capacity, duration), so that agents can tell a real fit from a headline rate. schema.org's Offer already has properties for each, and its billingStart property declares the price rise after an introductory period.
  • Deletion is not the same as a clean slate. Erasing the source data does not remove a profile or a model already derived from it. The FTC orders above show a regulator reaching for that derived work product; Kundert's proposed right to reset a behavioural profile without closing the account asks the same question from the person's side.

Personal Agent Protocol, PACT, and the Shopper Context Protocol: The Consent Layer of Agentic Commerce (October 2025 - October 2026)

Overview

On 6 October 2026 Sierra and Meta announced the Personal Agent Protocol (PAP), an open standard for how a personal AI agent authenticates with a business and what that business lets it do. Sierra's founders, Bret Taylor and Clay Bavor, describe its aim as authentication plus visibility of what agents are doing on a site. Genesys, Instinct, Rocket, Shopify, Stripe and Walmart are named as partners; Amazon, OpenAI and Anthropic are not part of the initiative. A v0.1 specification is due in October 2026 (The Next Web, 2026).

On the same day Decagon open-sourced the Personal Agent Consent and Trust Protocol (PACT), co-developed with Instinct (Decagon, 2026). PACT addresses one exchange in particular: a customer's personal agent contacting a business's support agent. It builds on Agent2Agent (A2A) and OAuth 2.0.

A third standard predates both. The Shopper Context Protocol (SCP) is an open standard for carrying shopper preferences, loyalty status and purchase intent across agents, channels and retail platforms. Cordial joined it in October 2025, with its chief product and engineering officer, Matt Howland, chairing the working group (Cordial, 2025), and the Retail AI Council formed an SCP working group in December 2025.

Together they form the consent layer of agentic commerce: who the agent is, who it speaks for, what it may do, and what the shopper has agreed to share.

Key Details

  • PAP builds on OAuth. The agent identifies itself, declares its intent and acts within permissions the user granted, which may allow reading only or writing as well, and which persist across channels. A business can accept the agent through its website, through an API such as MCP or OpenAPI, or by routing it to the business's own agent.
  • PACT involves three parties: the customer's personal agent, the business, and the provider hosting the business's agent. The personal agent discovers the business's A2A Agent Card and signs every request, so the business knows which agent is calling. It requests scopes through OAuth's device authorisation flow: the customer signs in with the business directly, and the agent never sees the password. When a conversation needs further permission, PACT uses A2A's authorisation-required state and continues the same conversation.
  • SCP is discoverable: a retailer declares SCP support through DNS records, and an assistant queries shopper context through OAuth-style permissions.
  • These protocols complement rather than replace the checkout and payment protocols (ACP, UCP, AP2) and the settlement rails (x402, MPP) covered in earlier entries. They govern the relationship between a person's agent and a business, not the transaction itself.
  • Category: Standards and Protocol Announcements; Security and Identity Solutions.

What This Validates

  • Agents need a declared identity and a declared mandate. The book argues that a business cannot serve an agent well while it has to guess who the agent is. PAP and PACT make identity and delegated authority explicit, the same move MX makes for content: state what a reader needs instead of leaving it to inference.
  • Consent belongs to the person, held close to them. These protocols adopt a minimum-disclosure model: a business asks for what a purpose needs and receives no more. The personal cog (Appendix M, 22.AG) applies the same model through disclosure tiers. An item may stay on the device, travel as a derived preference, or be shared whole on a transaction the person starts.

What This Challenges

  • Treating permission as trust. Each protocol governs the exchange: who may send what, to whom, under which terms. None describes the content being sent. An agent can be fully authorised to tell a restaurant that its user is vegetarian. No protocol in the stack can say whether that statement was typed by the user years ago, confirmed by an earlier booking, or inferred by the agent from order history. An authorised claim is still unchecked. The receiving machine has no stake in its own errors, so the means to check a disclosed item has to travel with it. That means its source, its claim basis (asserted or corroborated, Appendix M, 22.AH), its intended use, and when it was last stated or confirmed. Permission answers "may this be shared?"; provenance answers "how is this known, and is it still true?"
  • Assuming one consent standard. PAP, PACT and SCP overlap, and AyataCommerce is drafting a further open RFC, the Shopper Graph Protocol, built on purpose-bound questions and minimum disclosure. A business adopting one should keep the meaning of the payload independent of the protocol carrying it, so that the same labelled data serves whichever standard its customers' agents use.

Collate 2.0 and OpenMetadata: Data Catalogs Rebuilt as Context Layers (June - August 2026)

Overview

Products marketed as data catalogues in 2024 were, by 2026, sold as context layers, context engines and context graphs, and conference agendas grouped them as one category. The demand behind the new name is real. Organisations that pointed agents at their data found that a language model cannot tell which of several definitions of "active customer" the finance team uses, because that knowledge was never recorded in a form a machine can read. The label grew faster than agreement on what it means: a context layer can turn out to be a semantic search index, a prompt assembly service, or a business glossary with a chat window attached.

Collate is one of the clearer cases in the category. It is the commercial platform built on OpenMetadata, an Apache 2.0 open source project that describes an organisation's data estate as an inventory of typed, versioned entities: tables, topics, dashboards, pipelines, models, containers and APIs, with their lineage, owners, quality tests, glossaries and policies. Its founders, Suresh Srinivas and Sriharsha Chintalapani, worked on Apache Hadoop, Apache Atlas and Apache Kafka, and built Databook, Uber's internal data inventory, before founding Collate. Collate announced version 2.0 on 10 June 2026, describing it as an AI-native release built on a semantic context graph (Collate, 2026). The company states that general availability followed on 25 August 2026, with the previous product maintained as Collate Classic.

The release is organised around three primitives: Metadata (the graph of sources, assets, lineage, terms and policies), Meaning (the ontology, metrics and data products that ground assets in business terms) and Memory (corrections, definitions and preferences captured once, attributed to their authors, and inherited by later users and agents).

Key Details

  • The schema comes first. Every entity type is defined in a published JSON Schema before the code that implements it. The company states that the specification holds more than 700 schemas. The Java server classes, the Python and TypeScript SDK types and the OpenAPI description are generated from it, so the running system cannot drift from its own documentation.
  • A published crosswalk to W3C standards. The documentation maps each element to RDF, OWL and SKOS. A glossary is both an owl:Ontology and a skos:ConceptScheme; each of its terms is both an owl:Class and a skos:Concept, and mappings to external vocabularies are exported as skos:exactMatch, closeMatch and the other SKOS match properties, or owl:sameAs (OpenMetadata, 2026a). It reuses DCAT, PROV-O, Dublin Core Terms, DQV, FOAF, ODRL and CSVW rather than inventing its own terms, and declares its data asset class equivalent to dcat:Dataset, so a DCAT-aware consumer reads the catalogue without a custom adapter.
  • Typed relations between terms. Relations between glossary terms come in hierarchical, associative and equivalence families. Each type declares its RDF predicate, its symmetry or transitivity, and if it may cross glossaries. Finance can record that its "churn" is equivalent to "attrition" in the product team's vocabulary.
  • Validation and provenance in the same standards. Shapes in SHACL check cardinality, datatypes, identifier formats and required ownership. Lineage is expressed in PROV-O. JSON-LD contexts ship for each domain of the model, so an entity exports as linked data without transformation.
  • The graph is derived, and says so. The relational database is the system of record. The RDF knowledge graph is projected into an Apache Jena Fuseki triple store for SPARQL queries, OWL reasoning and SHACL validation at scale. It is labelled Beta and described as a "derived index, not a source of truth" that can always be rebuilt (OpenMetadata, 2026b). A status endpoint reports whether the projection is ready, rebuilding or degraded.
  • Inferred and asserted stay apart. Inferred triples are written to separate named graphs and marked as inferred, so a tag a rule derived is never mistaken for one a person curated, and each authored relation records who created it and when (OpenMetadata, 2026a).
  • Agent access and AI labelling. 2.0 adds conversational query over governed context, no-code agent authoring, natural-language automations, a Model Context Protocol server, and an AI governance module that logs every AI action and labels AI-generated content at the point of consumption.
  • The vendor's own measurements. Collate's announcement claims that a language model picks the right data asset for a prompt about 10% of the time without such context (Collate, 2026). Collate also reports a text-to-SQL evaluation on a subset of Spider 2.0-Snow in which execution accuracy rose from 59% to 94% with its context layer in scope and the model held constant. Both figures are the vendor's own and have not been independently reproduced.
  • Category: Ecosystem Maturity Signals; Standards and Protocol Announcements.

What This Validates

  • Meaning has to be declared, not inferred. The "active customer" problem is the book's thesis moved from the web page to the warehouse. A model that cannot tell four definitions apart is not short of intelligence; the distinction was never written down where it can be read. The vendor's benchmark, whatever its exact figures, points the same way: with the model held constant, what the machine was given decided the result.
  • A four-question test. A useful way to judge a product in this category is to ask what its context consists of, who declared it, which standard it conforms to, and whether it can be exported from the platform that stores it. A context layer earns the name only when its declarations can be inspected, validated and exported. MX holds itself to the same test: its fields are carried in the file, name their standards, and leave with the content.
  • Defer to existing standards. Collate builds on JSON Schema, SKOS, OWL, SHACL, PROV-O and DCAT rather than a private vocabulary, and the ownership argument follows from that choice: context described in standards governed outside the vendor belongs to the organisation that wrote it. Protocols Chapter 21 sets the same rule for MX fields.
  • Description before system. Defining every entity before writing the code that handles it is the discipline of a metadata application profile, and of cataloguing rules such as AACR2 and RDA before it: elements defined, obligations declared, encoding schemes named. The outcome is metadata that is structured, validated, versioned and machine-readable by construction.
  • How a statement is known travels with it. Keeping inferred triples apart from curated ones, and stamping each relation with who made it, is the claim-basis distinction of Appendix M, 22.AH, applied to a knowledge graph.
  • Honesty about what is derived. Labelling the graph Beta and naming it a rebuildable index, with a status endpoint that answers whether it can be trusted right now, is the candour the book asks of any system that serves machines.

What This Challenges

  • Context that stays in the estate. A catalogue describes the data an organisation holds, and its context is richest within the platform. The content an organisation publishes leaves that platform: a page, a PDF or a data file read by someone else's agent arrives without the catalogue behind it. Export as JSON-LD makes the description portable, but only if it is exported and travels with the item. MX puts the declaration in the file itself, so the means to check it arrives wherever the item goes. The two layers are complementary: one governs meaning inside the estate, the other carries meaning beyond its boundary.
  • Vendor figures are claims. The 10% and 59%-to-94% figures come from Collate, on a benchmark the company selected and ran. They are consistent with the book's argument, and a reader should still treat them as the vendor's assertion until someone else reproduces them. Corroboration, not assertion, is what lets a machine rely on a number.
  • A crowded category with a loose name. Palantir, Alation, Databricks, Atlan and Microsoft Fabric all compete for the same "context" label. Few publish the makeup of their context. Buyers should expect the four-question test above to separate the products quickly.

UK Healthcare AI Moves From Point-in-Time Approval to Lifecycle Assurance, and AI Airlock Phase 3 Opens (6 October 2026)

Overview

On 6 October 2026 the UK Government accepted all 44 recommendations of the National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare. The Commission is an independent advisory body, chaired by Professor Alastair Denniston, that the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, the MHRA, set up in September 2025. It published its recommendations on 10 September 2026 after a year of evidence gathering involving more than 12,000 people (MHRA, 2026a). Its central conclusion is that AI-enabled medical devices should be assessed and monitored throughout their working life, with "less reliance on point-in-time pre-market assessments" (MHRA, 2026b). On the same day the MHRA opened applications for the third phase of AI Airlock, its regulatory sandbox for AI as a medical device, with post-market surveillance and lifecycle oversight as its priority challenge area (MHRA, 2026c).

Note: This entry describes regulatory frameworks in general terms only. Nothing here is legal advice. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, organisation type, and use case. Consult qualified legal specialists for guidance specific to your situation.

Key Details

  • After deployment. The response commits the MHRA to "a conscious shift towards ongoing assurance rather than overreliance on pre-market assessments and passive adverse event reporting". An enhanced surveillance toolkit follows, whose mechanisms can be required singly or together (MHRA, 2026b).
  • Change control with declared boundaries. The MHRA will issue draft guidance on Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs) for public comment by December 2026. The proposed approach lets a manufacturer "establish boundaries to define the scope of allowable change" for an adaptive device while keeping its intended use, rather than pre-specifying each modification. Monitoring and reporting duties are to be written into the PCCP, so the device is watched while it iterates.
  • Staged authorisation. The MHRA will explore staged pathways, using proposed licensing powers and regulatory sandboxes, so that promising tools can be used in the NHS earlier under close supervision while further real-world evidence is gathered. NICE is to consider how evidence generated in a staged pathway can support its appraisals.
  • Operational conditions. Manufacturers are to identify, describe and communicate the conditions a device needs for safe deployment and use, and the MHRA will consider how those conditions enter future regulatory requirements.
  • Responsibility across the lifecycle. A working group of the DHSC, the MHRA, the CQC, professional regulators and their devolved equivalents will clarify how responsibility is divided between manufacturers and providers.
  • AI Airlock Phase 3. The priority topics are ongoing assurance that captures deployment-context factors (local infrastructure, workflow integration, data quality, user interaction); surveillance where clinical or organisational oversight is limited, as in direct-to-consumer or higher-autonomy use; and communicating performance, safety, updates and rollbacks to regulators, providers, professionals and patients. Testing runs in simulation, virtual and real-world (shadow deployment) environments. The current review wave closes on 31 October 2026, the first participants are selected in November 2026, and the phase is funded to April 2029 (MHRA, 2026c).
  • Delivery. A cross-system Programme Board oversees ten priority areas. An implementation plan with timelines for every recommendation is due by Spring 2027.
  • Roland Brüggemann of EVELIQ Trace drew the conclusion for assurance: approval at deployment does not assure the lifecycle. His chain runs from the initial claim through evidence, validation, decision, authorisation and deployment. It continues through real-world observation, change and revalidation to a current authorisation and a verified effect. The question he asks after deployment is whether "the evidence that justified approval still describe[s] the system that is operating today" (Brüggemann, 2026b).
  • Category: Governance; Agent Platforms and Agent Assurance.

What This Validates

  • A record must say which state of the system it describes. An approval, a validation report or a test result is evidence about a model, its data, its configuration and its deployment context at one moment. When any of those change, the evidence still exists but no longer describes the running system. The book's position is that every item should carry what a reader needs to check it. For a system that changes, it includes the version tested and the conditions of the test. With those recorded, a reader can tell current evidence from a historical record.
  • A declared boundary is checkable; an open-ended permission is not. A PCCP that states the scope of allowable change turns "the device may adapt" into a limit a machine can check every modification for before accepting it. That is the MX pattern of writing intended use as a declaration rather than leaving it to inference.
  • Permission to use is conditional, and the conditions should travel with it. Staged authorisation makes "authorised for controlled use" distinct from "authorised for general use". A record of the decision that omits its scope, its conditions and the evidence it is waiting for will read as unconditional to any machine that consumes it.

What This Challenges

  • Treating approval as permanent. Supplier documentation, procurement records and published product pages often state that a device is approved without saying which version, for which use, under which conditions, or as of when. Under a lifecycle regime those statements age. Metadata that records the approved version, the scope, the conditions and the date last confirmed lets a reader see when a statement has gone stale. Without it, the approval reads as permanent.
  • Monitoring that leaves no record of its effect. The Phase 3 topics ask how surveillance signals are acted on and communicated. A monitoring control in place shows intent; it does not show that anything was caught or that anyone responded. The Claude Code entry above records the same gap between a configured control and an effective one, and the record should say which of the two it holds.

A Practitioner Counts the Release Pace: Something New on Almost Every Working Day (3 October 2026)

Overview

On 3 October 2026 Andrei Kalamkarov, who leads the team that develops and maintains Concordia University's CMS, published "Using AI every day, and still falling behind". He had used AI coding tools daily since the GitHub Copilot beta in April 2022. Preparing a talk for his university's learning community showed him how many features and ways of working he had never tried. His explanation is that a familiar way of working keeps working, so "nothing breaks to tell me there was a better way" (Kalamkarov, 2026).

Key Details

  • The count. From Anthropic's and OpenAI's own release notes, Kalamkarov found something new on 179 of the 196 weekdays from 1 January to 1 October 2026, and several of the days with nothing were holidays. Claude Code alone published more than 200 releases in that period.
  • Two weeks as an example. OpenAI's GPT-6 Sol and Anthropic's Opus 5.5 shipped on 22 September 2026, two days before his session, and one of his demonstrations had already run on the previous models. In the following week came Sonnet 5.5, OpenAI's Dots (always-on agents, each with a cloud computer of its own), Gemini 4, and Claude Code mods.
  • From answering to doing, to doing on its own. His timeline runs from chat (November 2022) through tool calls, multi-file edits, computer use, MCP connectors, agents in the terminal, cloud agents and skills, to agents working in the browser (2026). The 2026 shifts all let the agent continue unwatched: self-testing agents, scheduled and event-triggered automations, goals, always-on personal agents, multi-agent workflows, event subscriptions and coordinating agents. Several vendors shipped the same idea within days or months of each other.
  • No manual. He cites Andrej Karpathy's December 2025 post, "I've never felt this much behind as a programmer", which describes a new layer of work, from briefing agents to deciding what they may do, with tools that are fallible and keep changing (Karpathy, as cited in Kalamkarov, 2026). Most networking and cloud certifications stay valid for three years; almost none of the ways of working Kalamkarov describes existed three years earlier.
  • Output and load. His team's release notes listed 155 changes in 2023-24, 184 in 2024-25 and 283 in 2025-26. The work got faster and harder at once: less routine typing, longer stretches of full attention, and every addition still reviewed, tested, kept accessible and maintained.
  • Category: Agent Platforms and Agent Assurance; Practitioner Evidence.

What This Validates

  • The machine audience is not fixed. The book treats agents as a class of reader, not a single product. When the tools that read a site change on most working days, content written for one tool's habits ages within weeks. Content that declares its own context in standard metadata does not depend on which agent reads it next.
  • A record must be dated to mean anything. Kalamkarov's demonstration was correct on the day it was built and out of date on the day it was shown. An audit result, a readiness score or a compliance statement is evidence about one state of one estate, read by the agents of one moment. Each needs its date and the conditions it was measured under, so a reader can tell when it has gone stale.
  • Rules outlast tool lists. His team found that rules about data hold up longer than lists of approved tools. That is the book's position on metadata: describe the content and its intended use, and let each tool read the description, rather than tuning content to a named tool.

What This Challenges

  • The one-off audit. A point-in-time audit describes the agents that existed when it ran. At this release pace, a site and its PDFs can fall behind without a single page changing, because the readers changed. Measurement has to repeat on a cadence that matches the change, and each run has to be compared with the dated record before it.
  • Daily use as proof of currency. A team that uses agents every day can still miss most of the year's shifts. Being busy with a tool is not the same as knowing what it can now do.
