[wordpress]
The 2024 dispute in the WordPress ecosystem, in which a hosting provider's access to the central plugin directory was restricted, is the worked example. It is treated at length in the series post "Whose Standard Is It Anyway?" Confirm the framing remains current before each print run.
[fair]
FAIR (Federated And Independent Repositories), a Linux Foundation effort launched in 2025 to decentralise package distribution for one large CMS ecosystem; its work for that ecosystem stalled for want of funding, and development continued in the TYPO3 community from early 2026. Its founders observed that neutrality the ecosystem will not fund does not materialise. See "Not the Main Sponsor" and "The Spec Was Never the Fragile Part.".
[vendor]
For the vendor-conflict argument and its historical cases, a publishing format captured through control of the infrastructure that favoured it, and a document standard pushed through a standards committee by weight of membership, see "A Rule You Sell Is Not a Standard." The contrasting tradition is the IETF's "rough consensus and running code.".
[osi]
The Open Source Initiative found that "open source" was too descriptive to register as a trademark and fell back on a separate certification mark for approved licences, relying on a public definition and on vigilance. The doctrine that an unenforced mark is forfeit is "naked licensing." See "The Badge and the Body.".
[hub]
The distinction between a registry that grants trust and one that merely points to it, and the observation that even federated systems tend to rebuild a central point for discovery and trust, are developed in "The Spec Was Never the Fragile Part," including its section on why a federated system still has a hub and MX does not.
[ietf]
IETF RFC 2026, section 4.1.2, requires at least two independent and interoperable implementations from different code bases before a specification advances on the standards track; a later clarification defines independence as different people, organisations, code and libraries. See "Two Implementations or It Isn't a Standard.".
[eee]
The "embrace, extend, extinguish" pattern, named in internal memos leaked in 1998, was applied to a cross-platform programming language and to a network authentication protocol whose proprietary extension was published under terms that blocked third-party implementation. See "Capture Happens at Version Two.".
[forks]
The licence-change forks of the 2020s, a configuration tool forked to a foundation-held successor after a restrictive relicensing in 2023, and an in-memory data store similarly forked within weeks in 2024, are the worked examples, including the point that the foundation-held forks drew wider organisational support than the originals. See "Exit Is the Only Real Vote.".
[aiact]
Under the EU AI Act, conformity with the harmonised standards confers a presumption of conformity for high-risk systems; the standards ran late and the application of the high-risk rules was tied to their availability, deferring obligations toward firm dates in 2027 and 2028. The European Accessibility Act, in force from 2025, is the nearer precedent. See "When the Law Points at Your Standard," and confirm the regulatory dates before each print run.
[weights]
A trained model's weights are neither code nor content, and the open-source community has not settled what openness requires of them; the integrity layer for weights is arriving from the code-signing world, while the readable, attested provenance record is the part MX fits. This is a boundary on MX's claims, not a claim. See "Neither Code nor Content.".
