# The Desk in the Photo: What the Machine Fills In When You Stop Telling It

This post owes its spark to [Garrett Sussman](https://www.linkedin.com/in/garrettsussman/), Director of Marketing at iPullRank, who described the episode below in a post on LinkedIn. The observation is his; the argument I hang on it is mine.

Garrett Sussman asked Google's AI Mode for desk accessories. Nothing exotic — a cup holder, maybe a riser, something that would fit the look of his office. He didn't upload a photo. He didn't describe the desk. He asked a vague, human question the way you'd ask a colleague who'd been to your place.

So the machine went and looked. It pulled photos of his desk out of Google Photos — one from June 2023, one from December 2021 — read off the big wooden desk and the industrial aesthetic, and recommended accordingly. The suggestions, he said, were actually solid.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the whole of where search is going, compressed into a cup holder. He asked for less. The machine supplied the rest from what it already held. And the rest was a photograph of his office taken two years before the question.

## The new bargain: ask less, let it infer more

The old query was a confession of context. You typed *best desk accessories industrial wood office* and you carried the whole burden of saying what you meant. The machine knew only what you put in the box. If it guessed wrong, it had guessed from your words, and you could see why.

The new query is a shrug. *Help me find something for my desk.* And the bargain underneath it — Sussman names it exactly — is that "as we get more comfortable with AI Search, we provide less explicit context. We ask more natural questions. We expect our AI ecosystem of choice to understand our preferences, history, relationships, purchases, photos, emails, calendar, and habits."

This is sold as intimacy, and on a good day it reads like it. The machine infers context you didn't explicitly provide. You didn't describe the desk; it knew the desk. The query stops being "best desk accessories" and becomes, in his words, "what should I buy that fits my desk, my room, my taste, my prior purchases, and the products already in my life?" That is a better question. It is the question a thoughtful friend would actually answer.

But a friend who knows you keeps the picture current. The machine does not. It answered a 2026 question with a 2023 desk, and the only reason the desk was 2023 is that 2023 is the last time he happened to photograph it. As he put it: his desk has changed since those photos were taken, he hasn't added new pictures, so Google was "technically using real context, but potentially stale context." The recommendations still worked. This time. On a cup holder, the cost of stale context is nearly zero.

Move the same mechanism off the desk and the bill comes due.

## This is the old failure wearing new clothes

Regular readers of this series will feel the floor give way here, because we have stood on this exact spot before, just facing a different direction.

The whole argument has been one thing said many ways: a machine reads the surface and emits the likely continuation, and where the surface leaves a thing implicit, it fills the gap by inference — and its favourite inference is to take a *state* and report it as a *fact*. Spanish would make you choose between *es* and *está*, between what a thing is and how it happens to be right now; English hands the machine a single "is" and lets it default to the permanent reading. A company *was* under investigation, and the answer engine renders what it *is*. A person *was* between jobs, and the machine launders the passing condition into a defining one.

The desk is the same move, photographed. A desk that *was* a certain way in 2023 becomes the desk that *is*. Sussman caught it because he knows his own office and the stakes were a cup holder. Nobody catches it when the subject is a company they've never visited and the stale state is the one that costs the deal. "Personalization can make recommendations feel smarter," he writes. "It can also make them confidently outdated." Confidently is the word that should keep you up. The machine does not hedge the way the photo's age would justify. It does not say *based on how your desk looked two years ago*. It just answers, in the flat present tense of a thing that knows.

And the supply of stale implicit context is only growing, because the bargain runs in exactly the wrong direction. The more natural our questions get, the less we say out loud — and the more the machine reaches for whatever it already holds to cover the difference. We are training ourselves to under-specify and training the machine to over-infer, and the two habits feed each other. Every question we strip down is a gap the machine fills from the archive, and the archive is always older than the question.

## You no longer control the prompt — so control the page

Here is the turn that should reorganise how a brand thinks, and Sussman gets to its edge before the post ends: "We need to think beyond SEO on our site."

He is right, and it is worth saying why in the terms this series uses. SEO was a discipline for a world where you optimised for the *query* — the words in the box, the keyword the user typed, the intent you could read off a search-console export. (Mark Williams-Cook's regex for slicing query data by intent — informational, comparison, transactional, navigational — is a beautiful artefact of exactly that world, and it still works, because the box still exists.) But when the query collapses into *help me find something for my desk*, the words in the box stop carrying the meaning. The meaning has moved into the machine's model of you, assembled from photos and purchases and habits you never consciously submitted. You cannot optimise the prompt any more, because the prompt is no longer where the decision is made.

What you can still do — the only thing left to do — is govern what the machine reads *about the entity*, and make sure the current truth is the legible one. If the desk has changed, the page is where you say so. Not your desk; the thing you actually own, the page about your company, your product, your work. When the machine assembles its model of you, it will reach for the most available signals: an old review, a two-year-old product spec, a press mention from the bad quarter, a partnership that lapsed. Those are your 2023 photos. They will be used as context whether you offer them or not, and they will be read in the present tense unless something more current and more explicit says otherwise.

So the instruction the series always arrives at lands again, sharpened by the new shape of the threat. Marianne Sweeny, in the same thread, puts the principle plainly: "We cannot optimize generative search because it is generative" — it is dynamic, it changes by instructions we don't see — and so the work is "understanding machine behavior, and using that understanding to optimize site content and technical structure." Understand that the machine *will* fill the gap. Understand that it fills it from whatever is available and reads the result as now. Then make the current truth the most available, most explicit, most clearly-dated thing it can find.

That is the same discipline, every time. Date the facts that are states. Mark what is current as current and what is permanent as permanent. Say which relationships and partnerships are live and which have ended, because the machine cannot tell a lapsed alliance from a standing one unless the form tells it. Put the present desk on the page, in words and structure a machine can read, so it does not have to go rummaging in the photo library of the web for a desk you no longer have.

## The same context, pointed at you

There is a colder edge to this, and it's worth naming before the post closes, because the desk story has a twin.

The context that powers the helpful answer is the same context the platform retains. A separate strand of the same conversation worries about how much non-public, personal material — drafts, messages, documents, the photographs of your office — now flows into AI tools and how long it lingers there. I won't relay the specific retention claims being passed around; they're contested and they're not mine to certify. But the structural point survives the details intact: the photo of the desk is *useful* and *kept* by the same act. The implicit context you stopped having to provide didn't stop existing. It moved into a store you don't manage, ages there, and gets read back to you — and to others asking about you — as the present.

You can't opt out of being inferred. That ship sailed when the query became a shrug. What you can do is make sure that when the machine reaches for the truth about you, the most legible thing it finds is the true one, current as of a date you put there on purpose. The page about you is the last surface where you still hold the pen. The desk in the photo will be used either way. Your only move is to make the real one easier to read.
