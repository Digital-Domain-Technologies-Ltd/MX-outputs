# The Languages That Won't Let You Leave It Implicit

In English you can say "the bridge collapsed" without ever admitting whether you saw it happen, read it in the paper, or are guessing from the traffic. The sentence carries no fingerprint of how you came to know it. You are free to leave that out, and most of the time you do.

In Turkish you are not free. The verb itself makes you choose. *Köprü çöktü* — with the suffix **-dü** — says the bridge collapsed and I stand behind it; I was there, or as good as. *Köprü çökmüş* — with **-müş** — says the bridge collapsed, so I gather; I'm reporting it, or inferring it, but I did not witness it. There is no neutral form that ducks the question. To speak the sentence at all, you have to declare your relationship to the fact.

Once you notice this, you start seeing it everywhere — a whole family of languages that grammaticalise the very things English lets you keep vague. And it is worth a writer's attention, because it turns out human grammars have been quietly practising the discipline this whole series has been arguing for: putting the meaning into the form, where it can't be lost.

## The interesting category is not what a language *can* say

Every language can say everything. You can express "I didn't see it myself" in English in four words, and a Turk can express subtle hedges of doubt that English handles with a raised eyebrow. The translatability of human languages is near-total; anyone who tells you a thought is literally unthinkable in English is usually selling something.

So the interesting category is not what a language *can* say. It is what a language **won't let you not say** — the distinctions it makes *obligatory*, the blanks it refuses to let you leave blank. English makes tense obligatory: you cannot utter a verb without placing it in time, even when time is beside the point. English does *not* make evidence obligatory, or certainty, or who exactly "we" includes. Other languages draw that line in different places, and where they draw it tells you what that language has decided a listener should never have to guess.

Here are the ones worth knowing.

## Provenance, baked into the verb

Turkish marks whether you witnessed something. Some languages go much further. **Tariana**, spoken by a few thousand people in the northwest Amazon and documented in loving detail by the linguist Alexandra Aikhenvald, has *five* evidential markers, and you must attach one to the verb. There is a form for "I saw it," another for "I heard it but didn't see it," another for "I infer it from evidence," another for "I assume it from general knowledge," and another for "someone told me." *"The dog bit the man"* is not a complete Tariana sentence. It is a sentence with a hole in it, and the hole is: how do you know?

**Quechua** does the same job with three little particles — *-mi* for direct knowledge, *-si* for "they say," *-chá* for conjecture — and a Quechua speaker drops them into ordinary talk the way an English speaker drops articles, without thinking, all day long. To a Quechua ear, a string of unmarked assertions sounds not confident but *evasive* — like a witness who won't say how he came by his story.

Stop on that, because this whole series has spent a lot of words on it under a different name. *Provenance.* When a German court ruled this month that Google authors what its AI Overviews say, the missing thing — the thing the machine erased when it blended a hundred sources into one confident summary — was exactly this: which claim came from where, and how solid each one was. Tariana grammar would not permit the erasure. The provenance is welded to the verb. You literally cannot generate the sentence without it.

## Whose knowledge is this?

Tibetan and Newar do something subtler still, called **egophoricity** (or, in the older books, the conjunct–disjunct distinction). The verb form changes depending on whether the *speaker* is the one who knows, intends, or controls the action. "I went" and "he went" take different verb shapes not because of person agreement as English knows it, but because *my* going is something I have privileged, intentional access to and *his* going is something I merely observed. It is the Turkish instinct — firsthand versus not — turned inward and made to track whose mind the knowledge sits in. Almost nobody outside linguistics has heard of it, and it is one of the most elegant things grammar does.

## Surprise gets its own grammar

Several languages have a dedicated marker for **mirativity** — information that is *new and unexpected to the speaker*, distinct from how you came to know it. Not "I heard that it's raining" but "oh — it's *raining*," the grammatical shrug of a fact that just landed on you. English smuggles this in with intonation and the word "oh." Other languages give the experience of being caught off guard its own honest morpheme.

## Two words for "we"

English "we" is permanently ambiguous and we have all suffered for it. "We need to talk about your performance" — does "we" include you, or is it management's *we*, the one you are not part of? Dozens of languages refuse to tolerate the blur. Tok Pisin, the lingua franca of Papua New Guinea, has **yumi** ("you and me") and **mipela** ("me and them, but not you"). This **clusivity** runs through much of Austronesian and through Quechua. In those languages the boss cannot hide inside an ambiguous "we." The grammar makes him pick a side.

## Part of you, or just yours

Many languages of Oceania and the Americas grammatically split **inalienable** from **alienable** possession. *My arm*, *my mother*, *my name* — things that are part of you or define you — take one construction. *My book*, *my canoe*, *my money* — things that merely happen to be yours — take another. There are two different words for "my," and choosing the wrong one is a grammatical error, not a philosophical one. The nature of the relationship is carried in the form, not left for the listener to work out from the noun.

## The social position you can't avoid stating

In Japanese and Korean, the relationship between speaker and listener is not a matter of word choice you might get around to — it is conjugated into the verb. Japanese *keigo* and the Korean speech levels force every sentence to take a position on who outranks whom. You cannot form a grammatical sentence that is neutral about hierarchy; politeness is not an optional politeness, it is structural. English gestures at this with "sir" and a softer tone. These languages make it load-bearing.

## Time as a ruler with marked distances

And to close the loop on the example that started all this — the idea that a grammar might carve up a familiar dimension differently. Some Bantu languages, ChiBemba among them, don't have one past and one future. They have *several*, sorted by **remoteness**: a tense for earlier today, another for yesterday, another for the distant past, and matching grades running into the future. Time is not a three-way split into past, present and future. It is a ruler with the distances marked, and you choose your rung every time you open your mouth.

(There is a louder claim in this neighbourhood — the economist Keith Chen's argument that speakers of languages which don't grammatically force a future tense save more money, because the future feels nearer when grammar doesn't wall it off. It made headlines and it is genuinely contested; treat it as a provocation, not a finding. But it points at the real question lurking under all of this: when a grammar makes you mark something, does it change what you notice?)

## Why a series about machines keeps ending up here

The flat version of this post is a listicle of charming facts about languages you will never speak. That is not the version worth writing, because there is a thread running through every example, and it is the same thread that runs through everything else here.

Each of these languages has taken something English leaves to *inference* and turned it into *form*. Provenance, in Turkish and Tariana. Certainty, in Quechua. Whose knowledge it is, in Tibetan. Who "we" includes, in Tok Pisin. Whether a thing is part of you, in Hawaiian. The social distance between two people, in Japanese. In English all of that floats in the air around the sentence, recoverable by a listener who shares your context and willing to guess when they don't. In these languages it is nailed to the words.

That is precisely the discipline Machine Experience asks of a web page, and it is worth seeing that the discipline is *ancient*. We tell you not to leave the machine to infer your context, your firsthand authority, your relationships and provenance — because the machine works the surface of your language and cannot recover the meaning you only implied. State it. Structure it. Mark it up. Put it in the form, because the form is the only thing that travels.

People hear that and assume it is a concession to the crudeness of machines — a dumbing-down for systems too literal to read between the lines. It is the opposite. Turkish has been refusing to let speakers leave provenance implicit for as long as there has been Turkish, and Turkish speakers are not crude. They are simply working in a grammar that decided, long ago, that *how you know* is too important to leave the listener guessing. The machine is a harsh reader, but it is not asking anything human grammars haven't asked of their speakers for thousands of years: say the part you were tempted to leave unsaid.

English lets you leave it implicit. That is a freedom, and like most freedoms it is mostly used to be lazy. The languages above took the freedom away, and what they bought with it was a listener who has less to guess wrong. When you mark up a page so a machine can't mistake you, you are not stooping to the machine. You are doing what half the world's grammars already do before breakfast.
